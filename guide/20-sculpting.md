# 20. Sculpting and Sculptural Materials

> **Sources:** H ch.13 (pp. 238–254); M ch.5 (pp. 53–54), ch.10 (pp. 143–144)
> **[Check current]** Product names (Chavant, Sculpey, Friendly Plastic, Foamcoat, etc.) and formulations change.

Unlike fine-art sculpture, which prizes "truth to materials," prop sculpture regularly carves one material and finishes it to look like another. All the other needs still apply — weight, cost, labor, rigidity (H 239).

## Carving vs. modeling (H 239–240)

- **Carving (subtractive):** wood, metal, rigid foams. Added material stays a distinct glued piece.
- **Modeling (additive):** clay, wax — new material merges with old. Must then be made permanent: fired (clay), cooled (thermoplastics), air-dried, or chemically cured (epoxy putty).
- Some models are **masters for molding** ([ch. 21](21-molding-and-casting.md)) or **maquettes** (small-scale 3D references).
- **Machining** shares tools with carving but aims for precise, fitting parts (H 239).

**Do you actually need to sculpt?** Sometimes applying a material with inherent texture (a ridged fabric) beats carving; in very low-budget theatre even painted-on "3D" detail can meet the need (H 240).

## The sculpting process (H 240–243)

1. **Reference.** Front, side, and top views at minimum (draw missing ones); many photos from all sides; real-world references even for fantasy creatures. Use a long lens, subject centered, to limit distortion (H 241).
2. **Make the reference usable:** grid-enlarge or print it full scale. Use it to compare by eye and calipers, to cut a **profile template** (a cut-out silhouette passed over the sculpture), or to build up/carve directly against (H 241).
3. **Rough the overall shape and proportions first** — don't detail an ear until both ears line up (H 242).
4. **Refine with progressively gentler tools.**
5. **Detail and surface last.**

### Subtractive techniques (H 242–243)
- Draw outer profiles on three faces; cut one profile on the band saw, **tape or pin the offcuts back on** to restore a flat face, cut the next profile.
- Big blocks: handsaws, or a **hot-wire cutter** for polystyrene (long two-person bows exist).
- Dry-assemble glued-up blocks, draw, separate, rough-cut, then glue.
- **Slice method:** cut front and side profiles from plywood, add top-profile "slices" on a frame, fill between them with pre-roughed foam, and carve flush to the slices — "connect the dots." Hart's three-foot garden gnome for *Broke-ology* was built this way (H 243–244).

### Additive techniques (H 243–245)
- Build up from small balls of material rather than one giant lump.
- **Armatures** for delicate forms — armature wire (spiral-wrapped with fine wire so clay grips), wood, pipe, rod; joined with epoxy putty, welding, or lashing ([ch. 17](17-structure-and-armatures.md)).
- Bulk out fast with crumpled **foil** or paper bound with tape or wire.

### Hard + soft strategies (H 245–246)
- Rough form in a **hard** material, details in a **soft** one (a wood bird head with self-hardening putty details).
- Or bulk in **soft, fast** material and skin with **hard** material for crisp carving (Bondo over XPS foam, carved when hard).
- Cure/fire in **stages** so handling doesn't wreck finished areas.
- **Waste-mold and recast** a soft sculpt in a hard material to "freeze" progress, carve crisp details, and polish smoother than clay allows.
- Mix materials freely — a "cast-iron" bench ornament built from resin castings, upholstery fringe, Ethafoam, and yarn (H 246).

## Tools (H 246–249)

| For modeling materials | For rigid materials |
|---|---|
| Ribbon/loop tools (reshapable), wooden spatulas and shapers, metal-tipped tools, clay-cutting wire with stick handles | Rasps, **Surform**-type "cheese grater" tools, files; **angle grinder** with flap wheels/sanding discs/wire brushes (works on wood and foam too); belt sanders; **oscillating multi-tool**; **rotary tool/die grinder** with endless bits |

## Materials

| Material | Character | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Oil clay** (plastilina; "Chavant," "Plasteline," "Plasticine") | Never dries; consistent for days; hardness from bubblegum to wood-like; heat to soften | For masters, not final props. Smooth with mineral spirits. **Sulfur-based clays inhibit silicone** — use **sulfur-free** (assume sulfur unless labeled; many shops ban sulfur clay) (H 249–250) |
| **Water/ceramic clays** | Kiln-fired 1200–2300°F | Rare in props except **breakable** items (air-dried or low-fired); air-dry clays look similar without a kiln (H 250) |
| **Polymer clay** (Sculpey, Fimo…) | PVC-based, oven-cured | Small items, jewelry, details (martini olives). **Dedicated oven — never food again** (H 250) |
| **Baker's clay / salt dough** | Flour, salt, water | **Nontoxic**; great for faux baked goods. Air-dry 2–3 days or bake low (150–350°F; browning = too hot); seal with shellac, varnish, or latex paint (H 250–251). *[Check: Hart's printed ratio appears garbled — test a recipe first.]* |
| **PCL** (Friendly Plastic, InstaMorph, Polymorph) | Softens in boiling water (~140°F), hand-shapeable | Jewelry to mask scale; machining heats and deforms it (H 251) |
| **Wax** (jeweler's carving wax, microcrystalline, etc.) | Carves, sands, and drills finely; heat-smoothable | Close-up film details; masters for casting (H 251–252) |
| **XPS foam** (blue/pink "Styrofoam") | Light, fast to carve, glue seams vanish | Dissolved by most solvents and spray paints; degraded by heat. **Fragile — must be coated** ([ch. 23](23-surfaces-texture-and-coatings.md#coating-foam-h-315317)). Glue with **water-based contact cement** ("green glue"); PU glue for foam-to-wood (hard glue line resists carving); two-part foaming PU as a carvable foam-to-foam glue — clamp it, it expands (H 252–253) |
| **EPS bead foam** | Huge blocks for big props; free packaging foam is less dense | (H 253) |
| *"Styrofoam" trap* | The trademark also covers brittle **floral/craft** foam | Floral foam was carved into fake toast and sealed with yellow PVA (H 253) |
| **Rigid urethane foam** | Denser, finer detail than polystyrene | Blocks from taxidermy suppliers, or cast your own from leftover A-B foam (H 253–254) |
| **Spray foam** | Adds carvable bulk to irregular armatures (foam sprayed on a real skeleton became a corpse in *The Bacchae*) | Air-cures — apply in thin layers or the center never cures. **Isocyanates** off-gas until fully cured; no odor warning; dust masks useless; use local exhaust (H 254) |
| **Basswood** | Fine, controllable grain | Ideal for learning wood carving; balsa is too mushy (H 254) |

### Polystyrene safety (H 243; M 143–144)
Hot-wiring or hot-knifing polystyrene releases **styrene** and related vapors — eye and respiratory irritant, nervous-system effects (dizziness means inadequate ventilation), IARC "possibly carcinogenic." Carving and rasping make dust. Use ventilation (local exhaust for big hot-wire rigs), an organic-vapor respirator per the SDS for hot cutting, and a particulate mask and goggles for rasping.

For food: Model Magic, Creative Paperclay, salt dough (baker's clay) and moldable thermoplastic pellets as fake produce, fish, crusts and butter, see [ch. 28](28-fake-food.md#modeling-compounds-ff-8-12-43-149) (FF).

**Foam clay** (EVA mixed with air-dry clay) sculpts horns, spikes, skulls and details onto EVA props and fills seams. It expands slightly as it dries unless heat-set straight after sculpting (KF 27; [ch. 29](29-eva-foam-props.md#foam-clay-kf-6-27-28-39-47)).

## For the classroom

- **Soap carving warm-up:** Hart's bar-of-soap polar bear (H 9) teaches subtractive thinking in an hour.
- **Slice-method sculpture:** Students build a 2–3 ft foam figure using plywood profiles and slices, then coat it ([ch. 23](23-surfaces-texture-and-coatings.md)).
- **Oil-clay master:** Students sculpt a relief panel or small object in sulfur-free clay as the master for their molding-and-casting project ([ch. 21](21-molding-and-casting.md)).
- **Food sculpture:** Students make a convincing plate of fake food using baker's clay, polymer clay, foam, and paint — and explain their material choices.
