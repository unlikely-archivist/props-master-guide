# 13. Adhesives, Fasteners, Tapes, and Assembly

> **Sources:** H ch.5 (pp. 59–77); M ch.5 (p. 51), ch.9 (pp. 131–132)
> **[Check current]** Brand names (Fabri-Tac, E6000, Gorilla, Fastbond, Flex Glue, Super 77, etc.) change and get reformulated. Learn the **category** each brand belongs to, so you can find a substitute when a product disappears (H 65).

## Assemblage and found objects (H 60–62)

Props are rarely one material. Treat everything around you (hardware-store parts, thrift-store items, toy parts, packaging) as potential material. Model makers call this **kitbashing**.

Hart's mini boombox is a good example. It's an MDF block with:
- wooden knobs and discs for dials and speakers
- window screen over the speakers
- a bent-wire handle
- a stripped-wire antenna with a bead on the end
- styrene panels
- model-railroad brick sheet cut into buttons

Collect parts as you find them, and organize them so you can find them again (H 61).

**Surface detail** (H 62):
- **Diapering** is the traditional term for decorating a plain surface with a repeating pattern.
- **Greebles / nurnies / wiggets** are small applied details that make a surface read as complex. The term came from model makers on the original *Star Wars*.
- Techniques:
  - scribe panel lines
  - add rows of rivet heads
  - put cut-out overlays on plain hinges to fake wrought iron
  - apply thin sheet for raised panels
- **Materials:** styrene sheet is the modern standard and holds a crisp edge. Industrial felt stiffened with shellac or resin is the traditional option for curved surfaces. Textured materials (grilles, fireplace screens, embossed papers) can be glued on and coated.

## How adhesives work (H 62–64)

*Adhesive*, *glue* and *cement* are interchangeable in practice. Adhesives bond in three ways, and some use more than one:
1. **Evaporation ("drying")**: water or solvent leaves. On wood, some is absorbed into the material.
2. **Curing**: a chemical reaction. Two-part products, or ones triggered by moisture or air.
3. **Heat**: hot melts soften and re-harden. Thermosets cure with heat and can't be re-softened.

### Working properties
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Open assembly time** | Time from applying glue to closing the joint |
| **Closed assembly time** | Time from closing the joint to clamping |
| **Clamp time** | How long the joint must stay clamped |
| **Initial tack** | How well parts stay put when first pressed together |
| **Cure time / initial cure / final cure** | Liquid to solid; set but weak; full strength |
| **Pot life vs. working life** | Usable time in the mixing cup vs. once spread. Epoxy sets faster in a mass |

### Bond properties to choose for
- flexible or rigid (rigid joints can be sanded)
- clear or colored
- permanent or reversible
- waterproof or water-soluble
- gap-filling or thin

**There's no miracle adhesive.** The choice depends on the materials, the type of joint and the conditions (H 64).

### Application rules (H 64)
- **Read the directions.** PVA goes on one side; contact cement goes on both sides and must dry before you join them.
- Bonds to bare material differ from bonds to paint, coatings or old glue. Prepare the surface.
- **Plan your clamping before you apply glue**, and **dry-fit** first.

## Adhesive families

| Family | Use it for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Animal (hide) glue** | Furniture repair | Slightly flexible; reversible with heat, so old hide-glue joints can be reglued (H 65) |
| **Starch/wheat paste** | Paper, papier-mâché | (H 65) |
| **PVA: white** (Elmer's) & **yellow** (carpenter's/wood glue) | Wood, paper, porous materials | Same strength in wood. Yellow is thicker, tackier and dries more rigid and sandable. Also comes as flexible PVAs, pH-neutral bookbinder's PVA and decoupage medium (Mod Podge) (H 65) |
| **"Flex glue"** | Flexible bonds, binders for pigments and textures | Theatrical vinyl-acrylic or flexible PVA products (H 65–66) |
| **Cyanoacrylate (CA, "super glue")** | Small parts, nonporous materials, many plastics | Fast and costly in quantity. Fogs clear plastics. **Bonds skin.** Debonder removes it (H 66, 69) |
| **Hot glue (EVA hot melt)** | Quick assembly, gap filling, raised details, faux sealing wax | **High-melt** for structural use. For burns, let it cool, don't wipe; keep cool water nearby. Melts polystyrene foam (H 66) |
| **Fugitive ("snot") glue** | Temporary, residue-free holds on paper and plastic | Needs its own gun (H 66) |
| **Epoxy** | Dissimilar or nonporous materials; waterproof joints; fast repairs | Viscosity ranges from liquid to putty. Rigid. Sensitizer (H 66) |
| **Contact cement** | Laminates, veneer, rubber, leather | Coat both sides, let dry, press. Can't reposition. **Barge** cement for leather. **Water-based "green glue"** contact cements are less toxic and **safe on polystyrene** (H 66–67) |
| **Latex adhesives** | Flexible, stretchy bonds; fabric, carpet | (H 67) |
| **Bridal/craft glue** (solvent-based) | Fabric, trims, beads, feathers, flowers | Like "cold hot glue." Can stand in for a hem (H 67) |
| **Household cement** (Goop-type) | Thick, gap-filling bonds on nonporous materials; small details | **Harsh solvents and long drying times mean a lot of fumes.** Better for repairs and details than structure (H 67–68) |
| **Spray adhesive** | Paper or fabric to rigid surfaces without wrinkling | Solvent and aerosol hazards. Avoid when possible (H 68) |
| **Construction adhesive** | Paneling and molding; heavy bonds | Applied with a caulk gun (H 68) |
| **Polyurethane glue** (Gorilla-type) | Foam to wood, dissimilar materials | Moisture-activated. Foams slightly, which fills small gaps. The hard glue line resists carving (H 68, 253) |
| **Urea-formaldehyde, resorcinol** | Veneers, laminates, marine joints | Long working time; waterproof; no creep. Formaldehyde off-gassing. Resorcinol leaves a dark glue line (H 68) |
| **Silicone** | Flexible, waterproof, watertight joints | Very hard to paint over (H 68) |
| **Solvent welders** | Compatible plastics | Dissolve and fuse plastic surfaces. See [ch. 16](16-plastics-and-foams.md#solvent-welding-h-170171) (H 68–69) |

### Material-pairing cautions (H 69–71)
- **Plaster** bonds poorly until it's fully dry (days to weeks). Seal it with paint or PVA first for CA or PVA bonds.
- **Polystyrene foam** dissolves in harsh solvents and melts with heat. Test first and use only low-temperature hot melt. FF reports that 3M **reformulated Super 77 with acetone, which dissolves XPS**. Use Super 77 Classic or 3M 78 Polystyrene spray, and spray both faces (FF 157, 195) **[check current]**. FF's laminating table is in [ch. 28](28-fake-food.md#rigid-foam-xps-ff-13-and-chs-4-17-22-23-27-30-39-41).
- **Polyethylene (#2, HDPE) and polypropylene won't bond with any adhesive**, and paint won't stick to them either.
- "Wood" on a craft-glue label means small decorative bits, not furniture joints.
- Consider whether glue is even the right joint. Welding, fasteners, tying, sewing or taping may be better.

### Removing glue (H 69)
Heat (hot melts), the original solvent (water or a solvent), dedicated debonders (CA), or mechanical removal by cutting or sanding.

## Tapes (H 69–73; M 51)

| Tape | Use |
|---|---|
| **Gaffer** | Strong cloth tape; low residue. **Spike tape** is narrow colored gaff for marking positions |
| **Masking / painter's** | Paint masking; temporary holds while glue dries; taping templates on |
| **Double-stick** | Carpet tape, mounting foam tape, transfer sheets for models |
| **Clear packing tape** | Quick waterproofing, e.g., over a wine label washed nightly |
| **Duct tape** | Emergencies only. Its permanent adhesive and plastic face reject paint |
| **Electrical tape** | Insulation; labeling; quick grips; **removable stripes on rented props** |
| **Floral tape** | Stretch it and it sticks to itself; wraps wire stems or wire armatures (pheasant feet) |
| **Mortite / floral putty** | Temporarily anchoring dressing onstage |

## Mechanical fasteners (H 73–77)

Fasteners hold by **friction** (nails) or by **capture** (bolts, rivets). Use them to reinforce glue joints, to join materials that can't be glued, or to make joints you can take apart.

| Fastener | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Nails** | Common, box, finish, brads; pins and tacks for upholstery |
| **Staples** | Classified by crown and width; hand, electric or pneumatic guns; sheet goods and fabric |
| **Screws** | Wood screws. **Drywall screws** are the shop's all-purpose screw but snap under side load. Self-tapping and self-drilling screws for metal; sheet-metal screws. Head shapes: pan, round, flat. Drives: slotted, Phillips, square/Robertson. Drill **pilot holes** and **countersink** to prevent splitting ([ch. 14](14-carpentry.md)) |
| **Nuts and bolts** | Hex, carriage and stove bolts; machine screws; lag bolts. You can **drill and tap** threads in metal. **Reversible**, so use them for knock-down joints and removable casters |
| **Rivets** | Permanent. **Pop (blind) rivets** install from one side; good for sheet metal |
| **Corrugated fasteners** | Picture-frame butt joints |
| **Plumber's strap** | Quick strapping and hanging |
| **Hook and loop (Velcro)** | Sewn, glued or self-adhesive; fully reversible |
| **Sewing, lashing, wiring** | When glue or fasteners won't work; can be decorative |
| **Monofilament (fishing line)** | Nearly invisible support or tension |
| **Capture** | Build around a part so it can't move, like a picture held in its frame |
| **Magnets** | Removable attachments and breakaways. **Rare-earth magnets** are very strong: embed them or epoxy them in, and mount them more strongly than their pull. **Pinch and crush hazard** |

## For the classroom

- **Glue lab.** Students glue six material pairs (wood–wood, foam–wood, fabric–wood, metal–plastic, plaster–wood, HDPE–anything) with three adhesives each, then stress-test the joints and record working properties. Few demonstrations teach "no miracle adhesive" better.
- **Greeble panel.** Students turn a plain styrene or MDF panel into a convincing machine surface using only applied details and found parts.
- **Fastener ID.** Sort a mixed bin of fasteners, name each one and give a prop use for it.
