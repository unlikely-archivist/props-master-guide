## What makes an effects prop different (M 9–10; H 9–10)

**Manual and special-effects props** have a trick to them, or are used in a way that would be dangerous or unconventional in real life. Mussman's examples:
- burning crucifixes
- burnt meatloaf
- campfires
- crash boxes
- exploding clocks
- gunshots
- magic tricks
- neck snaps
- rats that ooze blood

These props need special care. **Clean, reset, pick up or lock them up after every use.** They may be operated by actors or by crew (M 9–10).

Hart's *Prop Building Guidebook* deliberately leaves out trick props (breakaways, magic, edibles, electrical, pneumatic, mechanical and radio-controlled props), which the *Effects Guidebook* covers (H 9–10).

Three rules apply to every effect:
1. **Never ask an actor to perform an effect you haven't tested yourself.** If you haven't tried it, you don't know whether it works safely or how to teach it (M 149).
2. **Get expert help.** Special-effects suppliers want you to succeed safely and will recommend products and protective gear (M 149). Complex shows may hire an **effects designer**. Flame and explosives generally require a **licensed pyrotechnician** (H 7).
3. **Research the rules before you plan.** Check union rules (Actors' Equity for professional actors and stage managers) and state and local law. They may restrict guns, pyro, haze or fog, and smoking in your venue (M 149).

## Breakaways (H 3–4; H 77, 190–191, 274–275)

- **Two kinds** (H 3–4):
  - Rigged to break predictably and **reassemble** (chairs, swords).
  - **Single-use** pieces that break without injuring anyone, either on impact or from the shards. **Sugar-glass** bottles shatter safely over a head. Single-use breakaways are also **consumables**, so budget for the whole run.
- **Magnets** make removable and breakaway joints without holes or residue (H 77).
- Flexible materials and **cushioning** protect both actor and prop. Film stunt weapons are often single castings of foam rubber (H 190–191).
- **Gelatin** casts make **breakaway prosthetics** (Gloucester's eyes in *King Lear*). Gelatin softens under stage lights, so make them fresh (H 274–275).

## Breakaway practice [General knowledge]

- **Breakaway glass**: commercial breakaway bottles, glasses and window panes are made from brittle resins designed to shatter into harmless pieces. "Sugar glass" (cooked sugar) is the traditional version but softens in humidity and heat. Buy enough for the whole run plus rehearsals (H 3–4).
- **Breakaway furniture** is built with weak, glued or pinned joints (often in balsa or other soft wood) so it collapses along planned lines, then reassembles or is replaced.
- **Clean up** every shard after every use; plan who sweeps and when.
- **Test everything** the same way it will be used, many times, before an actor uses it (M 149).

## For the classroom

- **Effect safety plan.** For one effect (a breakaway bottle, a practical oil lamp, an onstage smoke), students write a full plan: hazards, controls, who operates and resets it, storage, an emergency response, and the "I tested it myself" log.
- **Policy research.** Students find and summarize your own institution's weapons, flame and haze policies and the current relevant union rules, then compare them with Mussman's 2008 guidance and discuss what changed and why.
- **Breakaway test.** Students make a simple breakaway (a scored balsa or foam chair rung, or a reassembling rigged object with magnets, H 77), test it repeatedly, and write the reset procedure.
