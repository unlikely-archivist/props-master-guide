# 49. Practicals, Electrics and Mechanisms

> **Sources:** M ch.1 (p. 10), ch.3 (p. 32); H ch.1 (p. 2), ch.5 (p. 77); B (pp. 81–89). Sections marked **[General knowledge]** aren't from the guide's sources.
> **[Check current]** Electrical codes, battery types and control technology change quickly. Mains (line-voltage) wiring should be done or checked by a qualified electrician under local code.

Practicals are props that work: lamps that light, radios that play, things that move. They sit where props, electrics and sound meet, so coordinate early.

## Practicals and electrics (M 10, 32; H 2)

Lamps, chandeliers, lanterns and battery "flames" are **practicals**. Coordinate early with the **master electrician** and **lighting designer**: wiring, control from the board, brightness, color. Battery-operated flame effects are usually preferred to live flame. Running water and working appliances also count as practicals. *[Pending: Effects Guidebook chs. 2–3 and 11, "Electricity, Wiring, Soldering"; "Lights"; "Control."]*

## Practicals in practice [General knowledge]

- **Who wires what.** Agree early with the master electrician: usually electrics handles anything plugged into stage power or controlled from the lighting board, and props handles the object and any battery-powered effects. Write it down ([ch. 10](../2-admin/10-working-with-other-departments.md)).
- **Mains-powered lamps**: check old lamps for perished wiring before use, and have electrics test them. Swap hot incandescent bulbs for LED where possible to reduce heat near shades and actors.
- **Battery practicals**: LEDs, small battery packs and switches make lanterns, candles, glowing screens and indicator lights without cables. Plan where the battery and switch hide, how the actor or crew turns it on, and how batteries are changed (they're running props, H 3).
- **Remote control**: wireless receivers let a lamp or effect be switched from offstage or the board. Test range and reliability in the actual venue.
- **Screens and devices**: phones, tablets and TVs onstage often show pre-made images or video. Lock devices so they can't receive calls or notifications, and turn screen brightness down to suit the lighting.

## Mechanisms [General knowledge]

- **Simple is reliable.** Strings, levers, springs, magnets (H 77) and gravity are easier to fix in the dark than motors.
- **Motors and actuators** turn electrical power into movement. Berls's student project builds a printed gearbox and threaded-rod linear actuator, with gear-ratio trade-offs and a circuit troubleshooting checklist (B 81–89; [ch. 40](../5-materials/40-3d-printing.md#moving-parts-a-linear-actuator-b-8189)).
- **Microcontrollers** (Arduino and similar) can run sequences of lights, sounds and motors from a button or a cue.
- **Pneumatics** (compressed air) give fast, strong motion but need proper fittings, regulators and safety training.
- **Always have a manual backup** or a plan for when the mechanism fails mid-show.

## For the classroom

- **Battery lantern.** Students convert a period lantern or candlestick into a battery-powered practical with a hidden switch and an easy battery change.
- **Who does what.** Students list every practical in a script and propose, with an electrician, which department handles each part.
- **Mechanism with a purpose.** Build Berls's linear actuator and use it in a small stage effect (B 81–89).
