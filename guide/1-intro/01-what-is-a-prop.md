# 1. What Is a Prop?

> **Sources:** M ch.1 (pp. 5–14); H ch.1 (pp. 1–10)
> **Citation key:** see [README](../../README.md#citation-key). Page numbers for H refer to the 2013 first edition.

A working definition: **a prop is any physical object in a performance that is not the actor, the costume, or the scenery** (H 2). In practice the boundaries are blurry. A sword can be a prop or part of a costume. A built-in bench can be scenery or a set prop. That blurriness is the reason the categories exist: they are a shared vocabulary that lets departments assign responsibility for every item and set priorities (M 5–6).

The single most important habit: **never assume who owns an ambiguous item.** Raise it in a production meeting and get it assigned to a department in writing (H 4). Hart notes that people have tried to make rules (an umbrella is a prop but a parasol is a costume), and that these rules never survive contact with a real production. Often items simply go to whichever department has the better stock or the right tools.

---

## What props do

Props are not decoration for its own sake. According to Mussman (M 12–13), props:

- **Tell the story** by carrying plot (the letter, the gun, the ring).
- **Define character.** A cup of coffee and a Bible in an apron pocket tell you who someone is before they speak.
- **Establish world**, meaning period, place, season, socioeconomic status and mood.
- **Shape the actors' work.** Props drive blocking and blocking drives props. Once actors have rehearsed with an object, even a small change can throw off timing.
- **Bridge scenery and costume**, tying the visual design together.

A single prop can touch many departments. Mussman's example is a table lamp: it must suit the period, be short enough that an actor behind it can be seen, match the sofa, be wired to the light board, put out enough light for the lighting designer, and not clash with the lead's costume (M 13).

## The core categories

Mussman organizes theatre props into three main categories with hand-prop subcategories. Hart adds the film and TV vocabulary. The table merges both.

| Category | What it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Hand prop** (Broadway/film: *action prop*) | Handled, carried or used by an actor; small to medium; not fixed to the set | Highest priority. Preset on prop tables or onstage (M 6–7; H 2) |
| **Set prop** | Objects on the set, mostly furniture, plus rugs and items on floors, walls and ceilings | Defines location, period and status. Distinguish from **set pieces**, which are scenic elements built and attached by the scene shop (M 10–11; H 4) |
| **Trim prop** | Set props hung on walls: curtains, blinds, pictures | (H 4) |
| **Set dressing** | Items that create place and atmosphere but are not handled by actors | Usually lowest priority. In **theatre** the props department handles it; in **film/TV** the *set decoration* department does (M 11; H 4) |

### Hand-prop subcategories and overlapping types

| Type | Definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumable / perishable** | Eaten, broken, torn or used up each performance (food, cigarettes, stage blood, torn paper) | Budget for the full run plus rehearsals, dress and previews. Food must be fresh every show; food poisoning is a real risk (M 7–8; H 3) |
| **Running prop** | Items replenished at intervals, including batteries | A newspaper may last several shows before it is too creased (H 3) |
| **Costume prop** ("propstume") | Accessories that complete a costume: watch, holster, sword belt, mask, jewelry, cane, glasses | Decide early with the costume shop who supplies them. Matching swords across the two departments need coordination (M 9; H 3) |
| **Personal prop** | Kept by one actor at all times; the crew does not handle it | e.g. a letter kept in a pocket (H 3) |
| **Practical** | Works as it would in life: lamps light, phones ring, sinks run | Coordinate with electrics. Battery "flame" is usually preferred to live flame (M 10; H 2) |
| **Dummy / static** | Looks real but does not function | e.g. a box that won't open, a carved phone (H 2) |
| **Manual / special-effects prop** | Has a trick or is potentially dangerous (crash boxes, gunshots, breakaway items, bleeding props) | Must be reset, cleaned or locked up after every use. See [ch. 46](../7-effects/46-effects-and-breakaways.md) (M 9–10) |
| **Breakaway** | Breaks on cue. Either rigged to reassemble (chairs, swords) or single-use and safe when broken (sugar-glass bottle) | Single-use breakaways are also consumables. Coordinate with the fight director or stunt department (H 3–4) |
| **Rehearsal prop vs. "actuals"** | Stand-in used in rehearsal vs. the performance prop | Should match size and weight; sometimes becomes the actual (H 2; M 120–121) |

### Film and television vocabulary (H 2–3)

- **Hero prop**: built for close-ups, in full detail.
- **Background prop**: seen at a distance, so often less detailed.
- **Stunt prop**: soft foam or rubber versions built for action scenes.

One film prop may exist in all three versions. Hart's example is a game board from *Jumanji*: the hero version opens, and the stunt version is a solid casting the actors could run with. How the three media differ is covered in [ch. 3](03-theatre-film-and-tv.md).

### A useful historical frame

Kabuki theatre divides props into **hommono** (real everyday articles that are bought, borrowed or rented) and **koshiraemono** (objects made specifically for the stage) (H 4). The distinction maps neatly onto the two halves of this guide: *sourcing* ([Part 3](../3-sourcing/15-shopping.md)) and *building* ([Parts 4](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md) and [5](../5-materials/29-wood.md)). For more history, see [ch. 5](05-history-of-props.md).

## For the classroom

- **Sorting exercise.** Hand students a list of 30 objects from a real script (glasses, a parasol, a sword, a rug, a built-in bench, a letter). Have them assign each to a category and a department, then argue the gray areas. The point is that *assignment is a conversation*, not a rule.
- **Discussion.** Why might the same object be a costume prop in one production and a hand prop in another?
- **Observation.** Students photograph a real room (kitchen, office, dorm) and annotate which items would be set props, dressing and hand props if it were a set. Ask what each item says about the person who lives there.
