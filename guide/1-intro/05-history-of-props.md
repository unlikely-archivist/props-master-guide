# 5. A History of Props and Prop Making

> **Sources:** H ch.1 (p. 4); J (whole book, especially pp. 9–10); M ch.2 (pp. 15–17). Most of this chapter is **[General knowledge]**, written from widely available theatre history rather than the guide's sources. **Verify dates and attributions before publishing or teaching them as fact.**
> **Companion:** for the history of the *objects themselves* (when forks, matches, telephones or zippers came into use), see [ch. 7](../2-admin/07-research-and-period-accuracy.md) and the [Period Props Timeline](../../appendices/E-period-props-timeline.md).

This chapter is about the history of the **craft and the department**: how people have made and managed stage objects over time. It gives context for why the job looks the way it does today.

## Where the word comes from [General knowledge]

"Props" is short for **properties**. The usual explanation is that in English theatre companies of the 16th and 17th centuries, the costumes and objects used in plays were the company's **property**, as distinct from the actors' own belongings. The word "properties" for stage objects appears in English records from at least the 15th century. Theatre manager Philip Henslowe's papers, from the Rose playhouse around 1598, include an inventory of stage properties, among them a rock, a tomb, a cage, a "Hell mouth" and assorted heads and animal skins. It shows how much a professional company owned and reused.

## Before the modern stage [General knowledge]

- **Ancient Greek theatre** used masks, hand props and stage machinery: the *ekkyklema* (a wheeled platform for revealing interior scenes) and the *mechane* (a crane that flew actors in as gods, the origin of *deus ex machina*).
- **Medieval religious drama** in Europe, staged by guilds and towns, used elaborate effects props: Hell-mouths that belched smoke, flying angels, and blood effects for martyrdoms. Guild accounts record payments for making and repairing these objects.
- **Japanese kabuki** distinguished **hommono** (real everyday articles that are bought, borrowed or rented) from **koshiraemono** (objects made specially for the stage) (H 4). The same division runs through this guide: *sourcing* in [Part 3](../3-sourcing/15-shopping.md) and *building* in [Parts 4](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md) and [5](../5-materials/29-wood.md).

## The property man [General knowledge]

From the 17th to the 19th century, Western theatres employed a **property man** (the term was nearly always male then) who kept, made and set the company's objects. In repertory and stock companies that performed many plays a season, a large reusable **stock** was essential, and stock pieces were adapted from play to play. This is the root of the prop storage and "pull from stock" practice in [ch. 13](../2-admin/13-strike-storage-and-records.md) and [ch. 19](../3-sourcing/19-recycling-and-reusing.md).

## Realism and the full stage [General knowledge]

The late 19th and early 20th centuries brought stage **realism**. Directors and producers such as the Duke of Saxe-Meiningen's company, the Moscow Art Theatre and, in the United States, David Belasco filled stages with real, period-accurate objects. Belasco became famous for transporting real interiors onto the stage, including a working Child's restaurant for *The Governor's Lady* (1912). Realism made research and sourcing central to the job, and it still shapes audience expectations. Hart describes a modern version of this pressure: "theatre props in a movie world," where audiences expect screen-level realism (H 15).

## Film and television [General knowledge]

The Hollywood studio system of the 1920s to 1950s built huge **in-house prop departments** with warehouses of furniture, weapons and dressing, and specialist craftspeople. Much of that stock later passed to independent **prop rental houses**, which still supply film, television and theatre ([ch. 16](../3-sourcing/16-renting.md)). Unions organised the work. In the US, props work in film and theatre falls under the International Alliance of Theatrical Stage Employees (IATSE) and its locals, which is why job titles and who may handle props on set are often set by contract (H 6–7).

## Materials change the craft [General knowledge]

Prop making has always borrowed the newest cheap materials:
- **Papier-mâché**, plaster and carved wood were the traditional staples ([ch. 36](../5-materials/36-shells-and-laminates.md)).
- After the Second World War, **plastics, polyurethane and polystyrene foams, fiberglass and latex** transformed what could be made light, strong and fast ([ch. 31](../5-materials/31-plastics.md), [ch. 32](../5-materials/32-foams-and-rubbers.md)).
- From the 1990s onward, **thermoplastic sheets** and cheap **EVA foam**, often pioneered by costume and cosplay makers, became standard ([ch. 33](../5-materials/33-eva-foam.md)).
- From the 2010s, affordable **3D printers**, laser cutters and CNC machines entered prop shops and classrooms ([ch. 40](../5-materials/40-3d-printing.md)). Hart in 2013 still described desktop printing as an emerging technology (H 10, 58).

Each new material also brought new hazards, which is why health and safety became a core part of the job ([ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md)).

## The profession today

- **A department in its own right.** Mussman describes props increasingly treated as its own department, with the prop master reporting to the production manager rather than through the technical director (M 17, 31–32; [ch. 2](02-the-prop-master.md)).
- **[General knowledge]** Professional organisations, training programmes and conferences now support props artisans. In North America, for example, the Society of Properties Artisan Managers (SPAM) and USITT's conferences give prop people a place to share methods. **[Check current]**
- **[General knowledge]** Weapons safety has changed sharply. Accidents with blank-firing guns, including the deaths of actor Jon-Erik Hexum (1984) and Brandon Lee on *The Crow* (1993), and cinematographer Halyna Hutchins on the set of *Rust* (2021), led many productions and schools to ban real firearms entirely. See [ch. 43](../6-weapons/43-firearms.md).

## For the classroom

- **Inventory then and now.** Students look at a historical stage-property list (Henslowe's is widely reprinted) and compare it with a modern prop list from [ch. 6](../2-admin/06-script-breakdown-and-prop-list.md). What has changed, and what hasn't?
- **Materials timeline.** Teams research when one prop material (papier-mâché, latex, fiberglass, EVA foam, 3D printing) entered theatre practice and present one historical prop made with it.
- **Realism vs. suggestion.** Students compare photos of a Belasco-style realist set with a minimalist production of the same period, and discuss what each asked of its props department.
- **Oral history.** Students interview a long-serving prop person about how materials, tools and safety rules have changed during their career.
