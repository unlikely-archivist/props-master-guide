# 3. Theatre, Film and Television

> **Sources:** H ch.1 (pp. 2–8), ch.2 (pp. 15–16); M ch.1 (pp. 6–11). Sections marked **[General knowledge]** aren't from the guide's sources.
> **[Check current]** Union jurisdictions, job titles and department structures differ by country, city, union contract and production size, and they change. Treat the structures here as typical, not universal.

The same object, a letter or a sword or a lamp, is propped very differently on a stage, a film set and a TV series. Three things drive almost every difference: **how close the audience gets**, **how many times the prop is used**, and **how the departments divide the work**. Mussman's book covers theatre only. Hart covers all three, and most of what follows builds on him.

## The quick comparison (H 2–8, 15–16)

| | Theatre | Film | Television |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Who designs props** | Usually the set designer, sometimes a props designer; often left to the prop master's interpretation (H 6–7) | Production designer or art director (H 7) | Production designer (H 8) |
| **Who supplies hand props** | Prop master and props shop | Prop master (H 7) | Props department; inside and outside crews (H 8) |
| **Who does set dressing** | The props department (M 11; H 4) | The **set decorator** and set decoration department (H 4, 7) | Set decoration |
| **Audience distance** | Fixed and fairly far; detail exaggerated or faked to read from the house (H 15) | The camera can go anywhere; extreme close-ups; all sides finished (H 15) | HD close-ups (H 15) |
| **Durability** | Must survive the whole run, often eight shows a week (H 15) | May be needed for one day, but breaking on set costs money (H 15) | Weeks of shooting plus storage between seasons (H 15) |
| **Time** | Weeks of build before tech; changes through previews (H 15) | Changes made on the spot, so props must be quick to modify (H 15) | Often 3–4 days from script to camera (H 8, 15) |
| **Who handles props during use** | Run crew and actors; the props team usually isn't backstage at every show | Union rules often **bar the prop master from handling props on set**, depending on jurisdiction (H 7) | Inside crew maintains and rigs props (H 8) |
| **Building** | In-house shop | Often outside shops or a temporary location shop (H 7) | Mix of in-house and vendors and rental houses (H 8) |

## Theatre

- **Repetition is the enemy.** A prop that survives one careful use may fail at the fortieth. Build for the run: wear, sweat, drops, quick changes, and crew handling in the dark (H 15).
- **Reading from the house.** Detail is chosen for the seat furthest away and the closest. Some details are exaggerated, some faked, some left out (H 15). Mussman's three stage types set how much detail matters: on an **arena** stage props must read from every side (M 37).
- **Sightlines and rakes.** Tall furniture gets shortened on thrust and arena stages. **Raked stages** need counter-raked props and anti-slide measures (H 15).
- **Live safety.** A loose part can fly into the front row. Flame-proofing rules apply (H 15).
- **"Theatre props in a movie world."** Audiences, directors and actors increasingly expect screen-level realism even at a distance, and a badly made prop can undermine an actor even when the audience can't see the flaw (H 15).
- **Set dressing is props' job** in theatre (M 11; H 4). See [ch. 51](../8-wider-craft/51-set-dressing.md).

## Film (H 2–3, 7, 15)

- **Versions of one prop.** A single film prop may exist as a **hero** (full detail for close-ups), a **background** version and a **stunt** version in soft foam or rubber. Hart's example is a *Jumanji* game board: the hero opens, the stunt is a solid casting actors could run with (H 2–3).
- **Lenses distort**, so proportions are judged through the camera, not by eye (H 15).
- **All sides show.** There's no "backstage" side to leave unfinished (H 15).
- **Firearms modified for blanks need a licensed armorer**, though the prop master remains ultimately responsible (H 7). See [ch. 43](../6-weapons/43-firearms.md).
- **Picture vehicles** (cars and other vehicles seen on camera) fall to the prop master (H 7).

**[General knowledge]**
- **Continuity.** Scenes are shot out of order, often over days or weeks. Every prop's state (how full the glass is, how much of the letter is burned, which hand holds the cup) must match from shot to shot. Props departments keep continuity photos and notes, and work with the script supervisor.
- **Multiples.** Anything that gets broken, eaten, burned, bloodied or used up needs enough identical copies for every take, plus spares. Agree the number of multiples with the director or assistant director early.
- **Clearances.** Anything branded or copyrighted that appears on camera may need legal clearance, so films often use "greeked" (fictional) labels and brands. See [ch. 39](../5-materials/39-paper-and-graphics.md).
- **The department.** Typical film props crews include a prop master, an assistant prop master, and on-set props people. Many crews also have a separate **set decoration** team (set decorator, leadperson, set dressers, buyers). Big productions may add a dedicated **weapons master** or armorer, a **food stylist**, and a **picture-car coordinator**.

## Television (H 8)

- The production designer supervises. An **inside** studio crew maintains and rigs props, while an **outside** crew deals with vendors and rental houses (H 8).
- **Weekly episodes can leave three or four days** from script to camera (H 8), so stock, rentals and fast modification matter more than custom builds.
- **Recurring props** (a character's phone, a hospital's equipment, a family's kitchen) must survive weeks of shooting and storage between seasons, and look identical every time (H 15). **[General knowledge]** Standing sets and recurring props make continuity a season-long job; keep a detailed photo record.
- **Commercials and music videos** tend to be more hands-on, especially non-union work (H 8).

## Other forms (H 8)

Prop skills also work in opera and dance (where props must survive dancers and huge spaces), magic, puppetry, animatronics, models, theme parks, haunted attractions, museums, trade shows and events (H 8). **[General knowledge]** Each brings its own needs: opera is like theatre at larger scale with long gaps between revivals; theme parks need props that survive years of public handling and weather; museums need conservation-safe materials and accuracy. See [ch. 53](../8-wider-craft/53-careers-and-professional-practice.md) for careers.

## Moving between media [General knowledge]

- **Theatre to screen.** Expect less build time, more multiples, continuity tracking and stricter union lines about who touches what. Finish every side.
- **Screen to theatre.** Expect to build for durability over a long run, to think about the back row as well as the front, and to own set dressing.
- **Everywhere.** The same questions from [ch. 22](../4-fabrication-core/22-the-build-method.md) apply: what must the prop look like and do, for how long, from how close, and with what resources?

## For the classroom

- **One prop, three media.** Give students one object (a love letter that is read, crumpled and burned). Teams plan it for a six-week theatre run, a two-day film shoot and a recurring TV prop: how many copies, what detail, what materials, who handles it.
- **Continuity drill.** Film a 30-second scene in three takes with a half-eaten sandwich and a glass of "wine." Students track and reset the props between takes, then watch the cut for mismatches.
- **Distance test.** Paint the same label three ways (full detail, simplified, suggested) and view them from the front row, the back of the house and through a phone camera at close range.
- **Department mapping.** Students draw the props and set decoration structure for a regional theatre, a feature film and a multi-camera sitcom, and mark who is responsible for a lamp on a side table in each.
