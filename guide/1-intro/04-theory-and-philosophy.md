# 4. Theory and Philosophy of Props

> **Sources:** M ch.1 (pp. 12–13), ch.2 (p. 16); H ch.1 (pp. 1–10), ch.2 (pp. 11–24); J (pp. 9–10); FF (pp. 4–5); KF (p. 2); B (pp. 23–32, 47, 57, 115). Sections marked **[General knowledge]** aren't from the guide's sources.

Every source in this guide is practical, but each one rests on a few ideas about what props are *for*. Knowing them helps you make decisions when no book has the answer. This chapter collects those ideas in one place. The practical method that grows from them is in [ch. 22](../4-fabrication-core/22-the-build-method.md).

## Props serve the story

The sources agree on this more than on anything else.
- **Mussman:** the prop master is above all a **visual storyteller** working alongside the director and designers (M 16). Props tell the story, define character, establish the world, shape the actors' work and bridge scenery and costume (M 12–13; [ch. 1](01-what-is-a-prop.md#what-props-do)).
- **Berls:** "We do not create for prop's sake; we create to move the story forward." Always ask whether a detail will read and whether it serves the story (B 25–26, 47).
- **Hart:** a prop's needs come first from what it must look like **in context** and what it must **do** in the scene (H 12–17; [ch. 22](../4-fabrication-core/22-the-build-method.md#part-1-what-does-the-prop-need-to-do)).

**[General knowledge]** A familiar version of the idea is **"Chekhov's gun"**, the playwriting principle attributed to Anton Chekhov: if a gun appears in the first act, it should go off by the last. The prop-maker's mirror image is just as useful. If an object is going to matter later, the audience must notice it, believe it and remember it, and the props team has to make that happen.

## A prop is something an actor acts with

- Props drive blocking, and blocking drives props. Once actors have rehearsed with an object, even a small change can throw off their timing (M 12–13).
- "Almost everything that can be classified as a prop is meant to interact with the human body in some way. Can someone actually use it?" (B 57).
- A poorly made prop can undermine an actor's performance even if the audience can't see the flaw (H 15).
- This is why the sources insist on **rehearsal props** that match the size and weight of the actuals (H 2; M 120–121; [ch. 12](../2-admin/12-rehearsals-tech-and-performance.md)), and on testing every effect yourself before giving it to an actor (M 149).

## Needs before means (H 11–24)

Hart's central method splits every problem in two:
1. **What does the prop need to do?** Look, other senses, function (including what it must *not* do), size, weight, balance, medium, and the reference behind it.
2. **What are your means?** Time, money, skills, tools, materials and space.

Only then do you choose a method. The point is philosophical as well as practical: **no material or technique is "correct" in the abstract**. Fiberglass, foam, wood and a 3D print can all be right or wrong depending on the needs and means (H 20; [ch. 22](../4-fabrication-core/22-the-build-method.md)). Berls makes the same point about printers: the printer is a tool, not the lesson (B 118). So does Hart: "Knowing how to operate a 3D printer doesn't mean you know how to make a prop" (H 10).

## Theatrical truth vs. literal truth

- **Reading, not replicating.** On stage, detail is chosen for how it reads at a distance: some exaggerated, some faked (H 15). FF's food props aim for what looks real under lights, not what is real (FF 4–5; [ch. 41](../5-materials/41-prop-food.md)).
- **"Invented" isn't "in use."** James warns that an object being invented doesn't mean ordinary people owned it. What matters is what the characters would plausibly have had (J 9–10; [ch. 7](../2-admin/07-research-and-period-accuracy.md#invented-is-not-the-same-as-in-use)).
- **Real vs. fake.** Sometimes the real object is best (a real snapped bean, a real drink). Sometimes a fake is better because it's safer, cheaper over a run, or more controllable (FF 4–5).
- **[General knowledge]** Theatre has long argued between **realism** (David Belasco and the Moscow Art Theatre filling stages with authentic objects) and **suggestion** (Peter Brook's "empty space," Brecht's visible theatricality, and minimalist staging in which one object stands for a whole world). Neither is correct. They are design choices, and the prop master's job is to serve the production's chosen language consistently.

## Objects as signs [General knowledge]

Theatre scholars, starting with the Prague School semioticians of the 1930s and 1940s (notably Jiří Veltruský's work on people and objects on stage), pointed out that **anything placed on stage becomes a sign**. A chair on stage is never just a chair: its period, condition, colour and placement all say something about the world and the people in it. Two useful consequences:
- **Nothing on stage is neutral.** An "ordinary" mug still says a decade, a class and a taste. Choose it on purpose.
- **Objects can change meaning.** A prop can be transformed by use (a broom becomes a horse, a letter becomes a weapon). Strong productions often build such transformations into the props plan.

## Process over product (B 23–32)

Berls argues that props training should value the **process**: planning with intent, failing safely, redesigning and reflecting, as much as the finished object. The finished prop is "an artifact" of that process (B 24, 31). He would rather hire someone who failed and came back to succeed than someone who has never failed (B 24). Hart's advice to learn each craft by making one small, complete object (H 9) comes from the same idea. Experience is built by finishing things.

## Economy and judgement

- **The project triangle**: cheap, fast or good; pick two (H 362; [ch. 8](../2-admin/08-budgets-and-paperwork.md#the-project-triangle-h-362)). Berls's version for printers balances budget, safety and time (B 16).
- **Labour has a cost.** Berls's example: a technical director's preferred bevels on 450 platform legs added about nine hours and more than $700 (B 115). A prop master's taste must be weighed against the show's resources.
- **Safety is not negotiable.** Every source puts safety above effect, accuracy and budget. See [ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md) and [Part 6](../6-weapons/42-weapons-safety-and-policy.md).

## For the classroom

- **One object, three worlds.** Give students the same chair. They choose and justify versions for a realist kitchen drama, an expressionist nightmare and a bare-stage Shakespeare, explaining what each choice "says."
- **Chekhov's prop.** Students find a prop in a play that is planted early and pays off later, then propose how to make the audience notice it on first appearance without overplaying it.
- **Real or fake debate.** For five objects (a drink, a cake, a candle, a sword, a newspaper), teams argue real vs. fake on safety, cost over the run, control and effect.
- **Process journal.** For their next build, students keep a short journal (plan, problems, changes, outcome) and write a one-paragraph reflection in Berls's style: what went well, what to improve, what to change about the process.
