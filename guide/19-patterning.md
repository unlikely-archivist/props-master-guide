# 19. Patterning and Draping

> **Sources:** H ch.12 (pp. 225–237); H ch.6 (pp. 95–97); H ch.8 (p. 140)
> **[Check current]** Pattern-unfolding software (e.g., "pepakura"-type tools) changes; Hart does not endorse specific programs.

**Patterning** builds 3D objects by cutting (and sometimes folding or bending) flat sheets from templates and joining their edges (H 226). The techniques come from several traditions — **costume and fashion**, **sheet metal**, **packaging dielines**, **paper craft**, and geometry — and the breakthroughs come from crossing them over: costume methods applied to EVA foam, sheet-metal developments applied to fabric, paper patterns used for steel armor.

## Fabric patterning (H 226–229)

- Fabric won't hold a shape unless stiffened or stuffed; most fabric objects are designed to *move* (a sack must expand to take a severed head and still keep it in).
- Shape comes from **seams** and **darts** (remove a wedge and sew it closed — a circle with a pie slice removed becomes a cone; darts can also be pinched without cutting). A **gusset** is a diamond or triangle *added* to expand fabric (the heel of a sock). Nips, gathers, and tucks refine fit.
- **Draping on a form:** costumers drape **muslin** on a dress form, pin, mark, and transfer to paper. Props makers do the same on any hard sculpture or object — there are no commercial patterns for dead geese (H 227). Record **grain lines, notches, and seam/hem allowances** on the paper pattern.
- **Learn by** working with existing patterns, **reverse-engineering** (unpicking) objects, and seeing patterns as collections of shapes you can rescale and recombine — a gunny-sack pattern, scaled and flipped, becomes a prisoner's hood (H 228). Commercial patterns for bags, drapes, animals, and dolls (scalable to human size) and many online patterns for helmets, gauntlets, armor, and horns save time (H 228).
- **Hybrid techniques:** a burlap scarecrow face was draped, then selectively stiffened with PVA and gathered with stitches (H 228).

### Soft sculpture (H 228–229)
Plush-toy logic for props — pillows, stuffed animals, realistic carcasses.
- Place seams where they disappear (color/texture boundaries) or where they become part of the design — similar to planning mold seams.
- Muslin patterns can mislead for very different fabrics; sometimes pattern in the real fabric.
- For symmetric objects, pattern half and flip.
- **Example (H 229):** a dead pheasant for *King Lear* — the bird was broken into components from a photo (legs, wings, body), each a simple two-piece pattern stuffed with batting and **sand** for weight, sewn together loosely so it flopped, then feathered.

### Tape-wrap patterning (H 229–230)
1. Wrap the object in cling film (small) or a garbage bag (large).
2. Apply at least two layers of masking, gaff, or duct tape in perpendicular directions — snug and flat.
3. Draw seam lines on the tape; cut the shell off.
4. Slit additional darts until every piece lies flat.
5. Trace onto fabric, leather, foam, etc.
(The same idea as costumers' "duct-tape dress forms.")

Software can now unfold 3D models into flat patterns, some accounting for fabric stretch (H 229).

## Surface developments (H 230–233)

From the sheet-metal trade: unfold every surface of a 3D shape into a flat **development** (or "stretchout").
- Break complex shapes into **geometric solids** — cube, prism, cylinder, cone, pyramid, tetrahedron, polyhedron, sphere — and **truncate** as needed (a truncated cone = bucket).
- A **net** is one arrangement of a solid's faces that folds up into it; most solids have several possible nets.
- **Spheres** can only be approximated from flat stock — use **gores** (like a globe; more gores = rounder). Stretch fabric needs fewer gores; sheet metal can be hammered into domes instead.
- Use developments when the object must be **hollow, open, or transparent** (boxes, buckets, megaphones, a clear acrylic canopy made of two circles and a rolled rectangle), when working in sheet materials, or when you want to **print graphics directly** on the flat and fold it up — and ship it flat (H 233).

## Paper patterns and pepakura (H 234–236)

- **Paper craft** (cut-and-fold, distinct from origami's single-sheet folding): score thick stock at folds; convex folds and seams leave gaps — bevel, tape, or fill them unless the piece will be covered or is just a mock-up; add **glue tabs**.
- **Pepakura**-style software unfolds digital models into printable, numbered patterns.
- **As a pattern for rigid materials:** test fit in cardstock, then trace onto metal or plastic — Hart's aluminum footlights and Debi Jolly's gauntlet started this way (H 234–235).
- **As the final object:** seal seams (tape/resin) → stiffen inside with resin (seal paper with PVA or shellac first if using plaster or mâché) → skim outside *thinly* with resin or body filler → sand and refill until facets become smooth curves (H 234–236).
- Software can also slice models into **stacked cardboard layers** (H 236).

## Foam patterning (H 236–237)

- **EVA foam** is patterned like paper (paper pattern → trace → cut → hot-glue). Its thickness means convex joints need **beveled edges** (harder when the foam is also curved). Rigid fillers defeat the foam's flexibility — cover the piece with fabric or a flexible coating instead, or accept some gaps. Layer thin EVA for raised details.
- **Puppet polyfoam** (soft PU): thick enough for glued edge joints (contact cement); spray-mount stretch knit fabric to the hidden side for strength, especially when stitching; shape by **pulling and pinching** (glue/stitches) or by **carving**; multiple darts make domes.

## Patterns for hard materials (H 95–97, 140)

Card-stock patterns precede sheet-metal armor; paper templates precede repeated wood parts; a cardboard mock-up can be disassembled to become its own pattern ([ch. 12](12-drawings-measuring-and-layout.md#mock-ups-and-patterns-h-9597)).

## For the classroom

- **Tape-wrap lab:** Students tape-wrap a found object (helmet, shoe, small animal figure), flatten the pattern, and sew a fabric or EVA "skin" of it.
- **Developments:** Students develop and fold a truncated cone (bucket), a cylinder with end caps, and an 8-gore sphere from cardstock — then repeat one in aluminum or styrene.
- **Pepakura-to-object:** Print a simple low-poly model pattern, build it in cardstock, then stiffen and skim it into a smooth, paintable prop (requires resin safety setup or a water-based alternative).
