## What makes an effects prop different (M 9–10; H 9–10)

**Manual and special-effects props** have a trick to them, or are used in a way that would be dangerous or unconventional in real life. Mussman's examples:
- burning crucifixes
- burnt meatloaf
- campfires
- crash boxes
- exploding clocks
- gunshots
- magic tricks
- neck snaps
- rats that ooze blood

These props need special care. **Clean, reset, pick up or lock them up after every use.** They may be operated by actors or by crew (M 9–10).

Hart's *Prop Building Guidebook* deliberately leaves out trick props (breakaways, magic, edibles, electrical, pneumatic, mechanical and radio-controlled props), which the *Effects Guidebook* covers (H 9–10).

Three rules apply to every effect:
1. **Never ask an actor to perform an effect you haven't tested yourself.** If you haven't tried it, you don't know whether it works safely or how to teach it (M 149).
2. **Get expert help.** Special-effects suppliers want you to succeed safely and will recommend products and protective gear (M 149). Complex shows may hire an **effects designer**. Flame and explosives generally require a **licensed pyrotechnician** (H 7).
3. **Research the rules before you plan.** Check union rules (Actors' Equity for professional actors and stage managers) and state and local law. They may restrict guns, pyro, haze or fog, and smoking in your venue (M 149).

## Weapons and firearms (M 147–149; H 7)

> **Important update.** Mussman's 2008 advice describes firing blanks from real guns onstage under strict procedures. **Practice has tightened considerably since then**, particularly after the fatal 2021 shooting on the film set of *Rust*. Many theatres and schools now **prohibit real firearms entirely**, using non-firing replicas, blank-firing replicas handled by a qualified person, or recorded or offstage sound effects instead. **Follow your institution's weapons policy, current union guidance and local law. Use a qualified armorer or weapons master where required.** The procedures below are historical context and a checklist of hazards, not authorization.

What the sources establish:
- **Film uses real firearms modified to fire blanks**, which still require licensing. A **licensed armorer** handles transport, storage and the practical details, while the prop master remains ultimately responsible (H 7).
- **Blanks are dangerous.** Wadding can penetrate flesh at close range. Never point any gun, "loaded" or not, at anyone, including the audience. Fire upward and away from the body for sound effects. Wear eye and ear protection (M 147–148).
- **One designated person** cleans, loads, hands off, retrieves and locks up each weapon. Load just before use and unload immediately after. Keep fingers off triggers. Never use a prop gun for pranks (M 147–148).
- **Starter pistols** have blocked barrels and fire only blanks; they're commonly used for offstage shots (M 147).
- Have any guns in stock inspected by licensed professionals, and learn how they work (M 148).
- **Dull every blade and sharp edge** on onstage knives, swords and axes (bench grinder, stone, metal sandpaper) (M 148). Stage-combat blades are made of **spring steel** (e.g., 5160) so they flex instead of snapping (H 158). Film combat blades in 7075 aluminum read as steel on camera, but dent too quickly for a stage run (H 160). An axe that really splits a melon nightly needs a genuine tool-steel edge (H 159), which is exactly the kind of prop that needs a careful safety plan.
- **Lock up all weapons, real or not**, every night (M 148–149; see also [ch. 7](07-rehearsals-tech-and-performance.md#performances-m-137138)).
- Coordinate weapons, breakaways and fights with the **fight director**, or the **stunt department** in film (H 4).
- For period names and dates of armor, polearms, swords and firing mechanisms (matchlock, wheel lock, flintlock), see [Appendix E §11](../appendices/E-period-props-timeline.md#11-arms-and-armor) and [ch. 27](27-period-research.md#arms-armor-and-authority-vocabulary-first). Accuracy never overrides the safety rules above.

## Breakaways (H 3–4; H 77, 190–191, 274–275)

- **Two kinds** (H 3–4):
  - Rigged to break predictably and **reassemble** (chairs, swords).
  - **Single-use** pieces that break without injuring anyone, either on impact or from the shards. **Sugar-glass** bottles shatter safely over a head. Single-use breakaways are also **consumables**, so budget for the whole run.
- **Magnets** make removable and breakaway joints without holes or residue (H 77).
- Flexible materials and **cushioning** protect both actor and prop. Film stunt weapons are often single castings of foam rubber (H 190–191).
- **Gelatin** casts make **breakaway prosthetics** (Gloucester's eyes in *King Lear*). Gelatin softens under stage lights, so make them fresh (H 274–275).

## Food and drink (M 7–8, 156–157; H 6)

- **Consumable food must be fresh for every performance.** Food poisoning has serious consequences for actors and for your reputation. Props supplies the ingredients; the run crew usually prepares the food, so **demonstrate the preparation** to them (M 8, 129).
- Ask about allergies and dietary needs **[added best practice]**.
- **Stage drinks** (M 156–157):
  - **Champagne**: ginger ale (it bubbles, and a shaken bottle still pops its cork).
  - **Dancing waiters with "full" glasses**: a disc of amber lighting gel inside the glass. This works for wine too.
  - **Dark liquor**: diluted tea, matched in a clear glass against the real thing. **Clear liquor**: water. **Wine**: juice or food coloring.
  - **Beer**: relabeled bottles of warm ginger ale or lemon-lime soda, re-capped with a bottle capper. Make printed labels for soda cans.
- **Fake food** (non-edible) (M 157):
  - Materials: clay, polystyrene, **spray foam** (expands about 3×; good for muffins and bread), casting resin, sponges, upholstery foam, chamois, Model Magic.
  - **Baker's clay** for baked goods (H 250–251).
  - Polyurethane foam cubes as an hors d'oeuvre base (H 181).
  - Real food dries and browns quickly. **Don't shellac real food** to "preserve" it. Mussman's (possibly apocryphal) story of a shellacked roast hen exploding under stage lights makes the point.
- Hart: fake food is often built because buying and preparing real food every night is expensive and wasteful (H 6).
- **For building inedible food in depth** (salt dough, candle wax, carved foam, cast food, clear-rubber drinks), see [ch. 28](28-fake-food.md) and [Appendix F](../appendices/F-fake-food-recipe-cards.md) (FF). For one edible bite inside a fake dish, see the [edible inset](28-fake-food.md#fake-and-real-together-the-edible-inset-ff-194200) (FF 194–200). Harrison & Honesty's sequel on edible props and allergens (*Volume 2*, 2025) isn't incorporated yet.

## Smoking (M 149–150)

> **[Check current]** Smoking laws, including those covering herbal cigarettes and e-cigarettes, vary widely and have changed since 2008. Check state and local law. If the law prohibits it, inform your supervisor and the artistic director; it's their call whether to accept the risk.

Where smoking is allowed:
- Use the mildest available product.
- Use **heavy ashtrays**, removably secured with putty or rolled gaff tape, with **clear liquid soap** in the bottom to snuff butts.
- Put **sand buckets** on both sides backstage for actors exiting with lit cigarettes.
- **Warn the audience.** The house manager posts signs and the stage manager supplies the details. Refund policy is the box office's call.

## Live flame (M 150–151; M 10)

- **Have working, inspected fire extinguishers onstage before any live flame**, even a match.
- Mussman: avoid live flame whenever possible. A battery candle takes a few dollars and a few hours; one real candle takes weeks of planning.
- If flame is unavoidable:
  - Use **wide, freestanding pillar candles** or securely held candles.
  - Light Sterno-type torches just before the entrance and extinguish them immediately on exit.
  - Flame-proof the surrounding fabrics and finishes.
- Fire codes and the fire marshal govern live flame. A permit may be required **[check current]**.

## Live sound effects (M 34, 9)

Some directors and sound designers prefer live sound. Props may supply:
- **crash boxes** (a wooden box of metal and plastic junk to shake or drop)
- **thunder sheets**
- **door-slam effects**
- offstage gunshot effects (see the weapons section above)

## Practicals and electrics (M 10, 32; H 2)

Lamps, chandeliers, lanterns and battery "flames" are **practicals**. Coordinate early with the **master electrician** and **lighting designer**: wiring, control from the board, brightness, color. Battery-operated flame effects are usually preferred to live flame. Running water and working appliances also count as practicals. *[Pending: Effects Guidebook chs. 2–3 and 11, "Electricity, Wiring, Soldering"; "Lights"; "Control."]*

## Blood and messy effects (M 8; H 3)

Stage blood is a consumable. Plan cleaning, costume coordination (wardrobe needs to know the blood and the fabric) and replacement quantities. *[Pending: Effects Guidebook ch. 7, "Liquid Delivery."]*

## For the classroom

- **Effect safety plan.** For one effect (a breakaway bottle, a practical oil lamp, an onstage smoke), students write a full plan: hazards, controls, who operates and resets it, storage, an emergency response, and the "I tested it myself" log.
- **Stage-drinks lab.** Students produce convincing champagne, whiskey, red wine and beer for a scene, and present their solutions for nightly prep and cleanup.
- **Policy research.** Students find and summarize your own institution's weapons, flame and haze policies and the current relevant union rules, then compare them with Mussman's 2008 guidance and discuss what changed and why.
