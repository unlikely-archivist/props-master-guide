# 6. Sourcing: Pull, Borrow, Rent, Buy, Build

> **Sources:** M ch.6 (pp. 78–91), ch.8 (pp. 109–119); H ch.1 (pp. 4–6), ch.5 (pp. 60–61), ch.19 (pp. 363–364)
> **[Check current]** Store types and suppliers change. Build and keep your own vendor list.

## Start with rehearsal props

The first physical step in propping a show is supplying **rehearsal props**, the stand-ins actors use from the start of blocking (M 109). Mussman insists this is *not* "propping the show twice": digging through stock for rehearsal props shows you which performance props you already own (M 109–110). More in [ch. 7](07-rehearsals-tech-and-performance.md).

## The four-way to-do list (M 110–112)

Go through the prop list and cost breakdown prop by prop and sort each into one of four lists:

| List | Typical candidates (M 111) |
|---|---|
| **To Find / Pull** | Items already in stock, or findable for free |
| **To Borrow / Rent** | Set props and dressing (often too costly to buy) |
| **To Buy** | Building materials, consumables, and things cheaper to buy than to rent or hunt down |
| **To Build** | Show-specific items, props that take abuse or must work a particular way, things that will be permanently altered, anything that must match designer drawings |

Questions to ask for each prop (M 111):
- Is it in stock?
- Will it be hard to find?
- Is it cheaper to buy or to build?
- Can it wait (dressing), or is it a priority (hand props)?
- How is it used?
- Does it need special ordering, with lead time?
- Does it still fit the budget?
- Who can do it: crew or overhire?

The list makes your plan visible and lets assistants **pick up where you left off**. It will change constantly: borrowed items turn into purchases, purchases into rentals, and props you spent days building get cut (M 111–112). ## Find and pull (M 118–119)

Good places to look:
- your own prop storage
- friends' and family's closets
- forgotten corners of the theatre building
- friendly theatres and schools
- board members' basements
- dumpsters (*"one man's trash..."*). Mussman notes this is risky and sometimes illegal.

Pulling from stock is efficient, but **don't permanently alter valuable stock pieces** you use regularly (M 119).

**Use the buddy system.** Don't go alone into sketchy places or lift heavy objects alone. "Props aren't worth that much" (M 119).

## Borrow (M 112–113)

Borrowing builds relationships with schools, other theatres, businesses and private lenders, and those relationships grow over time.

- **Barter** where your institution allows it, with comp tickets or a program credit (M 112).
- **Thank lenders**: a sincere note on letterhead, and a program acknowledgment if marketing agrees and you ask before the print deadline (M 113).

## Rent (M 113–115)

Prop rental houses operate in most major cities, and some theatres and schools rent out their stock. Before renting, check (M 113):

- Have I read and understood the rental terms?
- **Could I afford to pay full value if it is damaged?**
- Is renting cheaper than buying a similar item?
- Do I have budget for it?
- Am I allowed to alter it?
- Are the start and end dates correct?
- Have the director and designer approved it?
- Will I be refunded for an early return?

Renting can end a long search, but it isn't always cheaper than buying (M 114).

### Borrowing and renting etiquette (M 114–115)
- **If it can't come back in the same condition, buy or build instead.** Don't borrow anything you could never replace in good faith.
- **If something breaks, tell the owner.** Take responsibility and offer a repair or professional restoration.
- Follow the agreement and return items on time. Your reputation precedes you.
- **Give stage management a list of every borrowed or rented item** ("Heads up, that's rented"). It helps prevent damage and heads off a director's urge to alter the item.

### Rental and loan agreements (M 39–40, 90–91)
Use one **in both directions**, whether you are borrowing or lending, and write one if the lender has none. Typical contents:
- borrow (free) or rent (fee)
- renter's identity and contact details
- itemized list with description, **condition at checkout** and value
- start and return dates
- deposit and fees
- rules on alterations (written permission only)
- late-return penalties
- liability for loss or damage (usually full value plus forfeiture of the deposit)
- payment terms
- signatures and dates from both parties, each keeping a copy

A checklist version is in [Appendix B](../appendices/B-paperwork-templates.md#5-rentalloan-agreement-checklist). Have your institution approve the actual legal form.

## Buy (M 115–118)

Good places to shop:
- antique stores
- resale and thrift stores
- dollar stores
- craft and hardware stores
- discount retailers
- online

Buying tips (M 117):
- Take time to hunt for bargains, and resist impulse buys.
- Choose generic over name brand and used over new.
- **Check return policies.** Yard sales, auctions and many thrift shops don't take returns. Buying unusable props with an unreturnable budget is the worst outcome.
- When time is short, sometimes you go with your gut. Just be sure it's usable (M 118).

### Specialized supply sources (H 363–364)
| Need | Try |
|---|---|
| Lumber, sheet goods, hardware | Lumberyards, builder's merchants, home centers |
| Steel, aluminum stock | Steel suppliers |
| Pipe, PVC, fittings | Plumbing suppliers |
| Small metal parts, tools | Jewelers' suppliers |
| Paints, sculpting, casting, foams, plastics | Art and craft stores |
| Resin, fiberglass | Auto-parts and marine suppliers |
| Fabric, foam, leather, cane | Fabric, upholstery and leather suppliers, cane shops |
| Miniatures, detail parts | Hobby and model-railroad shops |
| Finishes, cleaners | Antique restorers, shoe-repair shops |
| Mineral oil, glycerin, acetone, alcohol, plaster bandage | Pharmacies |
| Foil, gelatin, containers | Grocery stores |
| Corrugated plastic | Sign shops |
| Hot-melt vinyl | Fishing-supply stores |
| Theatre-specific goods | Theatrical suppliers (mainly in larger cities) |

Manufacturers will usually tell you which retailers stock their products. Weigh shipping costs and delivery time against driving time. **Keep a vendor list** (H 364) and a contacts list: antique shops, area prop masters, designers, rental houses, repair shops, other theatres and freelancers (M 83).

### Catalogs and references (M 78–82)
Keep a working library of how-to and reference books (furniture styles, period ads, the 1897 Sears catalog reprint, weapons, bottles, coins and so on) and a **research file** of clippings, photos and instructions from past shows. If you keep supplier catalogs, **don't order from outdated ones**, and respect copyright when reproducing images. For organizing a research file and checking dates, see [ch. 27](27-period-research.md#building-a-research-file).

## Build (M 115–117; H 5–6)

Theatre "builds" everything. Build when:
- the prop is specific to the play or design
- it must survive abuse
- the antique would be too fragile
- it must look, weigh, feel or function a particular way

Follow designer drawings, and deviate only with the designer's approval (M 116). Part 4 of this guide covers building in depth. Two quick strategies:

- **Found objects and kitbashing.** Model makers "kitbash" by borrowing parts from commercial kits. For a prop maker, "the world is their kit to bash" (H 60–61). A golf ball becomes a curtain-rod finial (M 116). Collect interesting parts as you find them and organize them by material, by shape, or both (H 61).
- **Beating "creator's block"** (M 116–117):
  1. Research how similar real objects are built.
  2. If there's no reference, picture the finished object in detail.
  3. Mentally take it apart: how do the parts connect, and what's inside?
  4. Match each part to a material or found object.
  5. Still stuck? Ask a colleague.

## For the classroom

- **Sourcing sprint.** Give teams a prop list and a fake budget. Each team produces a four-way to-do list with justifications and a sourcing plan for the five hardest items.
- **Agreement drafting.** Students write a loan agreement for borrowing a valuable antique from a private lender. Discuss what makes the lender comfortable.
- **Kitbash challenge.** Students build a "sci-fi device" or "steampunk tool" from thrift-store and hardware-store found objects within a time limit.
