# 11. The Build Method: Needs and Means

> **Sources:** H ch.2 (pp. 11–24), ch.10 (pp. 182–191); M ch.8 (pp. 115–117)

Hart's central idea is a **universal method for building any prop**, whether a chair, a curtain or a dead deer (H 12):

> **Determine all the needs of the prop, then meet them with the means you have available.**

Complex props are broken into simpler parts, each with its own needs, until every part can be matched with a material and a technique.

---

## Part 1: What does the prop need to do?

### Look, in context
- The prop must look right **under the actual conditions**: gold under stage lights from 20 feet away is different from gold in your hand (H 12).
- It may need to **match the production** rather than reality. If the scenery is painted plywood imitating mahogany, a painted chair will look more cohesive than a real mahogany one (H 12).
- Art directors say: **"All the camera sees is the last coat of paint."** Color and texture can be changed, so the outer appearance rarely dictates the inner material (H 12, 183).

### Other sensory properties
How does it **sound**? Coins dropping on a metal plate can't be faked with painted poker chips. How does it **move**? A silk flag waves differently from a muslin one (H 12). How does it **feel**? Hart gave a dead lamb a flexible neck so it would flop realistically (H 13).

### Function, including what it must *not* do
- Will it be danced on, carried, hidden behind a back, dropped? "Shouldn't break when dropped" is a need too (H 12).
- **Plan for the worst case.** Told an actor will stand on it, assume they'll jump. Told a vase is dropped, assume it's thrown. **"It is better to overbuild a prop once than to have to rebuild it three times"** (H 13).
- Actors deviate from blocking in performance and handle props harder than in rehearsal (H 13).

### Size, weight, balance
- **Size**: set by the designer, but also by practical limits (the vase it must hold, the pocket it must fit) (H 14).
- **Weight**: lighter is usually better, but add weight outdoors so it won't blow away, or for realism, such as a "packed" suitcase (H 14).
- **Balance**: combat weapons are weighted toward the hand; tall items are weighted low (H 15).
- **Offstage path**: it must fit through the smallest door, elevator or car trunk between shop and stage, so design it to knock down if needed. Consider backstage storage and how much the crew can lift (H 15).
  - KF splits long foam props for travel with nested PVC pipes held by embedded magnets, and lists other knock-down options (KF 18–19; [ch. 29](29-eva-foam-props.md#making-props-come-apart-kf-1819-42)).

### Medium-specific needs (H 15–16)

| Theatre | Film | Television |
|---|---|---|
| Must survive a whole run | May only need one day, but breaking on set costs money | Must survive weeks of shooting and off-season storage |
| Audience distance: some detail exaggerated, some faked | Extreme close-ups; finish all sides (no backstage) | HD detail |
| **Sightlines**: shorten tall furniture on thrust and arena stages | Lens choice distorts | 3–4 days from script to shoot |
| **Raked stages** need **counter-raked** props and anti-slide measures | Changes are made on the spot, so quick-to-modify props are best | |
| Live safety: a loose part can hit the front row | | |
| Changes through previews | | |
| **Flame-proofing** requirements | | |

Hart also notes the effect of **"theatre props in a movie world"**: audiences, directors and actors increasingly expect cinematic realism even at a distance, and a poorly made prop can undermine an actor's performance even if the audience can't see the flaw (H 15).

### Research and reference
**Never assume you know what something looks like.** "We all know what a dog's head looks like until the time comes to sculpt one." You can't have too many reference images (H 18). Build samples of paint treatments and textures for approval (H 17–18).

**A successful prop is one built to do exactly what it needs to do.** Elegant methods and advanced skills are nice, but they're secondary (H 16–17).

## Part 2: What are your means?

- **Money and time** are the main resources. See [ch. 5](05-budget-time-and-feasibility.md).
- **Know material properties**: transparency, how a material shapes and joins, strength, weight, looks, and the time it takes to work. Cutting a 1" steel bar takes far longer than cutting 1" of wood, which matters a hundred cuts later (H 18).
- **Use stock shapes close to the final form.** Need a ½" tabletop? Use ½" plywood. Need a hemisphere? Buy a foam hemisphere rather than gluing up and carving a wood block (H 18–19).
- **Strength comes in kinds** (H 19):
  - **compression**: resistance to crushing
  - **tension**: resistance to pulling apart
  - **shear**: resistance to sideways force. A drywall screw snaps where a nail bends.

  Know how a material fails, too: does it break, buckle, bend permanently, or spring back?
- **Practical questions** (H 19):
  - Do you know this material?
  - Is there time to experiment?
  - Do you have the tools?
  - Can you get the material in time, and at what shipping cost?
  - Is there room to work?
  - **Is the safety infrastructure in place?**
- **Cost vs. labor**: cheap materials that need hours of finishing can cost more overall (H 20).
- **Sustainability**: reuse, buy local, plan the prop's afterlife, and design for disassembly (H 20).
- **Show-wide trade-offs**: spending more on one prop can be balanced by saving on another (H 20).

> There is no "wrong" way to build a prop. Best practices exist because foam, wood, papier-mâché, plaster, resin, steel and leather reliably meet common needs. (H 20)

## The five ways to shape any material (H 21–22)

| Process | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Cutting** | Removing material: slicing, sawing, carving, drilling. **Machining** is precise cutting | Saws, routers, lathes |
| **Assembling** | Joining parts, permanently or temporarily | Glue, screws, welding, sewing |
| **Bending** | Deforming without adding or removing material | Bent rod, molded leather mask |
| **Modeling** | Shaping amorphous plastic material | Clay |
| **Hardening** | A liquid or paste solidifies by drying, chemical cure, or heating and cooling | Joint compound, resin, metal, wax; also coatings and paints |

Compound processes are combinations of these:
- **Fabrication** = cut + bend + assemble (wood, metal, plastics).
- **Sculpting** = cut and/or model, often followed by hardening.
- **Molding and casting** = harden around a master, then harden inside the cavity.
- **Filling** = model + harden.
- **Lamination** (papier-mâché, fiberglass) = cut + saturate + harden.

When you meet an unfamiliar material, ask which of the five it responds to (H 22).

## Breaking complex shapes into simple parts (H 17, 22–24)

Very few props are one piece. A complex sword hilt is a stack of simple shapes (H 17). A **chair** has four legs, a seat and a back. The back legs usually continue into the back for strength, and stretchers keep the legs from splaying. Once you understand that breakdown, you can build anything chair-like (H 22–23).

**Example: Yoruban throne** (H 23). An ornately carved throne was treated as **an ordinary chair with sculptural figures glued on**. Because it would be fully painted, the chair could be wood and each figure could be sculpted in whatever material the artisan knew best. Students sculpted figures in parallel while the chair was built.

**Even a curtain follows the method.** It needs to look and act like a curtain, so the obvious material is fabric. Swatch within budget, cut, sew a rod pocket, hem. If your shop can't sew, buy curtains instead (H 23–24).

B's version of the same check, for tools and techniques: a technical director's preferred 25° bevels on 450 platform legs added about nine hours and more than $700 in labour, power and blades. Make sure a method or device actually saves time or money, or adds safety (B 115; [ch. 30](30-3d-printing.md#further-projects-b-102116)).

## Workflow

- Some makers plan everything on paper and others improvise. Hart works in between: **plan every process up front, build what's certain, then reassess** (H 22).
- Planning catches sequence traps. Hart's example is fur-covering an animatronic before realizing the motor needs an access panel (H 22).

## Structure vs. surface (H 183–191)

Because surfaces can be faked, build a **structure** to meet the strength, weight and rigidity needs, then **skin** it to look like anything. A giant skull doesn't need to be a solid foam block; an internal frame with a thin carved shell is lighter and cheaper (H 183). Principles are in [ch. 17](17-structure-and-armatures.md).

## For the classroom

- **Needs worksheet.** For a single prop (e.g., "a bottle smashed over a head nightly" or "a throne an actor leaps onto"), students list every need: look, sound, feel, function, size, weight, balance, offstage path, durability, safety, flame code. Then propose two different material/method solutions and evaluate each against the needs.
- **Breakdown drawing.** Students take a complex object (a lantern, a rifle, an ornate mirror) and exploded-sketch it into simple geometric components, assigning a material to each.
- **Five-process sort.** Name a technique (vacuum forming, slush casting, kerfing, saddle stitching, lathe turning) and have students identify which of the five processes it combines.
