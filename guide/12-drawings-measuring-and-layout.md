# 12. Drawings, Measuring, Layout, and Jigs

> **Sources:** H ch.6 (pp. 78–99); M ch.5 (pp. 56–57), ch.11 (pp. 159–162)

## From reference to material

Your starting point is a reference: a drafting, a sketch, a photo, a real object, or a verbal description. You then transfer it to material by measuring and marking (H 79). Reading designer drawings is covered in [ch. 4](04-script-analysis-and-the-prop-list.md#reading-prop-drawings-h-7983). This chapter covers turning references into measurements and accurate parts.

## Getting measurements from photos and objects (H 81–83)

- **Proportion first.** Correct proportions make a prop "look right" more than exact numbers do. Establish proportions, then scale everything from one critical dimension.
- **Compare against known things** in the photo: a staff as tall as the person holding it, your own hand against a hand-held object.
- **Standard sizes** are a guide. Doors and chair seats have standard heights. Mussman's typical dimensions (M 159–160):
  - chair seat 18"
  - dining table 30"
  - end table 20–24"
  - coffee table 18"
  - shelves 10–12" apart and no more than 12" deep

  Even fantasy objects read better within normal proportions; an oversized book should still have book proportions (H 82).
- **Documenting a real object** (you may get one chance):
  - Shoot front, side and top views, both sides if it's asymmetrical, and isometric views.
  - **Record overall width, length and height.**
  - Include a **scale reference** (a ruler, your hand, a coin).
- **Lens distortion.** Phone and consumer cameras shoot wide-angle, which exaggerates depth. Step back and zoom in, or use a "normal" lens. Shoot square-on to avoid converging lines (H 82).
- **Draw it yourself.** Your own drawings resolve the measurements and expose impossibilities before you commit materials (H 83).

## Measuring tools and accuracy (H 83–86; M 56–57)

| Tool | Use | Accuracy tips |
|---|---|---|
| Rulers, steel straightedges | Measuring, drawing and cutting straight lines | Keep parallel to the edge. Metal is most accurate; wood swells |
| **Tape measure** (25–30 ft typical) | General measuring | The sliding hook compensates for inside vs. outside measurements. **Check it against a straightedge**, because a bent hook makes every measurement wrong |
| Cloth/fiberglass tape | Fabric, curves | Cloth stretches up to ½" over several feet; reinforced tapes are better |
| **Calipers** | Diameters, small parts | Dial and digital models read directly; simple ones compare |
| **Architect's scale** | Reading scale drawings | US designers work in ¼" or ½" = 1'-0"; metric designers in 1:25 or 1:50. **Check the scale**: if a chair comes out 12 feet tall, you're on the wrong side. Engineer's scales differ |

- **Use one measuring device per project** so measurements stay consistent (H 85).
- **"Burning an inch"** (measuring from the 1" mark) avoids a bad hook, but it's easy to forget to subtract (H 85).
- **Volume for casting**: dunk the object in water and measure the displacement, or fill it with dry sand (H 85).
- **Drilling depth**: use a stop collar or a **masking-tape flag** on the bit (H 85–86).

## Marking (H 86–92)

- Mark with a **sharp pencil**, or a **marking knife** for line-width precision (circle knife marks in pencil so you can see them).
- **Mark the waste side.** Saw blades have **kerf** (width). Cut on the waste side of the line or your 6" piece becomes 5⅞" (H 86).
- **Squares**:
  - try square, combination square, framing square, speed square
  - **sliding bevel** to copy angles
  - bevel protractor
  - clear plastic drafting tools for fabric and paper

  **Check a square** by drawing a line, flipping the tool and drawing again. A V between the lines means it's off (H 86–88).
- **Check boxes for square by measuring the diagonals.** Equal diagonals mean square, so rack the box until they match (H 88).
- **Circles**: a good compass, or **trammel points** on a stiff bar for big circles (string stretches). **Dividers** step off equal spaces (H 88–89).
- **Geometry tricks** (H 89–92):
  - The diagonals of a rectangle cross at its center.
  - Compass arcs give perpendiculars, bisectors and angle bisections (useful for molding miters).
  - **To divide a board into N equal parts without math**, angle a ruler across it so that 0 and a multiple of N land on the edges (e.g., 21" for 7 parts), mark every 3", then square the marks across.

## Transferring and enlarging (H 90–94)

- Full-scale drawings on paper or a drafting table; CAD printed at full scale and tiled across sheets.
- Carbon or transfer paper. Or shade the back of your drawing with graphite and trace.
- Trace directly through translucent materials.
- **Pouncing**: perforate the drawing with a pounce wheel and dust chalk through the holes.
- Temporarily adhere the drawing to the material with spray adhesive.
- **Enlarging**: photocopy or tile-print, or project and trace (keep the projector square-on to avoid keystone distortion; lines thicken as they enlarge), or use the **grid method**: grid the reference, draw a larger grid on the material and copy square by square.

## Cut lists (H 94–95)

A **cut list** records every piece and its dimensions. It lets you estimate materials and batch your cutting, so you reset machines less often.

**The classic mistake is ignoring material thickness.** For an open-top 20" × 20" × 28" box in ¾" plywood:

| Piece | Qty | Size |
|---|---|---|
| Bottom | 1 | 20 × 20 |
| Sides (full width) | 2 | 20 × 27¼ |
| Sides (between) | 2 | 18½ × 27¼ |

Lay the parts out on a 4'×8' sheet to maximize usable offcuts, and **label your cut parts** so no one mistakes them for scrap (H 94–95). For tricky geometry, it's fine to build part of the prop and develop the rest of the cut list as you go.

## Mock-ups and patterns (H 95–97)

- **Mock-ups** in cardboard or scrap test scale, balance and feel, especially before committing expensive materials. A cardboard saddle mock-up is one example. See [ch. 7](07-rehearsals-tech-and-performance.md) for rehearsal use.
- **Patterns** record full-size shapes: a paper chair-leg template traced several times, or a cardstock armor pattern before cutting steel. A **flush-trim router bit** can copy a template exactly.
- **CAD and 3D software** let you mock up virtually, show the designer your intent, solve angles and hypotenuses, print templates, and drive CNC or laser machines. They don't replace knowing materials (H 97).

## Jigs (H 97–99)

A **jig** holds a fixed spatial relationship, either between tool and material or between parts. It can be as simple as a spacer block. Examples:
- band-saw circle jig
- taper jig
- drill-press stop block for repeat holes
- a jig holding a chandelier bowl while its arms are welded on
- a router circle jig

Use a jig whenever a task is **repetitive** (hundreds of evenly spaced tacks) or demands **precision**. Hart's observation: **experienced artisans spend more time measuring, laying out and building jigs; novices jump in with the saw** (H 97). A table saw is itself "a collection of jigs for a circular saw" (H 99).

## For the classroom

- **Photo-to-drawing.** Give students a photo of a period object with one known dimension. They produce a dimensioned orthographic drawing, then compare against the real measurements.
- **Cut-list quiz.** Students produce cut lists for three boxes in different materials and joint styles, then build one and check their math.
- **Jig challenge.** Teams design and build a jig that lets anyone cut ten identical parts, and present it to the class.
