# 9. The Prop Shop: Space, Tools, and Supplies

> **Sources:** M ch.5 (pp. 46–72); H ch.4 (pp. 45–58)
> **[Check current]** Product and brand names change. Building and fire codes for shops vary by jurisdiction; in a university, check with Environmental Health & Safety (EHS) and facilities.

## Spaces

Most prop masters maintain three spaces: an **office**, a **workshop**, and **storage** (M 46). Storage is covered in [ch. 8](08-strike-storage-and-records.md).

### The office (M 46–48)
Whatever form it takes, from a desk in a scene-shop corner to a dining table at home, you need somewhere to sit, plan, do paperwork, call, email, and keep your references. The basics:
- desk and chair
- filing cabinets and bookcases
- corkboard and whiteboard
- a computer with internet, a printer/scanner/copier, and a phone
- word-processing, spreadsheet and image-editing software
- binders, sheet protectors, a laminator and the usual office supplies

**Speak up about what you need.** Supervisors may not know (M 47).

### The workshop
**Layout like a kitchen work triangle** (H 46): give core processes dedicated stations (carpentry, soft goods, layout/drawing, molding and casting, metal) and keep flexible stations for everything else.

**Dirty room vs. clean room** (H 48):
- *Dirty*: woodworking, sanding, anything that makes dust.
- *Clean*: sewing and upholstery, gluing, clay, painting (kept away from dust), paper work.
- *Spray area or booth*: anything that releases fumes.
- *Metal area*: welding and grinding must be separated from flammable dust areas. OSHA requires a separate, separately ventilated room or 35 ft of distance (H 48) **[check current regulation]**. Many prop shops share the scene shop's metal area and machines.
- **Two sinks**: a **slop sink** for paint, plaster and casting cleanup, and a separate clean sink for food prep and washing props. Using one sink for both is unsanitary (H 48).
- Ideally an office or computer area for references and prop bibles.

**Ventilation** (H 46–47):
- Capture dust and fumes **at the source**: dust collection on machines, fume extractors at welding and soldering, spray booths and fume hoods.
- Provide general exhaust that doesn't recirculate into the shop, and fresh make-up air.
- Arrange airflow so it moves from the worker, past the material, to the exhaust.
- Hanging **air filtration units are not ventilation.** They neither bring in fresh air nor remove fumes.

**Floors** (H 47): smooth floors are easy to clean but treacherous under sawdust. On concrete, use anti-fatigue mats where people stand.

**Lighting** (H 47): good overhead light plus positionable task lights, and lights mounted on machines.

**Work tables** (H 47–48; M 50):
- Standard: a sturdy 2×4 (or heavier) frame with a 4'×8' top about 36" high.
- Plywood for strength, topped with replaceable **hardboard** for a smooth surface.
- **Locking casters** in small shops. Place tables away from walls so you can work from every side.
- Lower tables for sewing and detail work.
- Sawhorse tables for projects that need to sit overnight to dry.

**Emergency access** (H 43, 48):
- Keep ≥3 ft egress paths clear.
- Keep extinguishers, first-aid kits, eyewash stations, deluge showers, breaker panels and emergency stops reachable. **Never block them, not even "just this once."**

**Sharing space** (M 69–70): props often shares with the scene shop, the costume shop or the paint deck. Coordinate tool and table use, contain your mess, clean up after every session, and lock up.

Organize however works for your team, and feel free to reorganize an inherited shop (M 50). Board members always tour when the shop looks its worst, and a system makes cleanup fast (M 49).

## Tools: categories and choices

### Tool vs. machine (H 50–52)
- A **tool** is brought to the material. A **machine** has the material brought to it.
- **Hand tools** are human-powered. **Power tools** are corded or cordless. **Pneumatic tools** need compressed air.
- A **machine tool** constrains the cutter (a drill press), while a **freehand tool** goes anywhere (a cordless drill).

**Choosing between them** (H 51–52):
- Can the material be moved to and through the machine? If not, use a portable tool.
- Machines offer **precision** through fences and tables. Hand tools need **jigs** to match it.
- Power offers **speed**. Hand tools offer **control** for delicate work, like a rasp for the last bit of a curve or a hand stitch to close a pillow.
- Choose the tool to fit the problem, not the problem to fit your favorite tool.

**Machine anatomy** (H 52):
- **Table**: positions the work relative to the cutter.
- **Fence**: guides the work (adjustable on a table saw, fixed on a miter saw).
- **Shoe/base plate**: on handheld tools.

**Buying** (H 52–53):
- The right tool, even a cheap one, beats improvising with the wrong one.
- Buy general-purpose tools first and specialized ones when a technique recurs. A lathe is irreplaceable for turnings.
- **"Audition" a tool** by buying the cheap version. If you wear it out, upgrade, and you'll know which features matter.

### Tool families (H 53–58; M 55–68)

| Family | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Cutting** | Saws, knives, shears, routers, hot wire, plasma, laser | Blade vocabulary: **TPI** (keep ~3 teeth in the material; more TPI = smoother but slower and clogs more), **gullet**, **kerf**, **set** (H 53) |
| **Knives** | Utility/box cutter, **snap knife** (long fresh edge, good for foam), hobby/X-Acto, mat knife | KF instead resharpens a box-cutter blade on a stone or 1200-grit paper every few cuts for EVA foam (KF 8, 14; [ch. 29](29-eva-foam-props.md#where-the-sources-disagree)) |
| **Shearing** | Scissors, tin snips, paper guillotine | Clean cut, thin stock only (H 55) |
| **Rotary cutters** | Router, router table, shaper, planer, jointer | **Router**: loud, unguarded, torquey; a loose collet can launch a bit. Also extraordinarily versatile (H 55) |
| **Gripping/turning** | Pliers, wrenches, screwdrivers | Everyone in entertainment should carry an **adjustable ("crescent") wrench** (H 55; M 61) |
| **Striking** | Claw, tack, ball-peen hammers; rubber/rawhide mallets | A hammer multiplies force. Match its size to the task and the user. **Don't hammer with a drill** (H 56) |
| **Prying/pulling** | Pry bars, staple and tack pullers, pincers | Never pry with a screwdriver or chisel (H 56) |
| **Abrading/scraping** | Files, rasps, sanders, grinders; planes, chisels, scrapers | |
| **Measuring/marking** | See [ch. 12](12-drawings-measuring-and-layout.md) | |
| **Boring** | Twist bits (wood/metal/plastic), brad point, spade, **Forstner** (flat-bottom), hole saws; awls; hole and grommet punches (better than drills for textiles) | Use the right bit for the material (H 57) |
| **Spreading** | Brushes, putty knives, trowels | |
| **Heating** | Heat gun, strip heater, **dedicated** oven, torch | **Never use a plastics oven or dye pot for food again** (H 57) |
| **Clamping** | Bar, pipe, C, spring, **handscrew**, quick, strap clamps; rope and garrote; **cauls** | Also bench vises: woodworker's and engineer's (H 48–51) |
| **Digital fabrication** | CNC router, laser cutter, 3D printer | See below |

**Basic power-tool starter set** (M 63–66):
- **Essentials**: cordless drill/driver, jigsaw, hand sander, shop vac, hot glue gun, **staple gun** ("don't prop without one"), soldering iron, hot knife (for foam), electric knife (upholstery foam), fans.
- **Often shared with the scene shop**: band saw, table saw, miter saw, panel saw, drill press, lathe, bench grinder, belt and disc sander, reciprocating saw ("great for strike"), router, pneumatic nailers and staplers (need a compressor), metal chop saw, welder.

**Tool care** (M 66–67; H 34):
- Unplug by the plug, not the cord.
- Clean off dust and resin. Sawdust holds moisture and causes rust, and dust on motors causes overheating.
- Coil cords and lubricate as required.
- Replace and sharpen blades. Service machines yearly, including alignment.
- **Tag broken tools** and report them. Don't repair tools you don't own.

### Sewing equipment (M 67–69)
- Steamer
- Household and industrial irons
- Heavy-duty or industrial sewing machine
- Serger
- Washer/dryer (one machine can be a dedicated dye vat)
- Hand notions: tailor's rulers, cloth tape, needles, marking tools, pinking shears, seam ripper, tracing wheel, upholstery needles, elastic, snaps, Velcro, thread

See [ch. 18](18-fabric-upholstery-and-leather.md).

### Digital fabrication (H 10, 58)
CNC routers, laser cutters, plasma tables and 3D printers turn CAD files into parts. Hart treats them like buying a nut and bolt: a part you integrate, not a substitute for prop-making judgment. **"Knowing how to operate a 3D printer doesn't mean you know how to make a prop,"** any more than running a table saw makes you a furniture maker. Many theatre shops borrow time on the scene shop's machines, use makerspaces, or send files to online services.

**[Check current]** Hart (2013) describes hobby 3D printers as mostly ABS extrusion with laser sintering at the high end. Since then, desktop FDM printing in PLA and PETG and resin (SLA/MSLA) printing have become cheap and common, and many prop shops now own printers. Check current equipment and resin safety guidance.

## Shop supply stock (M 50–55; H 61)

A starting list:
- **Adhesives and tapes**: epoxy, construction adhesive, hot glue (low and high melt), spray adhesive, CA (super glue), rubber cement, white and yellow PVA; gaffer, masking, spike, packing, floral and electrical tape; Mortite. See [ch. 13](13-adhesives-fasteners-and-assembly.md).
- **Cleaning**: all-purpose cleaner, degreasers, adhesive removers, bleach, dish soap, rags, sponges, brooms. **Clean every stock prop before it goes into rehearsal**, especially anything that touches food or mouths (M 51).
- **Polishing**: furniture polish, metal polishes, steel wool. **Try polishing before refinishing.** Polish wood with the grain and dry metal right after polishing (M 52).
- **Materials**: lumber (1×4, 2×4, plywood, lauan, MDF, hardboard, dowels), joint compound and putty, foams (polystyrene, upholstery, floral, spray), casting and sculpting supplies, papers and boards, paints (latex, acrylic, scenic, spray), clear coats, dulling spray, dyes, flame retardant, Mod Podge.
- **Hardware**: screws, nails, bolts, washers, casters, glides, hooks, chain, wire, tubing, pipe, tie line, clothesline, zip cord, acrylic sheet.
- **Found-object parts bin** (H 61): beads, buttons, bottle caps, lamp parts, plumbing fittings, door hardware, costume jewelry, gears, springs, trim, dials and old electronics, toy parts, and more.

Store flammables in **lockable metal safety cabinets** as fire codes require (M 49–50; H 43).

## For the classroom

- **Shop audit.** Students audit your own shop for layout, ventilation, egress, PPE and labeling, and propose three improvements with costs.
- **Tool ID and safety checkout.** Run a tool identification exercise, then a supervised checkout on each machine, using the [safety chapter](10-health-and-safety.md) as the checklist.
- **Design-a-shop.** Given a floor plan and a budget, teams lay out a small prop shop and justify their zoning.
