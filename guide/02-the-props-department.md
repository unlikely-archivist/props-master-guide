# 2. The Props Department and the Prop Master

> **Sources:** M ch.2 (pp. 15–25), ch.4 (pp. 36–45); H ch.1 (pp. 6–10)
> **[Check current]** Union titles, contract terms and US tax and employment forms change over time and vary by employer. Treat the specifics here as examples.

## The job in one sentence, and why it's never one sentence

The textbook definition reads: *the prop master supplies all props needed for rehearsals and performances.* Mussman's point is that this badly undersells the role (M 15). On any given day a prop master may act as researcher, historian, shopper, carpenter, upholsterer, painter, chef, electrician, graphic artist, weapons handler, borrower, negotiator, organizer and diplomat. Above all, the prop master is a **visual storyteller** working alongside the director and designers (M 16).

## Where props sits in the organization

**Theatre.** Props is increasingly treated as a department in its own right, separate from the scene shop. The prop master reports to the **production manager** alongside the other department heads, rather than through the technical director as was once common (M 17, 31–32).

Typical structure:

- **Properties director**: in theatres with a permanent shop. Runs the shop and may hire separate prop masters per show (H 6).
- **Prop master** (also: properties master/manager/supervisor/director, props mistress, properties designer) (M 17). On Broadway the person may be billed as **props coordinator**, especially when not a union member, because "props master" is a union title there (H 6).
- **Assistant prop master**: able to step in for the prop master; supervises part of the staff (M 19).
- **Prop artisans**: may be generalists or specialists (H 7; M 19):
  - *props carpenters*: wood, metal, plastic, furniture
  - *props craftspeople*: sculpture, casting, upholstery, paper
  - *soft goods artisans*
  - *shopper/buyers*
- **Props design**: usually done by the **set designer**. Some set designers draft key props; many leave interpretation to the prop master, subject to their approval. Some productions hire a separate **props designer** (H 6–7).
- **Effects designer** for complex effects, and a **pyrotechnic designer** where flame or explosives are involved. Many jurisdictions require a license for pyro (H 7).

**Film** (H 7–8). The production designer or art director designs props; a film "set designer" is a drafter. The prop master supplies hand props and picture vehicles and works with the art director and the **set decorator**, who handles set props and dressing. Other film features:

- Builds often go to outside shops or a temporary location shop.
- **Union rules often bar the prop master from handling props on set.**
- On larger films, a **mill shop** in the set department builds moldings, doors and cabinetry.
- Real firearms modified for blanks require a **licensed armorer**, although the prop master remains ultimately responsible.

**Television** (H 8). The production designer supervises. An *inside* studio crew maintains and rigs props while an *outside* crew works with vendors and rental houses. Weekly episodes can leave three or four days from script to camera. Commercials and music videos tend to be more hands-on, especially non-union work.

**Other careers for prop makers** (H 8): effects, models, puppetry, animatronics, opera, dance, magic, display and exhibition work, trade shows, events, museums, theme parks, haunted attractions, private commissions.

## A sample job description (synthesized from M 18)

The prop master:

1. Supplies all rehearsal and performance props through planning and procurement.
2. Reads each script and produces a **prop list** and **cost breakdown**.
3. Works with the director and designers to acquire or build each prop to the design.
4. Coordinates with stage management and fills prop requests from rehearsal.
5. Meets production deadlines.
6. Loads props into the venue and dresses the set with the designer.
7. Coordinates schedules and needs with other department heads.
8. Attends production meetings, staff meetings, tech, dress, previews and notes sessions.
9. Is responsible for prop safety and maintenance from acquisition through strike.
10. Organizes the office, shop, storage, stock, tools and supplies.
11. Tracks expenditures, stays within budget and submits receipts and timesheets on time.
12. Hires, manages and supervises prop staff.
13. Manages rentals of the theatre's own prop stock to others.

## Skills and attributes

**Technical skills** used regularly (M 20–21):

- drafting and reading drawings
- woodworking
- sewing
- painting
- crafts
- metalwork (welding and soldering)
- computer graphics and spreadsheets
- research
- shopping

No one arrives knowing all of these. Mussman's advice is to learn constantly and to find help for what you can't yet do.

**Personal attributes** (M 22–23):

- accepts responsibility
- balances management with creative work
- has a business mind
- uses common sense
- solves problems creatively
- pays attention to detail
- is flexible, with a good attitude
- has a good memory
- knows theatre
- networks
- is open and organized
- teaches themselves
- is tough

**Managing staff** (M 19–20):

- Train continuously.
- Delegate with complete information up front, so things are built right the first time.
- Trust people who are eager even if they are not yet expert.
- **Praise publicly and critique privately.**
- Pass on compliments your staff may not hear directly.

## Professional etiquette (M 23–25)

- Return calls promptly and during business hours (not before 9 AM). Silence your phone in rehearsals.
- Tell people where you are when you leave the shop, which will be often.
- Be on time. Wear sturdy shoes and work clothes and tie long hair back.
- **Send thank-you notes** to lenders and helpers.
- Listen without interrupting. Own your mistakes without blaming anyone.
- **Keep your cool.** Supply what is asked even if you think it's silly, and raise disagreements calmly. If guest artists bring in props without consulting you, thank them but ask firmly to be consulted first.
- On stress: "It's a play, not brain surgery."
- **Backstage:** no talking aloud, eating or running. Don't peek at the audience. **Don't touch props that aren't yours.**

## Know your workplace (M ch.4)

Before or soon after taking a job, find out:

- **Type of institution**: commercial, regional professional, community, college or high school. Processes are similar, but the chain of command differs (M 36–37).
- **Venue and stage type.** Intimacy sets the level of detail. A **proscenium** has the audience on one side, a **thrust** on three, and an **arena** on four; on an arena stage, props must read from every angle (M 37).
- **Workspace**: an on-site shop may face noise restrictions during performances, and an off-site shop costs travel time (M 37).
- **Practicalities**: access, keys, alarms, late-night safety, parking, a company vehicle for hauling, and nearby hardware, fabric, craft and grocery stores (M 38).
- **Money**: how purchases are paid (petty cash or company card), how receipts and reimbursements work, and what the budget lines are (materials, labor, travel, shop supplies). Also ask what happens to rental income and whether mileage is reimbursed (M 39). See [ch. 5](05-budget-time-and-feasibility.md).
- **Communication**: work email, phone, business cards and the master calendar. Enter every date early: design meetings, first rehearsal, tech, dress, previews, opening and fundraisers (M 40).

## Protecting yourself (M 41–45)

- **Volunteers** should set their own clear expectations for hours and deadlines and recruit helpers.
- **Paid positions** usually come with a *letter of agreement* rather than a full contract. Make sure it states pay rate, pay period and method, term, benefits and job duties, ideally with the job description attached. Ask for the employee handbook. Positions may be freelance, seasonal (about nine months) or year-round.
- **Hours**: ask what is expected. Theatre is never a 40-hour week, and tech is long. Compare your list against your time **early**, and tell your supervisor if the two don't fit.
- **Self-care**: sleep, eat and know your daily limits. Read the handbook and use your vacation.

## For the classroom

- **Org-chart exercise.** Students draw the organizational chart of your own university's production program and locate props in it. Then compare it with a regional theatre and with a film crew.
- **Interview assignment.** Students interview a working prop master or artisan about a typical day and compare it with the job description above.
- **Discussion.** What changes when props reports to the production manager instead of the technical director? What problems does that fix, and what new ones might it create?
