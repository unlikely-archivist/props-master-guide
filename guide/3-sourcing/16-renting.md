# 16. Renting

> **Sources:** M ch.4 (pp. 39–40), ch.6 (pp. 90–91), ch.8 (pp. 113–115). Sections marked **[General knowledge]** aren't from the guide's sources.
> **[Check current]** Rental rates, insurance requirements and contract terms vary by rental house and change. Have your institution approve any contract.

Renting gets you items you couldn't afford to buy or build, especially period furniture and set dressing. It also brings a contract, a deadline and full liability for damage.

## Rent (M 113–115)

Prop rental houses operate in most major cities, and some theatres and schools rent out their stock. Before renting, check (M 113):

- Have I read and understood the rental terms?
- **Could I afford to pay full value if it is damaged?**
- Is renting cheaper than buying a similar item?
- Do I have budget for it?
- Am I allowed to alter it?
- Are the start and end dates correct?
- Have the director and designer approved it?
- Will I be refunded for an early return?

Renting can end a long search, but it isn't always cheaper than buying (M 114).

### Borrowing and renting etiquette (M 114–115)
- **If it can't come back in the same condition, buy or build instead.** Don't borrow anything you could never replace in good faith.
- **If something breaks, tell the owner.** Take responsibility and offer a repair or professional restoration.
- Follow the agreement and return items on time. Your reputation precedes you.
- **Give stage management a list of every borrowed or rented item** ("Heads up, that's rented"). It helps prevent damage and heads off a director's urge to alter the item.

### Rental and loan agreements (M 39–40, 90–91)
Use one **in both directions**, whether you are borrowing or lending, and write one if the lender has none. Typical contents:
- borrow (free) or rent (fee)
- renter's identity and contact details
- itemized list with description, **condition at checkout** and value
- start and return dates
- deposit and fees
- rules on alterations (written permission only)
- late-return penalties
- liability for loss or damage (usually full value plus forfeiture of the deposit)
- payment terms
- signatures and dates from both parties, each keeping a copy

A checklist version is in [Appendix B](../../appendices/B-paperwork-templates.md#5-rentalloan-agreement-checklist). Have your institution approve the actual legal form.

## Working with rental houses [General knowledge]

- **Rental rates** are usually a percentage of the item's value per week, often with a minimum. Long runs can make buying cheaper; do the sum (M 114). On a long run or tour, Wilson notes, hire charges can exceed the cost of making the prop (W 8).
- **Hire firms won't let you wreck their stock.** They keep furniture in good condition and won't appreciate you ripping out the stuffing to make it look battered, so a "once grand, now ruined" piece may have to be bought or built (W 8).
- **Ask about theatre rates.** Rental houses that mainly serve film and TV sometimes offer lower rates for theatres and schools, or for long runs.
- **Insurance.** Many rental houses require a certificate of insurance naming them as additionally insured, or a damage waiver fee. Check with your institution's business office early, because certificates can take days.
- **Pull day.** Go with the designer if possible, photos and measurements in hand. Tag what you want, and check each item over before it's written up.
- **Document condition at pickup.** Photograph every item from all sides, including existing damage, and make sure it's noted on the paperwork ([Appendix B](../../appendices/B-paperwork-templates.md#5-rentalloan-agreement-checklist)).
- **Transport** well: blankets, straps, and enough people. Damage in the truck is still your damage.
- **During the run**, protect rented items: felt pads, glass covers, no tape on finishes, and a clear "RENTED" label for the crew (M 114–115).
- **Return** on time, clean, and with all parts. Photograph the items again at return.

## Renting out your own stock

Many theatres rent their stock to others, and the prop master usually manages it (M 18). Use the same agreement in reverse (M 39–40), set rates and deposits in advance, photograph condition out and back, and know who pays for damage. Rental income may or may not come back to the props budget, so ask (M 39).

## For the classroom

- **Rent or buy?** Give students the weekly rate and value of three items and a run length. They calculate when buying becomes cheaper and what else should affect the decision.
- **Condition report.** Students photograph and write a condition report for a stock piece as if renting it out, then swap and check each other's reports for gaps.
- **Rental house visit.** If possible, visit a rental house or a theatre's stock and interview the manager about what makes a good renter.
