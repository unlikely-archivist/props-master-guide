# 18. Pulling, Altering and Kitbashing

> **Sources:** M ch.8 (pp. 118–119); H ch.1 (p. 6), ch.5 (pp. 60–62). Sections marked **[General knowledge]** aren't from the guide's sources.

The cheapest prop is one you already own or can find. The next cheapest is often something close that you change. This chapter covers pulling from stock and found sources, altering existing objects, and building from found parts ("kitbashing").

## Find and pull (M 118–119)

Good places to look:
- your own prop storage
- friends' and family's closets
- forgotten corners of the theatre building
- friendly theatres and schools
- board members' basements
- dumpsters (*"one man's trash..."*). Mussman notes this is risky and sometimes illegal.

Pulling from stock is efficient, but **don't permanently alter valuable stock pieces** you use regularly (M 119).

**Use the buddy system.** Don't go alone into sketchy places or lift heavy objects alone. "Props aren't worth that much" (M 119).

## Assemblage and found objects (H 60–62)

Props are rarely one material. Treat everything around you (hardware-store parts, thrift-store items, toy parts, packaging) as potential material. Model makers call this **kitbashing**.

Hart's mini boombox is a good example. It's an MDF block with:
- wooden knobs and discs for dials and speakers
- window screen over the speakers
- a bent-wire handle
- a stripped-wire antenna with a bead on the end
- styrene panels
- model-railroad brick sheet cut into buttons

Collect parts as you find them, and organize them so you can find them again (H 61).

**Surface detail** (H 62):
- **Diapering** is the traditional term for decorating a plain surface with a repeating pattern.
- **Greebles / nurnies / wiggets** are small applied details that make a surface read as complex. The term came from model makers on the original *Star Wars*.
- Techniques:
  - scribe panel lines
  - add rows of rivet heads
  - put cut-out overlays on plain hinges to fake wrought iron
  - apply thin sheet for raised panels
- **Materials:** styrene sheet is the modern standard and holds a crisp edge. Industrial felt stiffened with shellac or resin is the traditional option for curved surfaces. Textured materials (grilles, fireplace screens, embossed papers) can be glued on and coated.

## Altering an existing object

Hart warns that **adapting** a store-bought object can cost more than building one: you have to match its existing finish, and modern furniture is often honeycomb paper under a thin skin, with nothing to fasten to (H 6). Check before you commit.

**Rules for altering** [General knowledge]
- **Never alter rented or borrowed items** without written permission (M 113; [ch. 16](16-renting.md)).
- **Make alterations reversible** where you can on stock you'll reuse: slipcovers instead of reupholstery, removable paint or tape instead of permanent finishes (M 119).
- **Test finishes on a hidden area** first.
- **Photograph before** you start, so you can return the piece to stock (or explain what changed).

## For the classroom

- **Period swap.** Students take a modern thrift-store object (lamp, phone, clock, bottle) and alter it to read as a specific earlier decade, documenting before and after.
- **Kitbash challenge.** Students build a "sci-fi device" or "steampunk tool" from thrift-store and hardware-store found objects within a time limit (H 60–62).
- **Stock dig.** Give students a prop list and an hour in your storage. They pull what they can, and list what each pulled item would need to suit the show.
