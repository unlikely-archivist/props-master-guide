# 21. Molding and Casting

> **Sources:** H ch.14 (pp. 255–290); M ch.5 (p. 54)
> **[Check current]** Product lines (Smooth-On, Por-a-Kast, Vinamold, etc.), formulations, mix ratios, and safety data change — **always follow the current manufacturer's technical data sheet and SDS.** Resin chemistry is the most hazardous work in most prop shops; do not attempt urethane or polyester casting without the ventilation and PPE described in [ch. 10](10-health-and-safety.md).

## Why mold and cast? (H 256)

- **Multiples** — when building each by hand costs more, or for repeated details (rosettes); a **mold library** of ornaments lets a shop decorate huge pieces quickly (H 257).
- **A different final material** than the sculpted model.
- **Hollow skins** — masks, armor, lighter parts.
- **Replicas** of things that can't be used — body parts (an actor's real head is messy and often against Equity rules), valuables, details fixed to a building.
- **Breakables** — many cheap copies.

**But ask whether it's necessary** — molding materials are expensive and time-consuming (H 256).

## Decide in order (H 256–257)

1. What is the **cast** made of (from the prop's needs and your means)?
2. That limits the **mold material**, further constrained by what the **master** is made of and its **shape** (undercuts → flexible or multi-piece; through-holes → piece mold).
3. That determines **releases and sealers**.
4. Then budget, time, and what your shop is equipped (and ventilated) to handle.

Manufacturers' tech support can help if you describe the job well.

**Learn on cheap, forgiving materials first** — plaster, alginate, latex, papier-mâché — before silicones and resins, whose chemistry punishes mistakes (H 257).

## Vocabulary (H 258–259, 263, 270)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Master** | The original being copied |
| **Mold / negative** | The material surrounding the master, holding a cavity of its shape |
| **Cast / positive** | The copy (most materials shrink slightly — usually imperceptible) |
| **Press mold** | Clay-like material pushed in by hand |
| **Master mold** | A mold used to make more working molds (molds wear out) |
| **Release agent** | Prevents sticking (cast to mold, mold piece to mold piece) |
| **Sealer** | Closes porous master surfaces before releasing |
| **Mother mold / jacket** | Rigid shell supporting a flexible mold |
| **Waste mold** | Destroyed to free the cast — fast, seamless, one use |
| **Piece mold** | Multiple parts, reusable; needed for undercuts |
| **Undercut** | A recess that locks a rigid cast in a rigid mold |
| **Parting line / flashing** | Where mold pieces meet / the thin fin of material that leaks there |
| **Registration keys** | Bumps and sockets that align mold pieces |
| **Sprue / gate / vent** | Pour channel / passage to the cavity / air escape |
| **Beauty (impression) coat** | First thin coat that captures detail without bubbles |

**Golden rule:** cast **rigid materials in flexible molds** and **flexible materials in rigid molds** — rigid-in-rigid with any undercut traps the cast (H 267). Demold resins at the **"green" stage** (holds shape, still slightly flexible) and let them finish curing outside the mold.

## Mold types (H 260–265)

- **Box (bucket) mold:** walls around the master; pour over. Never pour under the master (hydraulic lift, trapped air).
- **Two-piece box mold:** embed the master halfway in an oil-clay bed at the seam line, carve **registration keys**, pour half one; remove clay, flip, apply release (*silicone sticks to silicone!*), pour half two.
- **Brush-up mold:** thin beauty coat, then thicker coats (colored layers help judge thickness); one piece (**glove mold**) or divided with **fences** (clay walls perpendicular to the surface with carved keys, metal shims, or stiff card for non-water materials). Support with a **mother mold** — uses far less costly rubber than a box mold.
- **Matrix mold:** cover the master in clay (~½" for silicone), build the shell over it, remove the clay, pour rubber into the gap — combines economy and convenience.
- **Multi-piece molds:** plan seams where flashing is easy to trim and away from focal surfaces; often easier to **cut the master into parts**, mold each simply, and assemble the casts.

## Release agents (H 265–267)

| Release | Good with |
|---|---|
| **Green soap / castile / oil soap** | Plaster-on-plaster, gypsum, papier-mâché |
| **Petroleum jelly** (thin it, or heat-level brush marks) | PU rubber, epoxy, polyester, silicone, plaster, papier-mâché |
| **Waxes** (paste, aerosol) | Epoxy, PU foam, polyester; pair with PVA |
| **PVA (polyvinyl *alcohol*) film release** — *not* white glue | Epoxy, polyester, PU |
| **Lecithin** (some food-safe sprays) | Epoxy, urethanes, polyester; paintable afterward |
| **Silicone spray** | Effective but transfers to casts (paint won't stick) and **contaminates the whole shop** |
| **Cornstarch/baking soda dust, soapy water** | Breaks surface tension for fine detail |
| **Plastic film / foil** | Crude release where detail doesn't matter |

**Resin in a plaster mold** (H 266, 279): seal the dry plaster (shellac/lacquer/paint) → wax → dust with cornstarch → PVA.
**No release for absorption casting** in plaster — it clogs the pores (H 268–269).
Silicone molds rarely need release, but light wax extends their life (H 268, 283–284).
Release builds up and transfers to casts — clean casts (dish soap; acetone for silicone release) before painting; skip release every few pulls (H 266–267).

**Sealers** for porous masters (plaster, stone, wood): shellac, paste wax, thinned petroleum jelly, paint. **No shellac under silicone** (inhibits cure) (H 267).

> **Conflict with FF.** *The Fake Food Cookbook* seals real-food masters (wings, shrimp, corn, rib bones) with Krylon Crystal Clear acrylic *or* shellac, then Ease Release 200, before pouring OOMOO (FF 38–39). Hart says the opposite: no shellac under silicone (H 267). FF's full food-casting workflow and Smooth-On numbers are in [ch. 28](28-fake-food.md#molding-and-casting-with-smooth-on-ff-1718-3741-5458-8589-99105-218222).

**Two-part mixing:** measure each part in its own cup, combine in a third; tare containers when weighing; mix exact ratios (H 267, 284).

## Casting methods (H 269–271)

- **Gravity casting:** pour and let it flow.
- **Slush casting:** swirl to coat the walls → hollow; brush material up the walls until it stops running; **roto-casting** machines spin capped molds for even walls.
- **Absorption (slip) casting:** a **dry plaster** mold absorbs water so a skin forms on the walls; pour out the excess at the desired thickness. For slip clay, casting latex, neoprene. (Dry plaster is room-temperature and light; damp plaster is cold and heavy.)
- **Fighting bubbles:** sprue to the highest point; vents exiting as high as the sprue; keep the stirrer submerged; tap the cup, skim foam; **pour a thin, high stream to one spot** and let it flow; vibrate the filled mold; tilt to free trapped air; **vacuum-degas** viscous silicones/urethanes.

## Materials

### Rigid
**Plaster (gypsum)** (H 271–274) — economical, intuitive, low-toxicity; uniquely absorbent.
- Grades vary enormously — USG's range spans ~2,000 psi molding plasters at ~70:100 water:plaster to ~10,000 psi gypsum cements (Hydro-Stone) at ~32:100. Soft plasters for waste molds; mid-strength for working molds; very hard for castings and vacuum-form bucks.
- **Mixing:** fresh, dry plaster; dust protection; water 70–100°F; flexible plastic bucket; **sift plaster into water, let it slake 1–2 min**, mix 2–5 min without whipping in air; tap and skim. The "islands" method is quick but gives inconsistent properties.
- Smear a beauty coat by hand, then pour. Reinforce big molds with wire or rebar.
- **Never pour plaster down a drain.** It's **exothermic** — large masses can burn. Demold when cool (~15–30 min); fully dry in 1–7 days before painting, gluing, or absorption casting.
- **Plaster bandages** make quick, strong mother molds (H 274).

**Polyurethane resins** (H 278–280) — fast, fine detail, enormous range (rigid plastic, rubbers, A-B foams).
- **Most toxic common casting material:** isocyanates off-gas for 24+ hours (years if mis-mixed); **no odor**, so cartridge respirators are unreliable — use a spray booth, chemical hood, glove box, or **supplied air**; cure pieces in a separate area; disposable cups and sticks, sealed and removed. Isocyanate asthma and **deaths** have occurred; sensitization can follow one exposure or decades.
- Moisture-sensitive (damp molds or humid days → tacky, bubbly casts); short shelf life (~5–6 months after opening); sticks to everything but polyethylene.
- Add colorants and fillers to the resin side *before* catalyst. Best cast in silicone molds.
- **Shell-and-foam trick:** rotocast a tough PU shell, fill with rigid foam → light, strong, detailed.
- Non-flame-retardant PU is highly flammable with toxic smoke.

**Polyester resin** (H 280–282) — cheapest resin; good for clear casts; shrinks more than epoxy.
- **Don't cast with laminating resin** (formulated hot — a mass can overheat, off-gas, or **catch fire**) and don't laminate with casting resin (won't cure thin).
- **MEKP catalyst** is explosive, corrosive, skin-absorbed. Too much catalyst = heat, cracks, fire risk; thick casts may need less.
- Styrene vapors (suspected carcinogen) — local exhaust, respirator, covered curing.
- **Auto-body filler (Bondo)** is polyester plus fillers; "Rondo" mixes it with resin. **Water-extended polyester** is cheap and bone-white but warps over years.

**Epoxy resin** (H 282) — mostly adhesive and laminating; pricier casting resin. Toxicity varies by product; >50% of exposed workers develop allergies. **Epoxy putty** models by hand, cures in ~1.5 hours, and machines when hard.

### Flexible
**Silicone rubber** (H 283–284) — the props favorite for durable flexible molds.
- Tin- or platinum-cure; brush, pour, or spray. Best release of any rubber (most casts need none). Heat-resistant (~400°F+), so it can cast **low-melt metals** (pewter, tin).
- Very flexible and tear-resistant → handles severe undercuts; usually needs a mother mold.
- **Cure inhibitors:** sulfur clay, latex (even latex gloves), shellac. Exact mix ratios (1:1 to 10:1); parts are colored to show complete mixing.
- Also a superb **casting** material for skin and prosthetics.
- **Budget hack — silicone-caulk molds** (H 284–285): clear 100% silicone kneaded with glycerin and a little acrylic paint, handled with soapy gloves, pressed on in layers; 24–48 hr cure; inconsistent — temporary use only.

**Alginate** (H 275, 286–289) — seaweed-based, nontoxic, skin-safe; the dental-impression material. Superb detail, but keeps shrinking — usable for only ~2 hours. Can't take moisture-sensitive resins. **The go-to for life-casting hands, feet, faces.**

**Latex** (H 276–277) — cheap, one-part; brushed glove molds, slush/absorption casting. Up to ~20 coats, each dried (milky color gone); shrinks 10–20%; ammonia fumes; **allergies** among artisans, actors, and crew. Reinforce casts with cheesecloth (bias for stretch, grain for strength); vulcanize per product; spoils in ~1 year (cottage-cheese smell).

**Others:** **gelatin** (remeltable molds; **edible** casts in a food-safe space; melts under stage lights — great for **breakaway prosthetics** like Gloucester's eyes) (H 274–275); **agar/moulage** (body molds, reusable) (H 275); **neoprene** (absorption-cast, harder than latex, ~3/16" walls for masks; toxic while curing) (H 285); **foam latex** (makeup effects; baked; degrades over years) and two-part **silicone foam** (H 285–286); **hot-pour vinyl** (remeltable; largely replaced by silicone) (H 277).

**In-a-pinch casting:** Plastic Wood, patching compounds (slow and crack-prone in mass), tinted **hot glue** in a *wet* plaster mold (H 285).

**Shore hardness** (H 286): Shore OO (very soft), A (flexible), D (rigid); higher = harder; scales don't convert; hardness ≠ strength.

## Molding the human body (H 286–290)

- **Alginate** for hands, feet, faces, ears; **Flexwax** (skin-safe low-melt wax, dipped in layers to ~¼", reusable); **skin-safe silicone** for larger areas (**patch-test on the wrist first**). No release needed.
- **Hand in alginate:** container with ½" clearance; warm-water mix; actor seated, hand posed ~10 minutes; wiggle to break suction and withdraw. **Cast immediately — on site if necessary** — usually a quick plaster positive, which is then molded in silicone for permanent casts. Don't make the actor come back.
- Whole bodies: cast in parts; fill in with mannequin parts; cast only what shows.
- **Never encase body parts in plaster.** Plaster heats as it sets and can trap limbs: in 2007 a schoolgirl in England put both hands into a bucket of plaster; as it set it trapped them and exceeded 140°F (60°C) and she ultimately lost eight fingers (H 290). Apply plaster to skin only in thin layers with cooling time (bandages after the first layer), with petroleum jelly as release, hair protected — and only under experienced supervision.

## Mold care (H 290)
Store molds in sealed tubs, unstacked; moisture, dust, sunlight, and air degrade them.

## For the classroom

- **Starter sequence (cheap → advanced):** (1) press objects into clay and pour plaster (Hart's first casting exercise, H 9); (2) two-piece plaster mold of a simple object with keys; (3) alginate hand cast → plaster positive; (4) brush-up silicone glove mold with plaster mother mold (only after silicone safety training); (5) water-based or low-toxicity casting resin.
- **Release matrix:** Students build a chart of mold/cast/release combinations from manufacturers' data sheets for materials your shop actually stocks.
- **Case discussion:** The 2007 plaster injury — what would a safe life-casting protocol look like in your program?
