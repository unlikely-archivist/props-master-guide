# 43. Firearms

> **Sources:** M ch.10 (pp. 147–148); H ch.1 (p. 7); J (firearms entries; see the [timeline](../../appendices/E-period-props-timeline.md#11-arms-and-armor)). Most of this chapter is **[General knowledge]**: it summarises widely published industry safety practice, not the guide's sources.
> **[Check current]** This is the most regulated and fastest-changing area in the guide. Firearms law, union and industry safety guidance (for example Actors' Equity rules in theatre, and the industry-wide safety bulletins used in US film and television), and institutional policies differ by place and are revised. **Follow your institution's policy, current union guidance and local law, and use a qualified armorer wherever required.** This chapter is an overview for planning conversations, not instructions for handling, modifying or firing any weapon.

## Why firearms are different

Every firearm prop, even a fake, carries risks no other prop does:
- **Real and blank-firing guns can kill.** Blanks contain gunpowder and wadding. At close range the blast and debris can cause serious and fatal injuries (M 147–148). Actor Jon-Erik Hexum died in 1984 after firing a blank-loaded gun against his own head. Brandon Lee was killed on *The Crow* (1993) and cinematographer Halyna Hutchins on *Rust* (2021) when guns on set fired real projectiles. **[General knowledge]**
- **Realistic replicas can trigger police responses** if seen in public.
- **Laws** on owning, transporting and using real, deactivated, blank-firing and replica guns vary greatly and are often strict.

For these reasons many theatres and schools now **ban real firearms entirely** ([ch. 42](42-weapons-safety-and-policy.md)).

## Types of stage firearm [General knowledge]

From least to most hazardous:

| Type | What it is | Typical use | Key cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Non-gun ("rubber gun")** | Solid rubber, resin, foam or 3D-printed replica; no moving parts | Holstered, carried, pointed, dropped, stunts | Safest option. Still treat as a weapon in public. Can be cast from a mould ([ch. 38](../5-materials/38-molding-and-casting.md)) |
| **Non-firing replica** | Metal or plastic replica with working mechanism that can't fire anything | Close-up handling, loading and cocking actions | Looks fully real. Store and transport like a real gun. Some places regulate realistic imitations |
| **Toy or airsoft gun** | Fires plastic pellets or nothing | Rarely suitable; often looks toy-like | In the US, toy and imitation guns sold commercially must carry an orange tip; removing it may be illegal **[check current]**. Pellet guns are not safe stage weapons |
| **Starter pistol** | Blocked barrel; fires blanks only | **Offstage** sound effects (M 147) | Still dangerous at close range and to hearing. Use eye and ear protection (M 147–148) |
| **Blank-firing replica** | Purpose-built to fire blanks only; can't chamber real ammunition | Onstage firing where policy allows | Blanks are dangerous. Requires a qualified weapons handler and strict procedures |
| **Real firearm adapted for blanks** | A real gun | Mainly film (H 7) | Requires a **licensed armorer** and legal licensing. The prop master remains ultimately responsible (H 7). Banned by many theatres and schools |

## Alternatives to firing a gun onstage [General knowledge]

Most stage gunshots don't need a gun to fire:
- **Recorded sound** from the sound designer, timed to the actor's action.
- **An offstage starter pistol** or other live sound effect, handled by one trained person (M 147; [ch. 48](../7-effects/48-flame-smoke-and-sound.md)).
- **Electronic or LED muzzle-flash** props that light without any blank.
- **Staging**: the shot happens offstage, in a blackout, or with the gun turned away from the audience.
- **Film**: muzzle flashes and recoil can be added digitally in post-production, and many productions now do this as standard.

## Handling rules from the sources (M 147–148)

- **One designated person** cleans, loads, hands off, retrieves and locks up each weapon. Load just before use and unload immediately after.
- **Never point any gun at anyone**, "loaded" or not, including the audience. Fire upward and away from the body for sound effects.
- **Keep fingers off triggers** until the moment of use.
- **Wear eye and ear protection** near any firing.
- **Never use a prop gun for pranks.**
- **Have guns inspected by licensed professionals**, and learn how they work (M 148).
- **Lock up all weapons, real or not, every night** (M 148–149).

## Standard safety practice [General knowledge]

These points appear in most current theatre and film firearm guidance. Your armorer and your institution's policy have the final word.
- **No live ammunition** anywhere near the production, ever: not in the venue, not in the prop room, not in anyone's bag.
- **A qualified armorer or weapons master** controls every firearm. No one else loads, unloads or modifies them.
- **Show the weapon** to the actor (and to stage management or the first assistant director) each time it's handed over, so they can see its condition.
- **Treat every firearm as loaded** until the armorer has shown otherwise.
- **Rehearse with non-firing versions**, and introduce any firing version only after a safety briefing.
- **Plan angles and distances** so a firearm is never aimed at a person. Stage the action so it reads as aimed, cheating the angle.
- **Everyone has the right to refuse** to work with a firearm they feel is unsafe, and to stop the work.
- **Secure storage and a log** for every weapon and every blank (see [ch. 42](42-weapons-safety-and-policy.md#a-weapons-policy-for-every-show-general-knowledge)).

## Period accuracy

For dating firing mechanisms (matchlock, wheel lock, flintlock, percussion cap, cartridge) and the names of period guns, see the [timeline](../../appendices/E-period-props-timeline.md#11-arms-and-armor) and [ch. 7](../2-admin/07-research-and-period-accuracy.md#arms-armor-and-authority-vocabulary-first) (J). Accuracy never overrides safety: a non-firing replica of the right period is almost always better than a firing gun of the wrong one.

## Making non-firing guns [General knowledge]

When a solid prop gun is enough, it can be:
- **Cast** in resin or rubber from a mould of a non-firing replica ([ch. 38](../5-materials/38-molding-and-casting.md)). Rubber versions are safer for stunts and falls.
- **Carved** in wood or rigid foam and hard-coated ([ch. 29](../5-materials/29-wood.md), [ch. 37](../5-materials/37-sculpting.md)).
- **3D printed** and finished ([ch. 40](../5-materials/40-3d-printing.md)).
- **Built in EVA foam** for fantasy or sci-fi guns ([ch. 33](../5-materials/33-eva-foam.md)).

Don't build anything that can fire, and keep solid prop guns under the same lock-up and public-display rules as replicas.

## For the classroom

- **Gunshot without a gun.** Students plan three different ways to stage the same onstage shooting without firing any weapon, and present the pros and cons of each.
- **Policy comparison.** Students research the current firearm guidance from one union or industry body and your institution's own policy, and summarise the differences.
- **Case study.** Students research one historic firearm accident on a set or stage, identify which safety rules failed, and present how current practice would have prevented it.
- **Prop gun build.** Students cast or 3D-print a solid, non-functional period pistol, paint it to read as metal and wood, and write the storage and handling rules for it.
