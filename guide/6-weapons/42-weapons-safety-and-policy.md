# 42. Weapons Safety and Policy

> **Sources:** M ch.10 (pp. 147–149); H ch.1 (pp. 4, 7), ch.8 (pp. 158–160); J (arms and armour entries); KF (p. 5). Sections marked **[General knowledge]** aren't from the guide's sources and must be checked against your institution's policy, union rules and local law.
> **[Check current]** Weapons law, union safety rules and institutional policies vary by place and changed sharply after the 2021 *Rust* shooting. **Your institution's policy and local law govern.** Nothing in this part authorises the use of any weapon.

Weapons are the most dangerous props a department handles. A prop that looks like a weapon can still injure, frighten the public, or bring the police. This chapter sets out the safety principles and the policy every show needs. Specific weapon types follow: [firearms](43-firearms.md), [swords, daggers and blades](44-swords-and-daggers.md), and [other weapons and armour](45-other-weapons.md).

## What the sources say (M 147–149; H 7)

> **Important update.** Mussman's 2008 advice describes firing blanks from real guns onstage under strict procedures. **Practice has tightened considerably since then**, particularly after the fatal 2021 shooting on the film set of *Rust*. Many theatres and schools now **prohibit real firearms entirely**, using non-firing replicas, blank-firing replicas handled by a qualified person, or recorded or offstage sound effects instead. **Follow your institution's weapons policy, current union guidance and local law. Use a qualified armorer or weapons master where required.** The procedures below are historical context and a checklist of hazards, not authorization.

What the sources establish:
- **Film uses real firearms modified to fire blanks**, which still require licensing. A **licensed armorer** handles transport, storage and the practical details, while the prop master remains ultimately responsible (H 7).
- **Blanks are dangerous.** Wadding can penetrate flesh at close range. Never point any gun, "loaded" or not, at anyone, including the audience. Fire upward and away from the body for sound effects. Wear eye and ear protection (M 147–148).
- **One designated person** cleans, loads, hands off, retrieves and locks up each weapon. Load just before use and unload immediately after. Keep fingers off triggers. Never use a prop gun for pranks (M 147–148).
- **Starter pistols** have blocked barrels and fire only blanks; they're commonly used for offstage shots (M 147).
- Have any guns in stock inspected by licensed professionals, and learn how they work (M 148).
- **Dull every blade and sharp edge** on onstage knives, swords and axes (bench grinder, stone, metal sandpaper) (M 148). Stage-combat blades are made of **spring steel** (e.g., 5160) so they flex instead of snapping (H 158). Film combat blades in 7075 aluminum read as steel on camera, but dent too quickly for a stage run (H 160). An axe that really splits a melon nightly needs a genuine tool-steel edge (H 159), which is exactly the kind of prop that needs a careful safety plan.
- **Lock up all weapons, real or not**, every night (M 148–149; see also [ch. 12](../2-admin/12-rehearsals-tech-and-performance.md#performances-m-137138)).
- Coordinate weapons, breakaways and fights with the **fight director**, or the **stunt department** in film (H 4).
- For period names and dates of armor, polearms, swords and firing mechanisms (matchlock, wheel lock, flintlock), see [Appendix E §11](../../appendices/E-period-props-timeline.md#11-arms-and-armor) and [ch. 7](../2-admin/07-research-and-period-accuracy.md#arms-armor-and-authority-vocabulary-first). Accuracy never overrides the safety rules above.
- **Lightweight foam weapons.** KF builds large swords, shields, claws and spears from EVA foam over rod or PVC cores, and summarises convention weapon checks (no functional weapons or projectiles, some events ban hard materials or limit size) (KF 5). **[Added best practice]** A foam skin doesn't make a rigid core safe for contact; see [ch. 33](../5-materials/33-eva-foam.md#adapting-kfs-methods-for-a-production-shop).

## A weapons policy for every show [General knowledge]

If your institution has a weapons policy, follow it. If it doesn't, push for one. A workable policy covers:

1. **One responsible person** for every weapon: the prop master, a weapons master, or a qualified armorer. That person controls check-out, hand-off, retrieval and lock-up (M 147–148).
2. **A written weapons list** for the show: every item, what it's made of, what it does, who uses it, and where it's stored.
3. **A log.** Every weapon is signed out and back in, every rehearsal and performance.
4. **Locked storage**, every night, with access limited to named people (M 148–149).
5. **Inspection before every use**: loose hilts, cracks, burrs, sharp edges, damaged tips, worn grips. Remove anything damaged from use immediately.
6. **Safety briefings.** Before weapons enter rehearsal, everyone who will be near them (actors, crew, stage management) gets a briefing on how they work and the rules for handling them.
7. **The fight director decides** how any weapon used in a fight is handled, and signs off on the weapons themselves (H 4). Many productions run a **fight call** (a slow run-through of all fights) before every performance.
8. **No horseplay, ever.** No pointing, no pranks, no "it's only a prop" (M 147–148).
9. **Public safety.** Realistic replica weapons carried in public can trigger emergency calls. Transport them in closed cases, never carry them visibly outside the venue, and consider telling local police when realistic weapons will be used, especially near windows, doors or outdoor spaces.
10. **Legal check.** Laws on owning, transporting and displaying replica and real weapons, knives and swords vary widely by country, state and city. Check before you buy, borrow or rent.

## Choosing the least dangerous option [General knowledge]

Work down this list and stop at the first option that meets the production's needs:
1. **Cut it or mime it.** Does the scene really need the weapon to be seen?
2. **A non-functional prop** (rubber, resin, foam, wood, 3D print) that can't fire, cut or hit hard.
3. **A purpose-made stage weapon** (stage-combat sword, non-firing or blank-firing replica) supplied and supported by a reputable stage armoury, under a fight director.
4. **A real weapon or real-edged tool**, only where policy allows it, with a qualified specialist and a written safety plan (H 159; [ch. 43](43-firearms.md)).

## For the classroom

- **Write the policy.** Students draft a one-page weapons policy for your programme using the ten points above, then compare it with your institution's actual policy.
- **Least dangerous option.** For five scripted moments (a gunshot offstage, a sword fight, a stabbing, a hunting rifle on a wall, a kitchen knife used to chop food), students choose the lowest-risk prop that still tells the story.
- **Inspection drill.** Students inspect a set of stage weapons using a checklist, log what they find, and decide what's safe to use.
