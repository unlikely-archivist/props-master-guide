# 27. Surfaces: Sanding, Filling, Texture, Distressing and Protective Coats

> **[Check current]** Product names (Bondo, Jaxsan 600, Foamcoat, Sculpt or Coat, Future/Pledge floor finish, etc.) change; check equivalents. Flame-retardancy rules are local. W's fire classes (BS 476 Class 0/1) are British and have since been superseded (see below).

Once a prop is built, the surface still needs work. It has to be safe to handle, ready for paint, as smooth or textured as the design calls for, and sealed for durability (H 308).

## Sanding (H 308)

- Beginners consistently **underestimate sanding and filling**, which often takes longer than the build itself. Sanding makes a prop safe to handle, helps paint and glue adhere, and turns a homemade-looking object into a believable one.
- **Grit**: a higher number is finer. Work up through two or three grits, and **don't skip grits** (80 → 120 → 220, not 80 → 220). The same grit number gives different results on paper than on a grinding wheel.
- Start with the most aggressive tool and grit the prop can take, then finish by hand.
- **Wet sanding** (with wet/dry paper) keeps dust out of the air. Use it before final glossy coats, and never on absorbent materials.
- **Glossy finishes need a smooth substrate at every layer.** A coat of metallic paint won't make a cinder block look like sheet metal.
- **3D prints.** B hand-sands prints from 220 grit up to as fine as 2,000, and warns against power sanding, whose heat melts the plastic. Alternatives are a vibratory tumbler, solvent vapour (for ABS) or heat. Seams in multi-part prints can be filled with acrylic caulk or low-temperature hot glue (B 15–16, 93; [ch. 40](../5-materials/40-3d-printing.md#finishing-prints-b-1516-55-9293)).

## Fillers (H 308–311)

**Fill, sand, prime, and repeat.** Primer reveals the flaws you missed.

| Filler | Character | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Joint compound** | Calcium carbonate; brittle | Fine on rigid, gently handled pieces; cracks when the piece flexes or gets knocked |
| **Spackle** | Gypsum plus glue | Similar to joint compound |
| **Painter's putty** | Traditionally whiting plus linseed oil | Small holes |
| **Wood putty** | Stronger; "stainable" (it rarely matches) | Nail holes; weak on broken corners |
| **Cabinetmaker's putty** | **Fine sawdust from the same wood plus wood glue** | Stainable fills that match |
| **Caulk** (acrylic/silicone) | Flexible, waterproof | Wet props; a texture ingredient |
| **Plastic fillers**: Plastic Wood, **auto-body filler (Bondo)** | Hard, little shrinkage, machinable; good for corners and edges | **Toxic fumes and sensitizing dust**: ventilate, wear a respirator, let it cure away from others, sand with vacuum attached, wear gloves and sleeves. **Rasp or Surform it at about 5 minutes**, when it's "cheese-hard," to save sanding. Apply with disposable plastic spreaders |

For quick fixes, stain pens, wax sticks, and a **brown marker** handle scratches when the house is already seated (H 309).

A **skim coat** is a thin overall layer of filler, sanded smooth. Choose a filler that doesn't drip and doesn't dry too hard to sand. Joint compound is usually too fragile for props that get handled (H 310–311).

## Texture (H 311–315)

Texture can come from three places:
1. **Intrinsic**: build from a material that already has the texture, such as reclaimed lumber or rusty steel. Or apply just a **skin** of it, like thin aluminum laminated to MDF (H 311–312).
2. **Added**: glue on fabric or leather (damask under paint reads as embossed metal), or apply texture compounds.
3. **Removed**: carve weathered-wood grain lines, or let spray-paint solvents eat unsealed foam into "rock" (ventilate and wear a respirator) (H 311).

KF carves, burns and presses textures directly into EVA foam: wood grain, scales, foil-pressed leather, hammered metal (KF 24–25; [ch. 33](../5-materials/33-eva-foam.md#textures-kf-2425-39-4344)).

**Plasterer's tools for wet coatings** (H 312):
- **Roping**: drag a loop of hemp rope through the wet coating for a "painted many times" look.
- **Combs** for weathered wood.
- Brushes, sponges and rag rolls; a putty knife or screed left slightly rough.
- Impressions from screens, fabrics or lumpy objects.
- **Plaster stenciling**: trowel compound through a thick stencil and screed it flat. Build thick designs in layers.

**Scenic dope** (H 312–314) is a catch-all for homemade texture pastes:
- **Basic modern recipe**: 1 quart latex house paint + 2–5 tubes **latex** caulk (not silicone) + 1–2 cups joint compound. More paint makes it thinner and self-leveling; less makes it thicker and dimensional, up to nearly sculptable.
- **Flexible version**: joint compound plus PVA, starting at 1:1.
- **Additives**: plaster or water putty (harder, more brittle), paper pulp, sand, sawdust, gravel (mixed in or sprinkled on).
- **Monster mud** (from the haunted-attraction world): roughly 1 part latex paint to 5 parts joint compound. Dip burlap or cloth in it and drape over forms for crusty texture and "sculpted" fabric drapery.
- It dries over days. It's water-resistant, not waterproof, so topcoat it for outdoor use.

**Ready-made texture products** (H 314–315):
- **Elastomeric roof coating (Jaxsan 600)**: thick, flexible, takes acrylic tints. Texture it while wet. **It can't be sanded and can't be made smooth.** Hart's example is faux ham slices coated on foam.
- **Sculpt or Coat**: thin coats, light texture; also a mâché medium.
- **Crystal Gel**: dries clear with body; makes faux brushstrokes on printed "paintings."
- **Acrylic gesso**: flexible. Thin coats smooth close-up camera props without burying detail.

**Polystyrene textures** (W 127–128, 140): each cutting tool leaves its own surface (knife, wire brush, hot wire, band saw); wafting a hot-air gun over carved foam shrinks it slightly into convincing masonry, though more vigorous melting releases too much styrene. Polymer plaster mixed with sawdust makes bark; polymer plaster dusted with talc can be impressed with texture.

**Particle textures** (H 315): sprinkle sand, sawdust or dirt into glue or binder. **Glitter** goes into thinned PVA or clear sealer. **Flocking** blows fibers onto adhesive for grass, dirt, carpet or velvet linings (model-railroad grass on a topographic map).

## Coating foam (H 315–317; W 136–141)

First, ask what the coating is for: paintability, protection from spray-paint solvents, impact resistance, texture, or a sandable smooth shell (H 315–317). Both expanded and extruded polystyrene need coating for stage use; coatings can also change the texture and improve **fire resistance**. Wilson groups the options as scrimming and papering, proprietary coatings, plaster-based mixtures and GRP (W 125, 136–137).

| Goal | Options |
|---|---|
| **Just paintable / solvent barrier** | Thinned PVA, **acrylic gesso**, liquid latex, elastomeric primers |
| **Tougher shell, sandable** | Joint compound + PVA, scenic dope; plaster (heavy, fragile when thin); commercial foam coatings (Rosco **Foamcoat**, FlexCoat, Vanillacryl, Sculpt or Coat); **two-part polyurethane foam coatings** (StyroSpray/Styroplast types); **water-based resins** (Aqua-Resin types) |
| **Textured, flexible, unsandable** | Jaxsan-type elastomerics |
| **Laminated shell** | Papier-mâché; **cheesecloth** cloth-mâché (thin, keeps detail) |
| **Fiberglass over foam** | **Polyester dissolves polystyrene.** Use epoxy, or a barrier (joint compound, foil, cling film) between foam and resin (H 315–317). Wilson seals the foam with several coats of paint or a layer of aluminium foil before polyester (W 141) |

Polyester resin eats foam; epoxy and polyurethane don't. Use laminating versions of resin for thin coats (H 317).

**Scrimming** (W 137–138): an old technique, long used to hold the parts of a construction together and give them one paintable texture, and often the base for other coatings. Glue lightweight cotton such as **butter muslin** on with **size** (animal glue) or **PVA**. Hot size is slippery and thin, so it's faster and lets fine detail show, thickening as it cools; PVA is slower and stickier but gives a somewhat tougher skin. The result is only a delicate skin, but it protects against minor knocks, takes paint well and adds almost no weight. Cut muslin to manageable pieces, brush glue on first, drop the muslin in, and work it with a wet brush, outward from the middle so the fabric goes into the detail; finish each piece before the next; big overlaps aren't needed; a little pigment in the size shows up gaps. Variations: other fabrics, or papers (brown paper scrumpled tightly and reopened repeatedly turns leathery); scrim also reinforces and visually unifies foam combined with other materials. Size recipe: [recipes](27-surfaces-and-texture-recipes.md#recipe-cards).

**Size gesso** (W 138–139): after scrimming, size mixed with an inert filler (whiting, marble dust or talc) to a creamy liquid, like old-fashioned gesso, brushed on in several coats, sands to a silky finish. Dilute the size with about half as much water again for easier sanding and less cracking in thick coats. It's delicate; strengthen it with a final coat of neat size, or glaze it with epoxy or acrylic varnish. Careful sanding and varnishing gives a finish like glazed porcelain (Wilson's polystyrene sink, with practical taps). Tint it; titanium dioxide gives brilliant white. **Wear a dust mask** when sanding: size dust is unpleasant and most fillers contain silicates.

**Proprietary coatings** (W 139): mostly water-based polymer emulsions with fillers, brushed, sprayed or trowelled. Rosco **Foamcoat** (heavily filled acrylic made for props) dries extremely tough and fire-retardant and sands fine, but it's hard work: there's always a trade-off between easy sanding and a tough film. Wilson also names an industrial insulation coating that dries to a flexible plastic film (texture it heavily or smooth it with a wet brush or rag when partly dry) and a fire-retardant coating useful as a quick coat for unseen surfaces. Theatre tends to stick with a product once it works; it's worth trying what other industries offer. **[Check current]**

**Plaster-based coatings** (W 139–141): plaster sets chemically, not by evaporation, so set time doesn't depend on thickness; you can **model detail** over the foam, retard it reliably, and vary reinforcement and additives. Unreinforced it must be thick to be anything but delicate, and neat plaster is "one of life's most unrewarding modelling materials," so add a thickener and/or finely chopped reinforcement. **Polymer plaster** (polymer solution instead of water) is tougher and grips the reinforcement better. Reinforce with jute scrim, open-weave glass cloth or multiaxial woven rovings: bed them into a layer of wet plaster for large areas, or dip strips in plaster for local strengthening. For heavy texture or over-modelling, mix in chopped reinforcement: **dry paper pulp** or cotton microfibres. Retard with **trisodium citrate** (stock solution 100 g per litre; about **10 ml, two teaspoons, per litre of mixing water doubles the set**, depending on the plaster); thicken with colloidal silica (a smooth modelling dough), fine sawdust or polystyrene dust (texture paste), or dry pulp. The simplest plaster coating is **plaster bandage** with a splash of PVA or acrylic medium in the water to retard and strengthen it: quick, it keeps much of the underlying detail, and it can be smoothed or modelled. A large carved crucifix was coated by spraying coloured polymer plaster from a hopper gun and smoothing it selectively with a wet brush as it set (W 141).

**GRP coatings** (W 141): epoxy and glass give very strong coatings; once cured, the polystyrene can be cut away to leave a tough hollow shell. Epoxy filled with colloidal silica, talc or marble dust can be modelled over the laminate, or applied on the lathe and turned as it cures. See [ch. 36](../5-materials/36-shells-and-laminates.md).

FF primes handled food props with FoamCoat, FlexCoat, CrystalGel or Sculpt or Coat. Paint applied straight to foam dries brittle and flakes (FF 15). See [ch. 41](../5-materials/41-prop-food.md#coatings-and-gels-ff-1315).

For **EVA foam**, KF heat-seals the surface, fills seams with Kwikseal or foam clay, then primes with brush-on Flexbond (which smooths) and/or spray-on PlastiDip (which doesn't) (KF 23, 28–29). See [ch. 33](../5-materials/33-eva-foam.md#filling-and-priming-kf-2829-36-40-47).

## Distressing and aging (H 317–319; M 158–159)

**Build it well, then distress it at the end.** Building "distressed" from the start gives weaker, less believable results (H 317).

**Agree the words first.** "Breaking down," "distressing" and "antiquing" are interchangeable for some people and specific for others; make sure you understand the designer's request, and discuss progress in concrete terms, with samples or references if possible (W 171). **Why age it?** Sometimes to enrich an object with a sense of history and draw attention to its age; more often to make it **disappear**: a freshly painted object stands out, an old one recedes and its significance is absorbed subliminally (W 171).

**How age shows** (W 172): colours fade; dust and grime collect in corners; objects in frequent use become polished or worn; silver tarnishes black, iron and steel rust, copper, brass and bronze corrode to greens and blues; indoor wood darkens, while wood exposed to wind and sun bleaches silver-grey and its soft grain wears faster, leaving the hard grain in relief; paint and varnish crack, peel, flake and blister.

**Most important of all: research and imagine the object's life.** How was it used, stored and handled? Was it repaired? Was it in a fire, a flood or a war? Random attacks produce the look of discount-store "faux vintage" (H 318–319).

**Physical techniques** (H 318; M 159):

- **Fabric**:
  - Wear it with a rasp, file or Surform; pull and stretch the trim.
  - Layer fabrics so they show through tears; sag and flatten cushions.
  - Unravel edges, add cheesecloth and pull threads.
  - Sew on shredded strips and crude repair stitches **without weakening the structure**.
- **Wood**:
  - Sand, rasp or file the edges and handling areas.
  - Take out chunks with a jigsaw or reciprocating saw.
  - Use a grinder flap wheel, belt sander, palm sander or rotary tool.
  - **Torch-scorch, then wire-brush** the ash to raise the grain.
- **Beat** wood or sheet metal with a chain or a bag of nuts and bolts, then darken the recesses and lighten the highs.
- **Scorch fabric edges**: test a scrap first, and keep water and an extinguisher handy. Many synthetics melt rather than burn.
- **Real dirt** rubbed in (H 318; M 159).
- **Plain 80-grit sandpaper**, by hand or power, erodes paint, wood, fabric and much else to great effect; Wilson uses a palm sander on faux-leather luggage (W 173).
- **Peeling and cracking paint** (W 173): smear a little petroleum jelly into corners before painting so the dried paint rubs away easily; or paint on a thin layer of latex, let it dry, paint emulsion on top, and work it with a wire brush into a broken, textured surface. **Crackle glazes** make an emulsion top coat break into bold cracks; **crackle varnishes** give subtler crazing like old ceramics.
- **Encrusted rust** for close viewing: build texture with sawdust and glue before painting (W 172).

Paint-based breakdown (dirt washes, rust colours, patina) is covered in [ch. 28](28-painting-and-finishing.md#breaking-down-h-340342-w-171173).

## De-shining (M 158)

Tech notes often say "please dull that down." Options:
- **Dulling spray** (a semi-permanent matte frost)
- light dusting with gray, brown or black spray
- frosting spray
- petroleum jelly (temporary; blurs reflections)

## Protective coats (H 319–320)

**Why**:
- Stop color rubbing off onto skin and costumes (some pigments are hazardous).
- Resist moisture outdoors.
- Resist heat: display windows can reach about 300°F, and props near engines get hot.

**Sheen**: **satin** usually suits stage and camera. Matte looks dead and soaks up light; gloss glares. Gloss suits retail and trade-show work (H 319).

| Coating | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Varnishes** (resin, acrylic, polyurethane, epoxy, oil) | A hard clear coat in many sheens |
| **Acrylic floor polish** (e.g., Future-type, sprayed from a bottle) | Even, flexible, easy |
| **Leather sealers** (flexible acrylic or polyurethane) | Seal veg-tan leather and dyes; also suede and vinyl |
| **Spray "lacquers"** | Usually acrylic or polyurethane in solvent. **Water-based versions behave differently**, so test them |
| **Tung oil** | Eco-friendlier wood finish with a golden sheen. 2–3 days per coat; props usually need 1–3 coats. Beware "tung oil finishes" containing little real tung oil |
| **Mineral oil** | **Food-safe** finish for bare wood |

Some water-based sealers clash with certain stains, red mahogany in particular. Oil finishes avoid the problem (H 320).

Sealing fabric changes its drape and look, so choose fabrics that are already water- or flame-resistant when you can (H 319).

## Flame retardancy (H 320; W 17–18)

Nearly every jurisdiction requires flame retardancy for items in front of an audience. A common field test is to hold a match to the material: it must self-extinguish within a set time, without flaring up or dripping molten bits (H 320). Fire rules exist to save life, by making a fire less likely to start and evacuation more likely to succeed if one does; set and prop materials help by slowing the spread of flame and limiting smoke (W 17).

- **British classes (as Wilson described them in 2003):** sets and dressings upstage of a safety curtain usually had to meet BS 476 Part 7 **Class 1** (lateral flame spread on a vertical surface); with no safety curtain, or downstage of it, **Class 0** might be required. Class 0 was confusing because it wasn't defined in BS 476 itself but in Building Regulations appendices, and really applied only to wall and ceiling linings; materials met it by being non-combustible or of limited combustibility, or, most commonly, by passing Class 1 with a low enough fire propagation index (BS 476 Part 6) (W 17). **[Verified 2026]** BS 476 national classes have since been replaced by the European **BS EN 13501-1** "Euroclass" system (A1–F, which also rates smoke and flaming droplets); in England the national classes were removed from building-regulations guidance in March 2025. Check what your venue and licensing authority now require.
- **Ask the fire officer early.** Local enforcement varied: some officers would allow small exceptions if consulted in advance, others not, but all would advise and help solve problems (W 17–18). The same applies to a US fire marshal (H 320).
- **Fire-retardant sheet and foam:** plywood up to 18 mm usually had to be certified in the UK (in practice, hardwood ply) (W 21); fire-retardant grades of MDF (W 21), FRA polystyrene (W 125) and fire-retardant upholstery foam and calico (W 143, 148) exist, so specify them when ordering.

Some finishes add retardancy, FR additives can be mixed into paints, and clear FR sprays exist. On fabric, though, FR treatment can stiffen it and make it itch against skin, so prefer **inherently flame-retardant** goods (silk, IFR muslin and canvas from theatrical suppliers) (H 320). Wilson's terms: **IFR** (inherently flame-retardant, made of a non-flammable material), **DFR** (durably flame-retardant; survives wetting and washing) and **NDFR** (non-durable; washes out) (W 176, 178–179).

**Treating materials yourself** (W 18): theatrical suppliers sell many retardants. Know what the material is made of, especially mixed-fibre textiles, so you choose the right one; get full data and instructions (some can be diluted, some can't); apply as directed, usually by dipping or spraying, **after testing samples** for effectiveness and for staining. Trims such as braid aren't covered by upholstery rules, so dip them in a retardant suited to their fibre (W 143).
- **Homemade retardant** for wood, natural-fibre fabric, paper and straw: borax and boric acid dissolved in hot water ([recipe](27-surfaces-and-texture-recipes.md#recipe-cards)). **Don't use this, or any solution not designed for the purpose, on anything worn against the skin.**
- **Casting and laminating materials** are best treated by mixing an additive in before use. Some products (such as certain polyester resins) come in Class 1 fire-retardant versions; for others, such as epoxy and latex, mix in **aluminium trihydrate** (sold as a pigment by large art suppliers and by chemical houses) or **hydrated magnesium carbonate** (a composites filler); Wilson calls these the two easiest to get and safe to use (W 18).

**Plan early.** Hart puts it this way: it's easy to flame-proof something that's already fairly flame-resistant, and far harder to fix a prop "built out of gas-soaked matchsticks." **[Check current: local fire marshal and code.]**

## For the classroom

- **Surface sampler.** Each student prepares 6"×6" boards showing a filled-and-sanded smooth surface, roped plaster, combed wood grain, monster-mud burlap, flocking, and a coated XPS sample.
- **Life-history distress.** Give each student the same new wooden crate plus a written backstory (forty years in a fishing shed; one year in a war zone; a well-kept heirloom), and have them distress to match.
- **Retardant test.** Students treat cotton, a poly-cotton blend and kraft paper with Wilson's borax solution, then run the field flame test on treated and untreated samples and record staining (W 18).
- **Disappear or stand out?** Show two versions of the same chair, one fresh and one aged. Discuss Wilson's point that ageing usually makes a prop disappear, and when a designer might want the opposite (W 171).
- **Foam-coating comparison.** Coat identical foam blocks with five coatings, then drop-test and scratch-test them.
