# 26. Structure, Framework and Armatures

> **Sources:** H ch.10 (pp. 182–197), ch.13 (pp. 243–246); M ch.11 (pp. 152–154)

## Structure vs. surface (H 183–184)

"The camera only sees the last coat of paint" (H 183). Build a **structure** for strength, rigidity and light weight, then **skin** it to look like anything.
- A giant skull can be an internal frame with a thin carved foam shell instead of a solid block.
- A sedan chair is a steel frame skinned with lauan.
- An intricate cut-wood chair back can hide a welded steel frame in routed channels, filled over and painted invisible.

## Engineering principles for prop makers

### Compression beats fasteners (H 184)
Hart's analogy: a cookie sitting on a table stays put, while a cookie taped to the wall falls when the tape fails. A **compression leg** sits *under* the platform, so the leg itself would have to crush before the platform dropped. A leg screwed to the side relies only on the fasteners. Design so loads travel **through the material in compression** wherever possible.

### Spanning gaps (H 184–187)
- **Wale/waler**: a stiffening rib along a span. **Cleat**: a thin strengthening strip.
- **Ridges stiffen thin materials.** That's why armor and shields have center ridges and plastic cases have internal ribs. Folding a sheet of paper lets it span a gap.
- **Strongback**: two boards in an L (one flat, one on edge), rigid in both directions. **Hog's trough** is a U of three boards. **I-beams** and plate girders give almost the stiffness of a solid beam for far less weight and material.
- **Square-cube law**: scale something up and its weight grows faster than its strength (an ant vs. a human). Big props need disproportionately more structure.
- A **truss** is a beam with the non-structural material removed: a set of triangles.

### Triangulation (H 187)
A triangle can't change shape even with pinned joints, while a rectangle racks. Add a **diagonal brace**, or at minimum **corner blocks or keystones**, as on a flat. Hart braced a heavy table with diagonal rods after it racked when pushed from the end (H 188).

### Gussets, scabs and fishplates (H 188)
- **Gusset plate**: a plate reinforcing a joint, as in roof trusses.
- **Scab** (wood) or **fishplate** (metal): a plate across an end-to-end butt joint. Use one on both sides for more strength.

### Grain (H 188–189)
Wood, paper, fabric, stone and even forged or extruded metals are **stronger along the grain**. A crack has to take a winding path through grained material. Hart compares grainless orzo with grained linguine.

3D prints have a grain too: they are layered "like plywood" and break between layers. Orient a print so its layers don't run across a weak point such as a button shank (B 66–68; [ch. 40](../5-materials/40-3d-printing.md#level-3-scanning-a-real-button-b-6069)).

### Plan your failure points (H 189–190)
Design so the **cheap, replaceable part fails first**: a bolt rated below the leg it holds, or many small windowpanes instead of one big one. Put **replaceable wear parts** (screwed-on glides and pads) under furniture so neither the legs nor the stage floor wear down.

> Prop makers are not engineers. Don't build anything whose failure could endanger many people. In some places that work legally requires a licensed engineer (H 190).

### Rigid vs. flexible (H 190–191)
Rigid materials either hold or shatter. Flexible ones absorb impact and spring back, which is why papier-mâché can outlast fiberglass in a prop that gets hit. **Add give**, and **cushion** strike points. Film stunt weapons are often single castings of foam rubber: in effect, a weapon-shaped cushion.

KF puts a rod or pipe **core** inside nearly every foam prop and matches its stiffness to the prop's length. A thin aluminium rod was fine in short swords but wobbled in a 100 cm sword (KF 7, 21, 46; [ch. 33](../5-materials/33-eva-foam.md#cores-and-pvc-pipe-kf-7-1617-21-46)).

### "Tech it out" (H 190)
Borrow the technical director's habit. Before making a cut list for a big prop, **redraw the design to work out all the hidden structure**.

## Building curves: formers and stringers (H 191–192)

- **Formers** are profile-cut ribs, usually plywood, that set the shape of a curved surface. **Stringers** connect the formers and add places to staple or glue the skin.
- Skin with **bending plywood**, lauan, cardboard, or laminates ([ch. 29](../5-materials/29-wood.md#bending-wood-h-107110)).
- Formers can follow the inside or the outside of the skin, whichever is easier to attach to.

## Armatures and mesh frameworks (H 191–193; H 243–246)

- **Materials**:
  - cane and bamboo (soak for tight bends)
  - wire (soft gauges by hand; heavier ones with heat)
  - **armature wire** for sculpture
  - **pencil rod** (about ¼" steel) for skeletal forms
  - PVC or ABS pipe with fittings for quick geometric frames
- **Joining**: tape, ties, **lashing**, epoxy putty, soldering or welding. Spiral-wrap thin wire around armature wire so clay grips it (H 244).
- **Bulking out quickly**: crumpled **aluminum foil** or paper bound with tape or wire, under clay or papier-mâché (H 244–245).
- **Chicken wire** (galvanized hex poultry netting) is a ready-made 3D mesh. Combine it with wood formers and stringers for control.
  - The traditional combination of wood formers, chicken wire and papier-mâché built most large organic props before plastics.
  - It isn't inherently amateurish: the topiaries in *Edward Scissorhands* and many parade floats are built this way, and it's among the least toxic, most economical methods for big shapes.
  - Cut it with good wire cutters, wearing **thick gloves**. Fold cut edges over when stapling. Join pieces by interweaving or wire-stitching (H 192–193).

## Knots and lashing (H 193–197)

**Terms:** working end, standing end, bight, loop.

| Knot | Use |
|---|---|
| **Square knot** | Joining two ends (right over left, left over right). The **granny knot** is the weaker look-alike |
| **Clove hitch** | Tying to a pipe or pole. Finish with two half hitches |
| **Bowline** | A fixed loop that won't slip, used for lifting |

**Lashing** (H 195–197) makes flexible or non-destructive joints in pole frameworks:
- **Diagonal lashing** for poles crossing at 45–90°: clove hitch → 3–4 wraps on one diagonal → 3–4 on the other → **frapping turns** between the poles to tighten → half hitches.
- **Shear lashing** for parallel or slightly angled poles, or to extend a pole: clove hitch → 4–8 flat wraps → frapping → half hitches.

## Flats and platforms (M 152–154)

Prop masters rarely build scenery, but the same framing principles apply:
- **Soft flat**: 1×4 frame on the flat, stretched muslin, primed with paint or starch.
- **Hard flat**: frame on edge, skinned with lauan.
- Rails sandwich the stiles, with a toggle in the middle, glued and fixed with **corner blocks and keystones**.
- **Platforms**: 2×4 frame and crossrails, legs with **diagonal braces**, plywood deck.

## For the classroom

- **Spaghetti or paper bridges.** Spanning a gap with folded paper or spaghetti teaches ridges, trusses and triangles in an hour.
- **Failure-point design.** Students redesign a breakaway-prone prop (a stool that gets kicked over nightly) so a cheap, replaceable part fails first.
- **Lashing lab.** Teams lash a bamboo tripod or frame using diagonal and shear lashings, then load-test it.
- **Former-and-skin build.** Students build a curved panel (a column section or a car fender) with plywood formers, stringers and bending ply.
