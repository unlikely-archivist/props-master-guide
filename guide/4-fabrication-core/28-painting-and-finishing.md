# 28. Painting, Dyeing and Finishing

> **Sources:** H ch.17 (pp. 321–351); M ch.5 (pp. 57–59), ch.11 (pp. 158–159); W ch.9 (pp. 156–173)
> **Companion:** [Painting and Finishing Recipes](28-painting-and-finishing-recipes.md): wash, glaze, FEV and faux-finish recipes, with tutorials for wood grain, aged brass, rust, metal leaf, dyeing and staining, plus dry-pigment paint, pore-and-figure graining, bronze-powder gilding and copper patina.

## Color basics (H 322–324)

- **Hue** is the color's name. **Saturation** (also called chroma or intensity) is its vividness. **Value** is how light or dark it is.
- Artists' primaries are red, yellow and blue. Printers use cyan, magenta and yellow. Three primaries, three secondaries and six tertiaries give a 12-hue wheel.
- **Complements** (opposite each other on the wheel) mix to gray. Add a touch of the complement to tone a color down.
- **Tint** means adding white; **shade** means adding black. Pure hues vary in value: yellow is far lighter than violet.
- **Mix by the most direct route.** Use as few pigments as possible so you can remix the color for touch-ups. Layering is often better than mixing: paint pure yellow, then glaze raw umber over it, rather than chasing one muddy "perfect" mix (H 324).
- Different paint types may not mix at all (oil and acrylic, for example).
- **Stage color behaves differently** (M 159):
  - White reads almost fluorescent, and creams read white.
  - Black can read burgundy or navy under some gels.
  - **Test colors under stage light.**

## Layers and transparency (W 156–157)

Nearly every prop needs paint or another finish, and it's rarely a single coat out of a tin: most finishes are a **series of layers or processes**, from a dirty wash and a little wax to the many layers of wood graining or marbling. **A few thin or broken layers work better than one thick continuous one**, because each leaves something of those beneath visible. Get transparency from washes or glazes of thin colour (perhaps partly wiped away), or by spraying or spattering more opaque paint; keep thick paint for base coats and drybrushing (W 156–157). Designers often have very specific ideas here (W 156).

Information comes from the overlapping decorating, DIY, art and scenic-paint markets, specialist technique books, and manufacturers' product and safety data online (W 157).

## Pigments and paint (H 324–325)

- Paint = **pigment** + **binder** (the glue) + **medium/vehicle** (what evaporates).
- The **Colour Index International** assigns every colorant a generic name (e.g., PB 16) and/or a constitution number, plus a CAS number. Good artist paints list these codes, so you can count the pigments in a tube, compare brands, and **check toxicity**. Lead and cadmium still appear in some artist and industrial colors.
- **"Hue" in a pigment name means an imitation.** It's cheaper and less toxic, but doesn't mix quite as well.
- House paint is tinted from only about 12–16 colorants, so its saturation is weak, and multi-pigment paints separate when thinned. A single-pigment black stays black; a hardware-store black may reveal a dozen colors when watered down.

## Paint types (H 342–344)

| Type | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Acrylic** | Acrylic emulsion. Water cleanup while wet; **cures water-resistant**. Takes additives such as sand or marble dust. Easy, sticks to many surfaces, huge color range |
| **Latex ("house") paint** | No actual rubber; acrylic, vinyl or PVA binders with lots of filler. Cheap and plentiful. 100% acrylic latex is the best grade |
| **Scenic paint** | Formulated for scenery: purer, concentrated pigments for vivid, mixable color; less binder; usually **flat** (gloss glares under lights). Use it to match the set or cover large props |
| **Casein** | Milk-protein binder. **Spoils** (you'll know by the smell) |
| **Soy** | Brittle. Water-based soy paints aren't necessarily greener once processing is counted |
| **Watercolor, gouache, tempera** | Natural binders. Watercolor and gouache **re-wet** after drying. Commercial "poster paint" is closer to gouache. Can be among the least toxic paints |
| **Oil / alkyd** | Slow drying. **Alkyds stick to more surfaces**, which is why etching and plastic primers are often alkyd. You can prime with alkyd and then paint acrylic over it. "Enamel" is just a marketing word for any hard, glossy paint |
| **Tinted finishes** | Urethane and shellac paints; "lacquers" (now often water-based) (H 342–344) |
| **Acrylic scenic paint systems** | Ranges of saturated pigment in polymer binder, synthetic and traditional colours. A little goes a long way; thin with water, mix with polymer media, or tint emulsion and other materials. Expensive, especially strong synthetic colours, and part-used tins may not keep long (W 157–158) |
| **Dry pigments** | Mixed with a binder: diluted **glue size** (traditional, economical, not fully waterproof) or acrylic or PVA media (more permanent and convenient; PVA is cheaper but white when wet, so colours dry differently). Pigments keep indefinitely, dry size and bottled media keep well (emulsions are spoiled by frost), and the same pigments colour resins, plaster and solvent binders too (W 158–159). Mixing: [recipes](28-painting-and-finishing-recipes.md) |
| **Household emulsion** | Mixed to order in a huge range, so economical for large base coats; sample cards simplify matching. Strong primaries are hard to get and the heavy filler makes them intermix poorly, but dry pigment or acrylic scenic paint modifies them. Beware cheap paints with poor coverage (W 159) |
| **Water-based glazes** | Polymer emulsion glazes make a surface uniformly matt or glossy, bind pigment in transparent layers, and **isolate layers** so the next can be partly wiped off (as in graining); harder ones serve as protective varnish. From soft PVA (thin it for clarity) to hard-wearing two-part acrylics. Frost spoils them, so avoid market-stall bargains (W 159) |

**Water-based vs. solvent-based** (W 157): water-based materials use water as the vehicle (for thinning and washing brushes) and dry by evaporation; solvent-based ones use an organic solvent such as alcohol or acetone, whose vapour is given off as they dry. Water-based materials are safer in that respect (though they may contain other hazardous ingredients) and serve for general work; solvent-based ones are used where performance justifies the precautions or no water-based option exists.

**Solvent-based products still in use** (W 159–160):
- **Shellac**, an insect secretion dissolved in alcohol or methylated spirits: a quick-drying varnish for wood finishing, pigment binding and sealing porous surfaces. Ventilate for large amounts; wash brushes in meths or borax dissolved in warm water.
- **French enamel varnish** (see [FEV](#fev-french-enamel-varnish-h-348-w-159160) below).
- **Car sprays** (cellulose aerosols): convenient and quick-drying, but propellant, solvent vapour and fine droplets mean ventilation and/or a respirator and goggles indoors; their use has declined with safety awareness.
- **Wood stains**: dye in white spirit, which carries the dye deep without raising the grain (a problem with water-based stains); ventilate to speed drying and prevent vapour build-up.
- **Gold size and bronze-powder binders**: the solvent versions perform better; water-based ones are safer but technically inferior.
- **Metal primers**: usually solvent-based, adhering better to bare metal and resisting corrosion; with water-based metal primers, degrease carefully and allow a long dry.
- **Solvent varnishes** are harder-wearing and more waterproof than water-based ones; **two-part polyurethanes with isocyanate hardeners** are more dangerous than single-component types and must never be sprayed without proper ventilation and protective equipment.

**"Water-based" vs. "water-borne"** (H 327): true water-based paints re-dissolve when dry, but synthetic emulsions *cure*. Dried acrylic won't wash out of a brush. Wrap a wet brush in cling film to pause work.

**The VOC caveat** (H 342): "water-based," "low-VOC," "no-VOC" and "VOC-compliant" don't guarantee safety. They may only mean the solvents are exempt or untested. **Read the SDS.** Anything that cures releases something.

## Tools

### Brushes (H 325–328; M 57–58)
- Parts: handle, ferrule, bristles (natural hair or synthetic). Brush names aren't standardized.
- **Flat-ferrule brushes**: lay-in and priming brushes, the wide **Dutch** brush, **chip brushes** (cheap and disposable; cut the bristles into specialty shapes).
- **Sash brush**: angled bristles, used on edge for sharp lines (cutting in).
- **Oval-ferrule brushes**: **fitch/foliage** brushes, scenic liners, angular liners used with a **lining stick** (a beveled straightedge).
- **Round stippling brushes** for stencils. **Lettering brushes** for signs. **Acid brushes** (tiny, bought in bulk) and **foam brushes** (leave no strokes).
- Keep a "good" set and a pile of cheap brushes. Students and glue jobs will ruin brushes, so let them ruin the cheap ones (H 327).
- Basic flat decorators' brushes, natural or synthetic, from 12 mm up, cover most work; artists' brushes for a lot of figurative work; long, fine flat brushes for glazing and varnishing; small artists' or model brushes for fine work (W 157).
- **Brush care**:
  - Never let paint dry in a brush.
  - Soak briefly, never overnight.
  - Oil soap works well for natural bristles.
  - Comb stubborn paint out with a wire brush.
  - Wash until the water runs clear (M 58).
  - Wilson's routine for every brush: wipe off surplus paint on rag or newspaper; rinse in the right solvent, getting paint out of the tops of the bristles; wash in warm soapy water and rinse out all the soap; shake out the water, shape the bristles by hand, and hang or stand the brush upside down to dry (W 157).
  - **Solvents don't go down the drain**: bottle the waste and ask your local authority about disposal. Used methylated spirits left to stand settles its sediment and can be reused. Brush restorer or paint stripper revives hardened brushes (try strong hand cleaner first), though some of their life is gone (W 157).

### Rollers (H 328–329; M 59)
- **Nap**: short for smooth surfaces, long for rough; ½–¾" is the general-purpose choice. 3–4" rollers suit props.
- Use **synthetic covers for latex**. Lambskin is for solvent paints. **Foam rollers** suit slick surfaces and spreading glue.
- Texture rollers, **rag rolling**, string-wrapped rollers.
- **Technique**:
  1. Charge the roller on the tray's slope; never submerge it.
  2. Start in an unpainted area and keep a **wet edge**.
  3. Finish with light, overlapping, feathered passes.

### Spraying (H 329–331; M 58)
- **Rattle cans**: shake for minutes. **Dusting** with a can is the quickest subtle toning.
- **Refillable aerosol sprayers** (Preval-type).
- **Garden and tank sprayers** (Hudson-type) throw big droplets for spatter and texture. Use a brass nozzle for finer control, **strain the paint**, and flush with clean water after every use.
- Electric guns; **HVLP** guns (even coats, less overspray; need a compressor); **airbrushes** for small props and gradients.
- **Spray technique**:
  - Sweep **parallel** to the surface; don't swing an arc.
  - Start and stop off the edge, and overlap passes.
  - Stop if the can sputters (it's nearly empty).
  - Use a **turntable**, or suspend the prop.
  - Rest the piece on three points so the bottom edge doesn't glue itself to the floor.
- **Spray in a booth with a respirator.** Clean reusable sprayers immediately (H 331).

### Other tools (H 331)
- Natural **sea sponges**.
- **Floggers** (strips of muslin on a stick, slapped or twirled on the surface).
- Feather dusters, combs, rags.
- Improvised tools are fine: a paint-filled balloon, or drops of paint on a spinning surface.

### Masking (H 331–332)
- **Masking tapes**: beige or blue painter's tapes rated for days of clean removal; auto-body tape for the crispest lines; polymer-edge tapes that stop bleed.
- Mask large areas with paper or plastic; use liquid latex masks; wax resist (batik) on fabric.
- **Paint parts before assembly** to save masking time. On Hart's footlights, masking took longer than painting.

## The painting process (H 332–342)

Prime → base coat → overpaint → break down.

- Make a **painter's sample (elevation)** for designer approval, with reference images.
- Think in "recipes" built from technique "ingredients."
- **Keep treatments simple enough to reproduce.** A 39-step finish can't be matched or touched up (H 333).

### Priming (H 333–334)
- Primer gives a **uniform base** across mixed materials and absorbencies (end grain drinks paint).
- **Etching primer** for metal; **plastic primers** whose solvents bite into the surface (ventilate, wear a respirator, and let them dry in isolation); spot-filling and elastomeric primers.
- **Shellac** (or a pigmented shellac primer) dries fast and sands well; water-based polyurethane sealers.
- Latex or water-based contact cement as a primer on fabric. **50/50 PVA and water on MDF edges.**
- Brush in figure-eights for flat color, and sand between coats for ultra-smooth results. Two or three coats, until it's no longer blotchy.
- Sometimes the primer *is* the base coat; sometimes you skip priming entirely (glazing over bare wood to show the grain).
- On flexible EVA foam, KF uses only flexible primers and paints, avoids most spray paints (which crack when the foam is pressed), and seals with a flexible spray varnish (KF 7, 29–30; [ch. 33](../5-materials/33-eva-foam.md#painting-and-sealing-kf-7-30-36-40-44-47)).
- On 3D prints, B primes with high- or medium-fill auto-body spray primer, PlastiDip, acrylic paint or gesso, then tests for rub-off and plans repaints (B 55, 93; [ch. 40](../5-materials/40-3d-printing.md#finishing-prints-b-1516-55-9293)).

### Base coat (H 334–335)
- The flat "average" color. Some treatments build light to dark, others dark to light.
- Thin paint hides brush strokes. **Brush in the direction of the grain** you'll paint later.
- Let it dry before washes or drybrushing. **Cut in** edges first.
- Spray areas you can't reach with a brush **dark** so they disappear.
- **Wet blending**: X-strokes with two brushes; feather with a clean damp brush for gradients; pre-wet the surface for organic flow; let a dominant color blend the others.
- **Overemphasize contrast and texture in the base coat.** Glazes will mute it, and the hidden bold colors make the surface come alive under changing light (H 335).

### Overpainting (H 335–339)
- Step back often. **Stage distance and lighting need more contrast** than looks right in the shop (H 335–336).
- **Wash vs. glaze**: a wash is thinned with more vehicle (water); a glaze is made translucent with clear **medium**. Thin acrylic too much with water alone and there isn't enough binder left to stick (H 336).
- **Overall glaze** to tone and unify (a brown glaze warms everything).
- **Shadow washes** into recesses: thin with half water, half alcohol for flow and working time; add a drop of dish soap; wipe the high points with a dry brush or rag (H 337).
- **Oil-paint wipe-back** (KF 40): over a varnished base, smear dark oil paint everywhere and wipe most of it off with paper and a little turpentine, leaving it in recesses. See [ch. 33](../5-materials/33-eva-foam.md#painting-and-sealing-kf-7-30-36-40-44-47).
- **Highlights**:
  - **Scumbling**: a nearly dry brush jabbed on, stipple-fashion.
  - **Drybrushing**: a nearly dry brush dragged lightly so it catches raised texture.
  - Repeat with progressively lighter tints.
- **Poured paint** for drips and stains: tilt the piece, or blow on it.
- **Wet-on-wet** techniques:
  - **blooms** (drop water, thin paint or alcohol into wet paint)
  - **spatter** (toothbrush and thumb; strike the brush against a stick)
  - **blotting** (sponge, crumpled tissue or rags)
  - **crumpled cling film** pressed into wet paint for pooled, hard-edged patterns

### Faux finishes (H 339–340)

> "Everyone knows what wood grain looks like until they have to paint wood grain." (H 339)

The texture underneath must support the finish ([ch. 27](27-surfaces-and-texture.md)). Sample recipes:

| Look | Starting recipe |
|---|---|
| **Aged silver metal** | Black base + silver drybrush (less silver looks older) |
| **Gold / copper / brass** | Dark brown base + metallic drybrush |
| **Leather** | Black base + brown drybrush |
| **Wood grain (faux bois)** | Base color, then a thinner grain color. While wet, drag a **rubber rocker grainer** (rock it for knots) or **graining combs** (rotate and shift for variation). Overgrainers, graining floggers, crusty old brushes. Start high-contrast, then tone it down with a glaze (H 339–340). Wilson's detailed method is below |
| **Marble** | A wet-on-wet base, glaze, spatter, **feather-dragged veins**, sponging, glaze |
| **Luminous depth** | Many translucent glazes. Add **mica, bronze or pearlescent powders**. Bottled metallics often look flat on their own |

### Wood graining in depth (W 160–164)

Stage graining ranges from simple treatments that help a glaring prop "disappear" to elaborate imitations of fine period timbers. It differs from traditional architectural graining in often portraying **old or damaged wood**, in being seen from further away, and in usually relying on **water-based** media where tradition used solvent-based ones; home-improvement books are still a good source of technique. Try ordinary brushes and cheap or home-made tools before buying expensive specialist ones (W 160).
- **How many layers?** Most graining is an opaque base coat under one or more broken or transparent layers that mimic the wood's **figure** and **pores**, often with a glaze between layers so the next can be wiped or combed away cleanly. A simple background-furniture treatment might be just a glazed base and a top coat wiped or brushed off directionally; a specific decorative hardwood might take a base, separate pore and figure layers, a toning coat, several rubbed-down varnish coats and wax, though it's rarely necessary to go that far (W 160–162). Dragging scrumpled greaseproof paper through a wet top coat gives a quick grain (W 161).
- **Use references.** Study real pieces of the wood: the most prominent pattern is the key to your technique; the **palest colour** leads to your base. Make samples to test colours, techniques and the whole process, remembering that every layer is changed by those after it (W 162).
- **Base colour:** usually plain (mottle it if the wood shows mottling), slightly **paler and brighter than the palest colour in the wood** after any glazing. White emulsion tinted with acrylic or dry pigment, or shop-mixed emulsion for big areas, just thick enough to cover without brush marks; two thin coats beat one thick. Rub fine work down with wire wool or fine paper. If you can cope with fumes and slower drying, tinted solvent-based eggshell makes a smooth base that needs no glaze (W 162).
- **Figure** comes from how the board was sawn relative to the growth rings and rays. **Quartersawn** faces show rings as close parallel lines and rays as intermittent stripes across the grain; **plainsawn** faces show broader, wider-spaced rings with rays as slashes along the grain, and near the centre the classic **heart grain** of interlocking vees. Paint bold ring patterns with a small brush and break them up by flogging or dragging a stiff dry brush; or brush on, wipe with a cloth and pull a rubber comb or bubblewrap through the thin layer left. A **heel or rocker** (a curved rubber or dense-foam surface with concentric ribs), bought or made, pulled through wet paint gives heart grain faster but with less control than painting it (W 162).
- **Pore and fibre effects** may be all that distinguishes woods without strong rings. Paint a thin transparent layer, wipe it with the grain using a rag or dry brush, then pull a tool through it: scrumpled paper, plastic or fabric, steel wool, scourers, fine rubber combs and bubblewrap each leave distinctive marks; one pull differs from many, a varied angle or a shaky hand from a smooth one. For pores both along and across the grain, wipe across then pull something coarse along, or pull along while fresh and across when nearly dry. Or streak the wet layer with a dry brush and then flog it for a subtle pore pattern, or spatter finely and flog or drag to stretch the droplets along the grain (W 162–164).
- Dragging through at the end of each stroke needs planning and **masking tape** where grain directions meet. For three-dimensional grain, Wilson used a thick, coloured flexible coating and a big home-made rocker (W 164).

### Metallic finishes (W 164–171)

Use them to persuade the audience something is metal, or to imitate a traditional finish such as a gilded frame (W 164).

| Finish | Character |
|---|---|
| **Metal leaf** | The brightest: effectively a continuous layer of polished metal. Few colours, but modifiable; shines up through stains and glazes, and can be wiped back to full brilliance for highlights (W 165–166) |
| **Bronze powders** (metallic pigments) | Tiny flat wafers of metal, dusted onto a tacky surface and buffed, or mixed with a binder into a brilliant but short-lived paint (W 165, 170–171) |
| **Ready-mixed metallic paint** | Most convenient but dullest; "silver" and "gold" can look like metallic grey and beige. Fine when that's enough, or as a base for another finish (W 165) |
| **Powdered graphite** | In shellac, PVA or wax, rubbed or painted on and buffed to a rich lustre like old polished iron or steel. The less binder, the brighter, and the more likely it ends up on performers' hands; not permanent (especially with wax) and may need renewing (W 165). Wilson finished a wooden axe blade this way |
| **Metal powders in resin** | Pure metal powders in a resin gel coat, polished after casting ([ch. 36](../5-materials/36-shells-and-laminates.md)) (W 165) |

**Background colour** (W 165–166): broken or partial metallic over a chosen ground is far commoner than total coverage, and the ground changes everything. Traditional gilding grounds were chosen to bring out real gold and silver: **scarlet, red ochres or earthy greens under gold on furniture**; paler raw sienna, yellow ochre or chrome yellow in architectural work; **greys and blues under silver**. Black or dark brown, especially left in the recesses of mouldings, emphasizes age; brighter, multicoloured grounds read more "theatrical." Glazes on top will affect the ground too, so make samples. (Compare Hart's red and black **bole**, below (H 349–351).)

**Bronze powders in detail** (W 170–171):
- **Dry dusting** into tacky gold size (prepared as for leaf) fills small gaps in leafing (buy powder matching the leaf) and is a finish in its own right: an even, satin coverage, quick on intricate surfaces, buffable a little. Broken effects are hard on flat surfaces, but you can highlight mouldings by **printing** with upholstery foam loaded with powder. Apply with a soft brush or felt or foam pad. The powder goes airborne: **mask and goggles**, and work where the fallout is easy to clean.
- **Wet:** thick, viscous binders give the brightest effect, because a layer of flakes floats and aligns at the surface; apply sparingly, almost drybrushing. Cellulose-lacquer binders are bright and fast (clear nail varnish does for tiny areas; clean up with acetone); Japan gold size, and purpose-made acrylic or shellac binders also work (white spirit, water and meths cleanup respectively). Mix as you go: a puddle of binder and a heap of powder side by side on an old lid; dip the brush in binder, then powder; keep alternating for line work, and revisit the binder often when drybrushing. **Premixed pots look muddier**, so only premix for a large area.
- **Chemistry warnings:** "gold" powders are mostly bronze or brass and react with ad hoc binders such as PVA or button polish, turning **green or blue** (fine if they're on and dry first). "Silver" powders are aluminium and **"gas" with water, potentially explosively if the mix is stored**, so use solvent-based media with them.
- **Combinations:** lay in a deep gold (or one darkened with pigment), drybrush a medium gold into it, then pick out highlights in pale bright gold. Aluminium powder with a little black makes a more convincing steel. Powders in **wax polish**, buffed lightly, allow subtle blends and ageing because the wax stays workable, and for the same reason aren't permanent.

### Breaking down (H 340–342; W 171–173)
- **Aging** (normal wear and patina), **distressing** (neglect or trauma), **weathering** (outdoors), **antiquing** (old but well kept). Most art directors want every camera prop broken down to some degree.
- **Treat age as a need**: How old is it? How was it used, stored and maintained? Look for script clues (left in the rain, decades in an attic). Research how each material ages: rust concentrates on edges and corners, copper grows **verdigris**, and paint fades and cracks where hands touch it.
- **Age relative to the production's period, not today.** A 1920s chair in a play set in the 1920s should look new (H 341).
- **Dirt washes and glazes** (W 172): a dirty or contrasting wash takes the newness off. On absorbent surfaces (bare wood, fresh matt emulsion) it can be very thin; on glazed or non-absorbent surfaces it needs more binder to stick. Brush on a weakly pigmented glaze and **immediately wipe it selectively** so the dirt stays in hard-to-clean corners. A little goes a long way: it's easier to add than to remove once your chair has dried "looking as if it has been dipped in farmyard slurry."
- **Rust** (W 172): the red and yellow **earth pigments** are themselves iron oxides and match rust's range. New rust is yellow ochre or an ochre/raw sienna mix; it ages through burnt sienna or red ochre ("classic rust") to burnt umber or Indian red. Use more than one, a lighter over a darker, stippled on thick with an old brush or foam. Avoid repetitive patterns: stand back and fix anything that catches the eye. For heavy encrusted rust seen close up, build texture with sawdust and glue first.
- **Copper and bronze patina** (W 172–173): natural corrosion runs from pale blue to mid-green; apply it as a base and pick out highlights or worn areas in bronze powder. The chemical patina on bronze sculpture and fittings is usually a deep, translucent brown, best done as glazes over leaf or another metallic. These corrosions are less crusty than rust.
- **Tools**:
  - watery brown, gray or black washes (thinned with denatured alcohol to flow into cracks)
  - an overall brown glaze "bath"
  - dusting with black or brown spray
  - **Design Master Glossy Wood Tone** floral spray, a props favorite since the 1980s
  - a Preval loaded with a wash, FEV or dye (H 341–342)

## Dyes (H 345–348)

A **dye** soaks into the material; paint sits on top as a coating. Dye fabric when paint would stiffen it. Dyes also work on wood, leather, paper and some plastics.

- **Equipment**: stainless steel pots (avoid copper, iron and brass); glass, stainless or plastic stirrers. A **dedicated washing machine** can serve as a dye vat; never share it with laundry. If you borrow the costume shop's dye vat, respect it and clean up after yourself.
- **Heat**: silk, wool and blends around 190°F; cotton and linen can boil.
- **Add wet to dry**: paste powders with a little water first, and dissolve salts separately. **Add acid to water**, never water to acid.
- **Test swatches.** The color in the bath isn't the color on the cloth, wet fabric looks darker, and dye lots vary. Rinse until the water runs clear (up to 40 min). Use a fixative if the dye calls for one.

| Dye class | Fibers |
|---|---|
| **Acid** | Wool, silk, nylon (near boiling) |
| **Basic** | Also acrylic fibers; diluted, gives watercolor-like scenic effects |
| **Fiber-reactive** (Procion MX type) | Cellulose fibers: cotton, linen, rayon, raffia, reed (cool to lukewarm) |
| **Disperse** | Synthetics: polyester, acetate, nylon. Very hot; some carriers are toxic; can irritate skin |
| **Direct** | Cotton, linen, viscose; simple, but needs a fixative |
| **Leather dyes** | Alcohol- or acrylic-based |
| **Union/household** (Rit, Tintex type) | Combination dyes for blends; easy, less brilliant |

- **Blended fabrics**: dye in two baths, one dye class per fiber. **Never mix dye classes in one bath.**
- **Dyeing polyethylene** with union dye: alternate a hot (not boiling) bath with ice water so the plastic doesn't deform.
- **Solvent dyes for PVC and ABS**: a few drops in THF-based "clear cleaner," brushed on. Take full solvent precautions.
- "**Aniline dye**" is a theatre misnomer for acid and basic dyes. True aniline dyes are for wood and leather.
- **Tea and coffee** baths age paper and tone fibers (a sepia mop head, for example).
- **Removing color**:
  - Diluted bleach works on cellulose, but damages fiber and **ruins silk and wool**. Spraying bleach is an inhalation hazard.
  - Color removers (sodium hydrosulfite, thiourea dioxide) work in a hot bath with ventilation.
- **Distressing with dye**: neutral dye baths; spray dye or FEV selectively (mask the overspray; floors get slippery); sponge or pour.

## FEV, French enamel varnish (H 348; W 159–160)

Shellac tinted with alcohol-soluble dye or metallic powder and thinned with denatured alcohol. A starting recipe is about 4 parts alcohol to 1 part white shellac plus a few drops of dye (H 348). Bought ready-made in brilliant transparent colours, it's thinned as quick-drying glazes and stains, used full strength to paint glass, clear plastic or metal, and used to **break down fabric colour or pattern**, especially sprayed; ventilate well, particularly when spraying (W 159–160).
- **Pros**: transparent; tints metal while keeping it metallic; builds rich layers; stays flexible on leather and fabric.
- **Cons**: alcohol fumes (ventilate and wear a respirator); not washable; shellac isn't waterproof; results are unpredictable. **Test first.**
- **Food version (FF 84, 131, 160):** *The Fake Food Cookbook* uses a much thinner FEV, 1 part shellac to 10 parts denatured alcohol tinted with brown and red **leather dye**, as a transparent glaze for bacon, ham rind and turkey skin. See [ch. 41](../5-materials/41-prop-food.md#glazes-and-finishes-ff-12-16-84-131-160).

## Wood stain (H 348–349)

- Stain penetrates bare wood to change its color without hiding the grain. Some stains are really thin pigment glazes, which you can imitate with acrylic glazes.
- Brush or rag it on, then wipe off immediately, after 10–30 minutes, or after hours depending on the depth you want. Test on scrap. Layer stains for complex color.
- **Water-based stain**: faster, soap cleanup, fewer fumes. **Oil-based**: better penetration and evenness, but strong fumes for 8–12 hours or more. Hart became sensitized to stain fumes. Let stained pieces dry **away from actors and coworkers**. Water-based is usually the better choice for props.
- **Seal stained wood**, or it rubs off on skin and costumes. Stain-and-poly combinations can't be adjusted afterward.
- End grain soaks up more stain.

## Other finishes (H 349–351)

- **Cold gun blue** on steel: wipe on, wipe off for a blue-black finish with some rust protection. Most other chemical patinas are too toxic and complex for a typical prop shop, so fake them with paint instead.
- **Markers**: black hides screw heads and shiny edges; brown hides furniture scratches.
- **Metal leaf** (real gold, or imitation Dutch metal in gold, silver and copper colors):
  1. Seal the surface (acrylic gesso if you want to sand it smooth).
  2. Lay down **bole**, usually paint: red bole warms gold; black bole gives a cold, Art Deco look.
  3. Apply **gold size** and let it reach tack.
  4. Lay the leaf with a static-charged brush; it can't be repositioned.
  5. Brush and burnish, patching missed spots.
  6. Seal with shellac (amber shellac warms it) or polyurethane/acrylic (H 349–351).
- **Working with metal leaf, Wilson's method** (W 166–169):
  - **Materials.** Real gold and silver leaf are beaten so thin that handling them is slow, skilled work; theatre usually substitutes **Dutch metal** (copper-zinc; No. 2½ is close to 22-carat gold, and a warmer, more coppery grade is often useful onstage) and **aluminium** (unvarying colour and a poor imitation of silver; bright silvers are generally harder than golds). Pure copper leaf exists too. These are thicker, so much of the work can be done with clean fingers. **Loose** leaf suits intricate 3D surfaces; **transfer** leaf (backed with tissue) is quick on plainer ones. Usually 140 mm squares, from 25-leaf books to packs of thousands; **ribbon gold** is continuous transfer leaf on a roll (W 166). "Schlag" is another name for imitation gold leaf (W 182).
  - **Size.** Water gilding is slow and skilled and rarely used in theatre. **Oil gilding** uses gold size, a varnish that dries to a tack: traditional linseed-oil sizes are rated **1 hour to 24 hours** (the nominal wait before gilding); **Japan size** is faster still; slower sizes give a finer surface and longer **open time**; all wash up in white spirit. **Water-based acrylic sizes** tack in minutes and stay open almost indefinitely, but don't self-level, froth if overworked, and **never fully harden**, so they don't suit handled props; oil sizes toughen with time. For maximum wear, use **epoxy** as the size, testing when to apply the leaf (W 166–167).
  - **Prepare** the surface and ground colour: leaf reproduces every flaw, so for traditional polished gilding use several coats of coloured gesso or shellac and pigment rubbed silky with fine steel wool, or flow-coat painted wood with self-levelling varnish. The surface just needs to be dry and neither absorbent nor greasy: prime bare wood; degrease metal and plastic (W 167).
  - **Size** thinly and evenly with a soft brush, no puddles (they dry slower); leaf sticks only where there's size. Wait for the right tack: faintly sticky to a fingertip or knuckle but leaving nothing on the skin. Don't start too soon. Some water-based sizes lose a bluish tint when ready (W 167).
  - **Lay** loose leaf onto the size and settle it gently with fingertips (a clean dry brush helps in intricate areas) without touching the size, without pressing hard, and without worrying about overlaps or small gaps: aim to cover the whole surface first. Tear leaf into small random pieces for a broken effect. Press transfer leaf on, rub the back of the tissue with a finger or burnisher, and peel (W 167–168).
  - **Fill gaps** with small pieces (or dust with matching bronze powder to speed things up); for broken effects, half-close your eyes or step back to spot over-large gaps (W 168).
  - **Polish in** with a soft dry cloth folded into a tight "nib" for corners: dab up and down to stick the leaf, then rub side to side to polish it and break off the overlaps. Polishing kills the size's tack, so gaps found now need fresh size (W 168–169).
  - **Protect**: Dutch metal and copper tarnish and aluminium dulls. Later tinting or ageing protects the leaf; to keep it bright, a thin coat of shellac or animal glue prevents tarnish at a slight cost in brilliance, though theatre timescales often make it unnecessary (W 169). Modify the leaf with dark dyed shellac, or pale matt emulsion worked into corners (W 169). Steps: [recipes](28-painting-and-finishing-recipes.md).
- **Chrome**: true chrome plating is outsourced even by large shops. Fake it with chrome-look paints or polished aluminum over a **flawless** smooth surface, and test samples first.
- **De-shine** reflective props with dulling spray (M 158; see [ch. 27](27-surfaces-and-texture.md#de-shining-m-158)).

## Disposal (H 344–345)

- No significant amounts of paint down drains.
- Wipe brushes, wash them in a container of warm soapy water, rinse in a second container, and finish in the slop sink.
- Dry out leftover latex with kitty litter or sawdust, or spread it on newspaper, before discarding. **Donate usable paint.**

## For the classroom

- **Color-mixing drill.** Students match five paint chips using only three or four pure pigments, recording their recipes. Then they rematch one a week later from their notes.
- **Faux-finish boards.** Students produce wood grain, marble, rusted iron, aged brass and leather on primed boards, each documented as a recipe.
- **Break-down study.** Students paint two identical cast props, one new and one at forty years old, and present the "life story" behind their choices.
- **Grain from life.** Students bring a real piece of wood, identify its most prominent pattern and palest colour, and build a three-layer water-based graining sample to match it (W 162–164).
- **Gilding samples.** Students gild the same small moulding over three grounds (red ochre, black, pale blue) with Dutch metal, then compare how each ground changes the "metal" (W 165–169).
- **Dye lab.** Students dye cotton, silk and polyester swatches with fiber-reactive, acid and union dyes, then compare results and wash-fastness.
