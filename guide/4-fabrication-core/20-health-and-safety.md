> "Your goal in life is not to finish a single prop; your goal is to build props for the rest of your life." (H 44)

## The core idea: safety as infrastructure

Hart argues that no material is simply "good" or "bad." What matters is whether the **safety infrastructure** for it is in place (H 26):

- ventilation, and dust and fume collection
- personal protective equipment (PPE): gloves, eye and face protection, respirators, hearing protection
- fire protection
- training and certification
- disposal and cleanup procedures

A professional shop that follows every fiberglass precaution is safer than a hobbyist sanding MDF at home with none (H 26). If your shop **can't** use a material safely, that material is not an option for the prop (H 19).

Further principles (H 26–27):
- **Reading about or watching a technique doesn't make you ready to do it.** Neither does a manufacturer's demonstration video.
- **Laws are a minimum**, and they lag behind the chemicals on the market.
- **Home shops can be worse than work shops.** You breathe curing fumes 24 hours a day, and no workers' compensation covers you.
- **Don't let the lack of a perfect plan stop you from improving.** It's never too late to start protecting yourself (H 19, 26).

Employees should follow shop procedures, use the PPE provided, **report injuries, accidents and unsafe conditions**, and never use a machine they haven't been trained on (H 26, 33). Wilson's version: if you're not sure something is safe, don't do it; ask a competent person (W 28). Because prop work spans so many materials and processes, at unpredictable scales, prop makers need to be **particularly vigilant** (W 16).

## Risk assessment (W 16–17)

British law (as Wilson described it in 2003) obliges employers, and the self-employed for themselves, to assess the risks their work poses to workers and anyone else, with further regulations on manual handling, hazardous substances and electricity. Amateur companies and individuals may not be bound by all of it, but the measures are a useful framework (W 16). **[Check current]**
- **Risk = how serious the hazard is × how likely it is.** An extreme hazard that's very unlikely is low risk; a less severe one that's more likely may be higher. Anything that doesn't come out negligible must be dealt with (W 16).
- **Risk belongs to a process, not a product.** Styrene inhalation is a far greater risk laying up polyester GRP in an open mold than pouring a little into a small closed mold (W 16–17).
- **Reduce unacceptable risks to negligible** by changing the process or circumstances: substitute a safer adhesive, or install extraction for sawdust. The higher the risk, the more urgent (W 17). This matches Hart's hierarchy below.
- **Likelihood is the hardest part to estimate**, and the most important for severe hazards. **Review an assessment when circumstances change, or after an accident or near miss**, which may show you misjudged the likelihood (W 17).
- Wilson shows part of a written assessment for life casting, listing each substance (petroleum jelly, plaster bandage, alginate, soap release, barrier cream, cleanser) with its form (dust, liquid, solid), exposure limits, routes of exposure, quantity, storage and whether a data sheet is held (W 17). A blank template is in [Appendix B](../../appendices/B-paperwork-templates.md#14-process-risk-assessment-ch-20).

## Personal readiness

- **Dress for the shop** (H 28; M 146):
  - Remove ties, watches and jewelry around machines.
  - Tie long hair back and up, and roll sleeves up securely.
  - Tie apron strings behind you.
  - **Avoid wool sweaters**: their long fibers can drag you into a machine.
- **Eyes**: wear impact-rated safety glasses with side protection **whenever power tools are running anywhere in the shop**. Chemical splash goggles and face shields are separate items for separate hazards (H 28).
- **Gloves**: use the right kind for the task (cut-resistant, leather for heat and welding, chemical-resistant). **No gloves on many spinning machines**, because a glove can drag the whole hand in (H 28).
- **Feet**: never open toes or heels in a shop, theatre or set. Wear steel toes where needed, and only if they fit, because they never break in. Wear supportive, laced shoes for long standing (H 28–29).
- **Hearing**: damage starts around 85 dB and is cumulative. Wear rated earmuffs or plugs (H 29).
- **Tetanus**: keep your shot current (every 10 years) (H 31).
- **Body**: lift with your legs, keep the load close, and **ask for help**. Many jobs list 50–75 lb lifting requirements (H 41). Use dollies and hand trucks (M 62). Vary your posture, use anti-fatigue mats, and take regular breaks (H 41–42).
- **Fatigue, illness and impairment**: no alcohol or drugs, and avoid medications that warn against operating machinery. Headaches and dizziness can mean chemical exposure, so get to fresh air (H 30–31; M 141).
- **First aid and CPR**: every prop master and technician should be trained (M 142).

## Shop practices

- **Keep it clean**: tools and cords on the floor are trip hazards, and dust and liquids are slip hazards. Unswept dust gets kicked back into the air (H 29–30).
- **Don't work alone** with power tools or toxic materials. If you must, arrange check-ins, keep a phone with signal at hand, and install **kill switches** (H 30).
- **Warn others** before loud or hazardous operations (H 30).
- **No eating or drinking** in work areas (H 30).
- **First-aid kit**: wall-mounted, with a clear path to it, kept stocked (M 141–142).
- **Eyewash stations and deluge showers** in professional shops (H 48).
- **Protect others as well as yourself**: every precaution applies to people around you too (W 28). Keep offcuts cleared and tools, materials and extension leads out of walkways (W 28).
- **Electrical safety** (W 28): check plugs and leads regularly and replace damaged ones; fit the right fuse for each tool; use residual-current (RCD) protected sockets where possible; don't run extension leads where tools or materials can damage them. In the UK, portable tools get regular **PAT** (portable appliance) testing (W 180).
- **Accident reports for every injury**, however minor. A small cut that later becomes infected, or a slip that becomes back pain, is only covered by workers' compensation if it was documented (H 31; M 144–145).

## Tools and machines (H 33–34; M 145–146)

- **Sharp blades are safer**: less force means more control.
- **Before every cut, check where your fingers and body are relative to the blade.** Hart describes nearly losing a thumb on a table saw and seeing classmates cut fingers holding straightedges for razor knives.
- **Footing and balance** first.
- **Clamp the workpiece** rather than holding it by hand when the tool could grab it.
- **Keep guards on.** Check guards and alignment on unfamiliar machines.
- **Unplug before changing blades or adjusting**, unless the machine is labeled for adjustment while running.
- Use the right tool for the job: a screwdriver is not a chisel, and a chisel is not a pry bar.
- Don't carry or unplug tools by the cord. Keep fingers off triggers while carrying.
- **"Just this once"** is how injuries happen. So is the inattention of habit.
- **Ladders**: use them only as designed, with latches locked and both hands for climbing. *Never* stand an extension ladder on an A-frame (M 146).
- **Woodworking basics** (W 28): guards in place, push sticks whenever possible, chisels and gouges always cut away from your body and free hand, gloves for big sawn timber and ply sheets, goggles whenever power tools run (make it a habit to wear them all the time), and ear defenders with many tools.
- **Knives** (W 136): cut away from your body and other hand; keep spare knives in a tray, not loose on the bench; don't use sharp knives when tired, ill or likely to be distracted.
- **Drills and grinders** (W 54, 56): clamp the work; use a side handle and both hands; the breakthrough at the end of a big hole is the most dangerous moment; never grind with a thin cutting disc; contain sparks with barriers.

Table-saw specifics are in [ch. 29](../5-materials/29-wood.md#the-table-saw); welding specifics are in [ch. 30](../5-materials/30-metal.md#welding-safety-h-142144-w-62).

## Chemicals and hazardous substances

### Know what you're using
- **Physical hazards**: explosive, flammable, combustible, compressed or reactive. **Health hazards**: carcinogens, toxins, irritants, corrosives, reproductive hazards, **sensitizers** (H 34–35).
- **Carcinogens have no known safe exposure level** (H 35).
- **Use the least toxic product that does the job** (H 36). Wilson's example of the same principle is replacing a hazardous adhesive with a safer one (W 17). Hart's example: methyl ethyl ketone (MEK) strips paint fast and harms your brain, while soap and water takes longer. If a hazardous product is truly the only option, invest in the infrastructure to use it safely.

### Sensitization (H 35; also H 279, 282, 304)
The hardeners in **two-part products** (epoxies, polyester resin and auto-body filler, polyurethane casting resins and foams) are sensitizers. You may use them for years, even decades, with no reaction and then become **permanently allergic**: hives, oozing sores, even anaphylaxis. Hart's technical editor developed this after 30 years in props. Over half of workers exposed to epoxy develop allergies (H 282).

### Safety Data Sheets (SDS) and labels
- Keep an SDS for **every product in the shop**, accessible to everyone (H 36; M 143), kept safely where it can be found in an accident (W 114). The SDS lists ingredients, hazards, exposure limits, PPE, first aid, storage and spill response. Manufacturers must provide them.
- Read labels. **"Nontoxic" covers only acute toxicity**: chronic hazards and untested chemicals can still be labeled nontoxic. **"Natural" means nothing** (H 38).
- **Keep products in labeled containers.** A temporary unlabeled container is acceptable for one shift only. "Mystery containers" can't be disposed of properly, can't be described to poison control, and endanger firefighters (H 37–38).
- **Ventilate, use one product at a time, and don't mix chemicals** (M 143).
- Free guidance: ACTS (Arts, Crafts and Theater Safety), the American Lung Association, your department of labor (H 36). Monona Rossol's *Health and Safety Guide for Film, TV & Theater* is Hart's "required reading" (H 369). **[Verified 2026: the 2nd edition (2011) appears to be the latest.]**

### Disposal (H 36; also H 274, 344–345)
- Never pour oils, solvents or toxic chemicals down drains.
- **Never pour plaster or uncured resins down drains.** They harden in the pipes.
- Dry out leftover latex paint before putting it in the trash, and donate usable paint.
- Seal resin-contaminated trash and remove it from the shop, because it keeps off-gassing (H 36).
- **Solvents**: bottle the waste and ask your local authority how to dispose of it; used methylated spirits settles and can be reused (W 157).

### Substance-specific hazards from Wilson
- **Polyester, epoxy and urethane** (W 114): avoid all skin contact; epoxy hardeners, the polyester catalyst **MEKP** (highly corrosive; severe burns) and **isocyanate** urethane components are the most dangerous, so devise ways of working that keep you away from them. Watch contaminated gloves and resin on container sides. **Styrene's safe level is only just above the point where you can smell it.** All three heat as they cure: a big mass left in a pot can give off toxic fumes or catch fire, so cover it with cold water and take it outside, mix only what you need, and spread leftovers thin. Pour urethanes slowly to avoid splashes, and throw away clothing contaminated with the isocyanate part.
- **Never let neat polyester accelerator and catalyst touch** (W 108).
- **Mold rubbers** (W 97): silicone, urethane and polysulphide compounds all involve hazardous chemicals; diisocyanate hardeners are particularly dangerous. Goggles and gloves, no skin contact, no eating or drinking, careful clean-up, and **warning signs round uncured rubber** if others use the space.
- **Fillers** (W 109–110, 114): most are fine powders, many contain crystalline silica, and some (such as fumed silica) go airborne easily: goggles and a suitable dust mask, and close containers straight after use. **Glass strands** on cured laminates are as sharp as needles; **dust from freshly cured resin** may still be toxic.
- **Plaster** (W 91): bags up to 35 kg, dust, eye protection with wet plaster, skin drying.
- **Polymer clay** (W 76): some contain phthalate plasticizers; don't eat or drink while handling, don't make food or drink props, pipes or cigarette holders from it, and never bake above 130°C.
- **Low-melting metals** (W 123): lead alloys need local exhaust or a respirator and gloves for handling and filing; prefer lead-free. Molten metal plus a drop of water sprays metal as steam forms.
- **Aluminium bronze powder** reacts ("gasses") with water, potentially explosively if a mix is stored; use solvent binders (W 171). Bronze powders go airborne: mask and goggles (W 171).
- **Two-part polyurethane varnishes** with isocyanate hardeners must never be sprayed without proper ventilation and protective equipment; cellulose car sprays need ventilation or a respirator and goggles indoors (W 160).

**3D printers.** B asks instructors to read the SDS for every filament (checking for ultrafine particles and VOCs), to question using any filament without one, and to take fume questions to the administration or safety office rather than relying on judgement (B 14–15, 118). See [ch. 40](../5-materials/40-3d-printing.md#read-the-sds-b-1415-118), which also summarises current NIOSH guidance **[Verified 2026]**.

## Skin protection (H 38–39)

- Solvents (including those in water-based paints, adhesives and cleaners) and resins pass through skin.
- Choose gloves using the manufacturer's **permeation charts**. No single glove resists everything. Nitrile is common, as are neoprene, vinyl and multilayer gloves. Post the charts at your glove station.
- **Keep latex gloves out of the shop.** They don't stop chemicals, can speed absorption, and cause latex allergies. Latex also **inhibits silicone cures** (H 283).
- Don't reuse disposable gloves: breakthrough is invisible and lasts at most about 8 hours. Check gloves for holes, don't share them, and wash your hands afterward.
- Protect your forearms with sleeves or longer gloves. Wear aprons or suits, splash goggles and face shields as needed. Sanding dust from "dry" materials can still irritate skin.

## Respiratory protection (H 31–32, 39–41)

> "Do not breathe anything but air." (H 31)

If you can smell it or see dust or fumes, you are breathing it, and some hazards are invisible and odorless.

**Hierarchy:** substitute a safer material → capture at the source → ventilate → enclose → *then* use respirators, the **last line of defense** (H 41).

**Source capture and ventilation:**
- dust collection on each machine, or a shop vac on portable tools
- downdraft tables
- fume extractors for soldering and welding
- **spray booths** for spraying
- **fume hoods and glove boxes** for urethane casting, which off-gasses for hours
- keep yourself **upwind**: fresh air should flow past your face to the contaminant and then to the extraction
- change filters regularly (H 31–32; M 147)

**Respirators:**
- Dust masks are respirators and must carry a **NIOSH rating**. Unrated "pollen" or "paint" masks are useless.
  - Letter: **N** (not oil resistant), **R** (oil resistant), **P** (oil proof).
  - Number: **95/99/100**, the percent filtered (100 = 99.97%, "HEPA").
  - **N95** covers wood dust, powders and most welding fume. Use R or P ratings when oils are present (H 40). Wilson's European equivalent for wood dust is a disposable **EN 149 FFP2** mask or better (P2 for polystyrene dust): make sure it fits, keep it clean, store it in a clean bag between uses, and throw it away after the recommended time, usually about **8 hours of use** (W 22, 28, 136). **[Check current]**
- **MDF** (W 22): MDF is wood dust (mostly softwood) bound with urea- or melamine-formaldehyde glue, and machining it releases dust and some formaldehyde, a carcinogen. After a decade of scare stories (and the myth that MDF was banned in the USA), the UK Health and Safety Executive's 1999 assessment found machining MDF no more hazardous than other timber products and recommended the same controls: **extract dust at source, and wear masks where extraction isn't possible**.
- **Gases and vapors need chemical cartridges.** The **organic vapor** cartridge is most common in props. Formaldehyde and ammonia need different cartridges. Cartridges don't stop particulates, so use combination cartridges or pre-filters (H 40).
- **Chemical cartridges saturate silently.** Track hours of use (about 8 continuous hours is typical) and store the respirator sealed in a bag (H 40–41).
- Some hazards **no cartridge stops**, including **isocyanates** (urethane foams and resins) and **MEKP** (polyester catalyst). These need supplied air or enclosure (H 41, 279).
- **Respirator programs**: in US workplaces, OSHA requires a medical evaluation, **fit testing** and training before anyone wears a respirator, including a disposable dust mask, and **no facial hair at the seal** (H 27, 40). In a university, EHS runs this program.

For EVA-foam hazards (rotary-sanding dust, fumes from burning textures in with a soldering iron, heated PVC pipe and puzzle mats, spray rubber primers), see [ch. 33](../5-materials/33-eva-foam.md#safety-kf-9-15-16-22-24-29-36-40) (KF 6, 9, 16, 24, 29).

## Fire safety (H 42–44; M 149–151)

**Extinguishers.** Have the right types, in the right number, at the right locations (near ignition sources and throughout the shop).

| US class | Fuel | Extinguisher |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Ordinary combustibles: wood, paper, fabric, many plastics | Water, ABC dry chemical |
| **B** | Flammable liquids and gases | BC or ABC dry chemical, CO₂ |
| **C** | Energized electrical equipment | BC or ABC, CO₂ (no residue) |
| **D** | Combustible metals | Dry powder (special) |
| **K** | Cooking oils | Class K |

Water spreads B and K fires, worsens D fires, and can electrocute you on C fires. Props shops mostly risk A, B and C fires, so ABC units suit most areas (H 42–43).

**Hot work** (H 43, 142):
- Weld away from combustibles (OSHA cites 35 ft) **[check current rules and your hot-work permit process]**.
- **Fire-watch** after welding or grinding, because sparks can smolder in sawdust: about 30 minutes (H 43, 142); Wilson says inspect the area for an hour (W 62). Keep an extinguisher and fire blanket at hand, use a steel bench, and keep drawings and clutter away from the work (W 62).
- **Never connect spark-producing grinders or abrasive cut-off saws to a wood dust collector.** The result can be a dust explosion.

**Flammables** go in a flammables cabinet away from exits and ignition sources. Don't use flammables near someone else's ignition source (H 43).

**Flame-proofing for audiences** (H 16, 43–44, 320):
- Local codes may require props to be fireproof, fire resistant or flame retardant. Definitions vary by jurisdiction.
- **Decide the requirement before you build.** Some materials can't be adequately treated, and a fire marshal can remove a finished prop.
- Treatments can stiffen fabric or make it itchy, so **inherently flame-retardant fabrics** are often better (H 320).
- The common field test is holding a match to the material: the flame must self-extinguish within a set time, with no flare-up and no molten drips (H 320). **[Check current: local fire marshal, applicable NFPA standards.]**
- Wilson describes the British classes of the time (BS 476 Class 1 and Class 0, now superseded by Euroclasses), fire officers who will advise if consulted early, and how to treat materials yourself, including a homemade borax retardant and fire-retardant additives for resins and latex (W 17–18). Details in [ch. 27](27-surfaces-and-texture.md#flame-retardancy-h-320-w-1718).
- **Store flammables** in suitable metal cupboards (W 15), and specify fire-retardant grades of polystyrene and foam even for shop stock (W 125, 143).

Melting candle wax for fake food has its own rules: a candle thermometer, never above 220°F, never unattended, an ABC extinguisher at hand (FF 33). See [ch. 41](../5-materials/41-prop-food.md#safety-specific-to-food-props-ff-19-and-every-recipe).

## For the classroom

- **SDS scavenger hunt.** Students pull the SDS for five products in your shop and report: hazards, required PPE, cartridge type, disposal method, and whether a safer substitute exists.
- **Glove lab.** Using a manufacturer's permeation chart, students choose gloves for acetone, epoxy, polyurethane resin and latex paint.
- **Case studies.** Discuss Hart's sensitization story and the plaster-entrapment injury in [ch. 38](../5-materials/38-molding-and-casting.md#molding-the-human-body-h-286290). What infrastructure would have prevented each?
- **Risk-assess a process.** Students fill in the Appendix B risk assessment for two versions of one job, such as polyester GRP in an open mold versus a small closed-mold pour, and explain why the same resin scores differently (W 16–17).
- **Required checkouts.** Make the safety orientation and machine checkouts a graded prerequisite for shop access. Coordinate with your EHS office.
