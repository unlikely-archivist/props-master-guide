# 5. Budget, Time, and Feasibility

> **Sources:** M ch.4 (pp. 39–40), ch.6 (pp. 83–91), ch.7 (pp. 104–108), ch.9 (p. 127); H ch.2 (pp. 18–21), ch.19 (pp. 361–366)
> **[Check current]** Prices, tax rules, the federal mileage rate and purchasing procedures change often and vary by institution. Use your institution's current figures and forms.

Two resources shape every props plan: **money and time**. Hart adds a third, **expertise** (H 362). Before you spend anything, find out whether the show is feasible with what you have.

## The project triangle (H 362)

**Fast, cheap, good: pick two.** In practice these are sliding scales, not on/off switches:

- A slightly more expensive solution that saves a lot of time is usually worth it. One that costs twice as much to save an hour usually isn't.
- Cheap materials can cost more overall once labor is counted. Hart's example: cheap plywood that needs hours of filling and sanding can end up costing more than MDF or finish-grade ply (H 20, 362–363).
- Props share a budget, so savings on one prop can fund a better solution for another (H 20, 362).
- **Safety is a cost, never an option.** If a material needs ventilation, a respirator program or special gloves, include them in the cost, or choose a different material (H 362).

## Money

### Set aside a contingency
- **Mussman:** shave 25% off your materials budget the moment you receive it and treat it as a buffer. A $1,000 budget becomes $750 to plan with (M 104).
- **Hart:** add 20–25% to each estimate (H 364).

Both say the same thing: things will go wrong, and the money to fix them has to come from somewhere.

### Build a cost breakdown *before* spending (M 105)
For each prop on the list, estimate materials and labor. **Estimate high.** Record actual costs next to the estimates as you go. Over time this becomes your best reference for future shows. Template: [Appendix B](../appendices/B-paperwork-templates.md#2-cost-breakdown--build-estimate).

Hart's estimating rules (H 363–364):
- **Count everything**, including screws, glue and stock you will need to replace. His chandelier for a Broadway *Merchant of Venice* needed 700 feet of lamp cord, a "couple of bucks" item that came to $350.
- Include **tax, shipping and delivery fees**.
- Keep a typical-price list for materials whose prices fluctuate, such as lumber and steel.
- Use records of past builds to estimate repeat items.
- Budget **prototypes separately from copies**. The first one costs more because you are working out the method and building patterns and jigs.
- Budgets are often fixed before designs are, so you may have to **budget in reverse**: set a ceiling and find a method that fits under it.

### If the numbers don't work (M 105–107)
1. Revisit the plan. Could you borrow instead of buying, or buy instead of building (or the reverse)?
2. Show your supervisor the prop list next to the cost breakdown. They may spot errors or alternatives.
3. Bring in the director and designer. Most will adjust the list when given good information and alternatives.
4. Never assume more money will appear, and **never wait until dress rehearsal** to announce a shortfall.

When rehearsal requests arrive, check the budget before promising anything. Find out how essential the item is, and use the contingency for things the production truly can't live without (M 127).

## Time

> "Performance dates never change as a result of props being incomplete." (M 106)

### Labor vs. turnaround (H 364–365)
- **Labor time**: the hours someone actually works on the prop.
- **Turnaround time**: the calendar time until the prop is ready. This adds shopping, shipping, setup, **drying and curing**, cleanup and delivery, and depends on how many hours a day anyone can work on it. An eight-hour job at two hours a day takes four days.

### Estimating better
- **Keep a work journal** of how long tasks take. Hart discovered that putting casters on furniture (16 holes, 16 bolts) took 30–60 minutes he had never budgeted (H 364–365).
- **Break big tasks into small ones you can estimate.** You may not know how long a dead goose takes to build, but you know how long it takes to glue on twelve feather pads (H 365).
- Add a **time contingency** for the forgotten hammer or the dried-out can of paint (H 365).
- Estimate at **your** speed, not a faster colleague's (H 366).

### Plan backward from deadlines (H 365)
Work back from the hard dates:
- Order by Friday or lose the weekend.
- The last coat needs three hours to dry before curtain.
- The designer must deliver final drawings by a certain date if the prop is to be ready for first tech.

Then tell people those dates.

### Time-saving habits (M 106)
- Batch your shopping and plan routes.
- Gather all materials and tools before starting a build.
- Don't procrastinate.

### Feasibility check (M 104–107)
Do you have enough **money**, **time**, and **people with the right skills**? If not, raise the flag now (see [ch. 3](03-collaboration-and-communication.md#raise-your-red-flag-early-m-107108-127-137)).

## Expertise (H 366)

Build in materials you know, especially on a tight schedule. A strong carpenter should lean on wood. Hart suggests that an unfamiliar material like fiberglass doesn't belong on a short timeline, and that a hard-coated foam may be the better choice. Experiment when the schedule allows, with a backup plan ready (H 20).

## Running a shop crew (H 365)

- Give each artisan a mix of large and small tasks so no one sits idle while paint dries.
- Post a **master to-do list** of big and small projects. Artisans **write their names** on the tasks they take, so two people don't start the same job.
- Keep quick filler tasks ("shorten the wand one inch") for the fifteen minutes before lunch.

## Paperwork and accounting (M 39, 83–91)

Ask finance and your supervisor how purchases work before your first shopping trip. The common documents (templates in [Appendix B](../appendices/B-paperwork-templates.md)):

- **Purchase orders (POs)**: for vendor accounts and card charges. Record items, quantities, prices and the **account code**. Usually signed by the department head and their supervisor. Staple the receipts on and keep a copy.
- **Petty cash / reimbursement forms**: receipts totaled by account code.
- **Budget ledgers**: one per budget line per show (materials, labor, travel, shop supplies), with a running balance.
- **Timesheets**: for you and any freelancers, submitted on time.
- **Mileage logs**: date, destination, odometer readings. Commuting is excluded. The reimbursement rate changes yearly **[check current]**.
- **New-hire paperwork** for freelancers, filed *before* their first timesheet (US examples are W-4 and I-9) **[jurisdiction-specific]**.
- **Tax-exempt letter**: nonprofits and schools can often avoid sales tax, and some wholesalers require proof of a business or tax ID.

At closing, **close the budget**: chase outstanding receipts and submit every reimbursement and PO (M 140).

## For the classroom

- **Estimating lab.** Students estimate materials, labor and turnaround for three props from a real list, then compare against actuals from a past production (or against each other's estimates).
- **Triangle debate.** For one prop, present three methods with different cost, time and quality, and have teams argue for each.
- **Journal assignment.** Students keep a time journal across a semester of shop hours and calculate their own rates for common tasks.
