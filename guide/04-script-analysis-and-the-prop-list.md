# 4. Script Analysis, Design Documents, and the Prop List

> **Sources:** M ch.7 (pp. 92–108); H ch.2 (pp. 12–17), ch.6 (pp. 79–83)

Mussman divides propping into two halves: **preliminary preparation** (script, designs, meetings, feasibility) and the **physical work** of getting props (M 92). The prep work is what keeps the physical work sane.

## Reading the script

**Read it twice** (M 92–93):

1. **First read, for the play.** Read it as a story. Get the plot, the characters, the world and the mood.
2. **Second read, for props.** Use a legal pad or spreadsheet and stop at every prop reference. Record:
   - the page number
   - the prop
   - who handles it
   - every detail about its condition or use

### Where props hide (M 93–94)
- **Setting description**: period, location and mood. Often mentions set props and dressing.
- **Character descriptions**: socioeconomic status, age, occupation and sometimes a signature object.
- **Stage directions**: blocking and the condition of objects ("holding her half-eaten sandwich").
- **Dialogue**: where most props live. Watch for small details ("a Hershey's kiss... melted into a chocolate foil pancake").

Some playwrights specify everything, while Shakespeare's stage directions are sparse and the props mentioned in his dialogue can be vague. If you don't understand the plot, get a synopsis or do research. You can't have useful conversations about a play you don't understand (M 94).

### Ignore the published prop list (M 94–95)
The list printed at the back of an acting edition documents the **original production**. Your production has its own concept. Glance at it if you like, but don't build from it.

### Devised or unscripted work (M 95)
Expect the list to grow throughout rehearsal. Stay in close contact with the director, SM and designer, and stay flexible. Make sure the creative team understands the realities of time and budget.

## Noting prop specifics

For every prop, think about:

- **Period and style.** Confirm the production's period early, because directors often relocate classics (M 95).
- **Function and action.** What does it *do*? Mussman's example is an armchair that a heavy chef dances on in the last scene: it must be reinforced and checked throughout the run. Newspapers with specific headlines may be read up close (M 96). Hart's rule is to **plan for the worst case**: "If you are told the actor will stand on a table, assume they will jump on it" (H 13).
- **Consumables.** How many does each performance use? Multiply by every rehearsal, dress, preview and performance (M 8, 96).
- **Effects.** "If it shoots, catches fire, creates smoke, changes shape, or performs a trick," flag it for extra planning (M 96).
- **Size and weight.** Does it need to fit in a pocket, hold a specific vase, or pass through a door during a scene change (H 14–15)? Should it be **heavier** for realism or stability (H 14)?
- **Department crossovers.** Electrics, costumes, fight choreography, sound.

## Reading design documents

Final designs usually arrive **six to eight weeks before opening** (M 96). Get the whole package and study it for the size, style, period, position and quantity of furniture and dressing, plus the mood, the characters' class and the period (M 97).

| Document | What it is | Use for props |
|---|---|---|
| **Ground plan** | Scale top view of the set on the stage | Furniture positions and footprints (M 97) |
| **Elevations** | Scale front and side construction drawings | Dimensions. Often includes elevations for special props (M 97) |
| **Paint elevations** | Scale color and technique drawings for the scenic artist | Matching palette and finishes (M 99) |
| **Model** (in a model box) | Scale 3D replica with color and texture | Answers most size, shape, style and color questions (M 99) |
| **White model** | Uncolored massing model | Fit and proportion (M 99) |
| **Rendering** | Perspective color sketch | Mood. **Sizes may be distorted**, so it is less reliable for measurements (M 99) |
| **Research images** | Designer's reference pictures | Detail and texture reference (M 99–100) |
| **Prop research / designer's prop list** | Images or lists of desired props | Saves you research time (M 100) |

### Reading prop drawings (H 79–83)

- Drafted props use **orthographic projection**: front, side and top views without perspective. The line types are:
  - solid lines for edges
  - **dashed lines** for hidden edges
  - **section** views, with crosshatching where the object is cut
  - break lines and dimension lines

  The US/Canadian standard is set by USITT, though designers bend it for clarity.
- **Critical measurements** must be exact because they relate to something else, such as a doorway or the object a box must hold. CAD drafting forces precision on every dimension, so ask which ones actually matter (H 80).
- **Think in three dimensions.** Hart once found that a fully dimensioned wagon drawing would have left one handle floating in mid-air (H 80–81).
- **Sketches** convey feel and leave room for interpretation. Squiggles usually mean ornament, so research the style and check whether the motif repeats elsewhere in the design (H 81).
- **Photographs**: ask *why* you were given the photo. It may be an exact copy, a guide, or one element to combine with others (H 81). See [ch. 12](12-drawings-measuring-and-layout.md) for getting measurements out of photos.

## Building the prop list

> "This list is the most important document the prop master will produce." (M 100)

**What it is:** a complete record of every prop in the production in every category: hand, set, dressing, costume, consumable, practical and effects. It is organized so that **anyone** can find each prop's act, scene, page, user and details (M 100).

**What it isn't:** a shopping list or a to-do list. Those come later (see [ch. 6](06-sourcing.md)).

### Format (adapted from M 101)
- Header: theatre, show title, list version and date.
- **Set props and dressing first**, with page references where they are mentioned.
- **Then hand props, in script order**, by act and scene.
- Columns: page · prop · character · notes (description, period, function, source, status).

Use a spreadsheet. A template is in [Appendix B](../appendices/B-paperwork-templates.md#1-prop-list).

### It's a living document
Mussman calls the first version the **preliminary prop list** and considers the list "final" only on opening night (M 100). Merge in the director's list (usually heavy on hand props) and the designer's list (usually heavy on set props and dressing). Update it after every meeting and every rehearsal report.

### Distribute it (M 108)
Once the director and designer have approved it, share it with:
- the lighting, costume and set designers
- the director and assistants
- choreographers
- stage management, who use it to track where props live and what is still owed to rehearsal
- the master electrician (practicals)
- the costume shop manager

## The prop bible (M 102–103)

A binder of at least 1.5 inches with sheet protectors (or a well-organized shared drive) holding **everything**:

- script
- production calendar
- contact sheet
- current prop list
- script notes
- director's and designer's notes
- reduced ground plans, renderings and prop elevations
- designer's research and your own research
- paint and fabric swatches
- key emails
- rehearsal reports
- important paperwork

Mussman keeps **financial records separate**. The bible travels to meetings and shopping trips, and the receipts are safer in the office.

## For the classroom

- **Prop-list lab.** Assign a short one-act. Students read it twice and build a full preliminary prop list in a spreadsheet. Compare the lists in class: what did people miss, and where were props hiding?
- **Design-reading quiz.** Give students a ground plan and elevation set and have them extract the dimensions and footprint of every furniture piece.
- **Worst-case brainstorm.** For five props, students list every way an actor might misuse each one and the construction response to each.
