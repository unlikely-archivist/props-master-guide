# 30. Metalworking

> **Sources:** H ch.8 (pp. 129–160); W ch.3 (pp. 50–67)
> **Companion:** [Metal Projects](30-metal-projects.md): a folded sheet-metal tray, a jig-bent ring, a welded candle stand, a soldered joint, cold-bent semicircles, S-scrolls and hoops.
> **[Check current]** Welding equipment, consumable designations and OSHA hot-work rules change. Check manufacturer guidance and your institution's hot-work permit process. Metal prices fluctuate. W's filter-shade standard (BS 679) and electrical ratings (13 A/20 A sockets) are British and from 2003.

Metal lets you make props that look delicate but survive abuse. Prop shops mostly use **hollow shapes** to keep weight down (H 130). Welded mild steel builds quick, durable, **fire-resistant** structures; welded joints approach the strength of the surrounding metal, and steel bends into curves. Weight for weight it's no stronger than wood along the grain, but it can be made into efficient hollow sections, its density packs the same strength into smaller parts, and it has no knots or grain to make it unpredictable. The drawbacks: it's harder to alter or attach other materials to, costs more, and doesn't suit elaborate shaping (W 50–51).

## Common metals (H 130–131)

| Metal | Strengths | Cautions |
|---|---|---|
| **Steel** (mild) | Cheap, strong, easy to cut, bend and weld, even for beginners | Rusts unless coated |
| **Aluminum** | Light | Pricier. Welding needs expensive equipment and training. Nonferrous, so it can be cut on wood machines with the right blades |
| **Copper** | Foil, wire, tube; easy to bend and solder | Expensive in sheet, so it's often faked with paint |
| **Brass** (copper + zinc) | Hardware, **lamp parts** (standard pieces that assemble into larger forms); easy with hand tools | Usually soldered or brazed rather than welded |
| **Bronze** (copper + tin) | Statuary; brazes well | |

**Forms:** rod, tube, pipe, **box tube** (square or rectangular), flat bar, plate, angle, channel, I-beam.

**Sizing:**
- **Sheet** is measured by **gauge**, which is weight-based and differs between metals. Lower gauge means thicker. Stock thicker than about ¼" is **plate** (H 131).
- **Tube** (structural) is sold by outside diameter (OD) and wall thickness. **Pipe** (for fluids) is sold by nominal pipe size and **schedule** (Sch 80 has thicker walls than Sch 40). OD = ID + 2 × wall (H 131–132).
- **ERW** tubing has an internal weld seam. **DOM** and seamless tubing have smooth bores, which matters when you need tubes that telescope or sleeve (H 132). ERW (electrical resistance welded) is the light tube office furniture is made from: sheet folded into a tube and resistance-welded along its length. It comes in rectangular, round and elliptical sections from a few millimetres to about 50 mm wide, walls 1–2 mm (in the UK often still sold in imperial sizes with the wall given as a gauge), in 6.1 or 7.5 m lengths. It's the most useful section for light structures; small solid round, square and flat bar suit detailed work. Buy **"black"** steel; **"bright"** steels are pricier precision-engineering stock. Angle makes small brackets for joints to timber or other steel (W 51).

**Buying** (W 51): steel prices depend on many factors, so phone for quotes, and ask for a stock list (unlisted sizes may still be available). **Record what you pay** for each section so you can budget, design economically and shop around. Suppliers have minimum orders and free-delivery thresholds, so keep a stock of the sections you use most and hang on to odd pieces of unusual ones.

**Ferrous vs. nonferrous**: a magnet sticks to ferrous metal (except some stainless). Nonferrous metals can often be cut on woodworking machines with the right blade and speed (H 132).

## Degreasing and marking (W 51–53)

- **Degrease first.** Steel comes greased against rust; left on, the grease gets on your hands and clothes and burns off as fumes when you weld (W 51–52; H 157–160). Wilson's cheapest, most benign method: on trestles somewhere a little water won't matter, rub economy washing-up liquid all over with a rag, then rinse with clean rags and warm water. You needn't dry it; wire-brush off any light rust later (W 52).
- **Marking:** a scriber scratches a precise bright line; on shiny stock such as ERW, colour the area with marker first and scratch into that. For less accurate work, marker pens or correction pens work on any steel. Wood setting-out tools work, but grease from steel transfers to wood and is hard to remove (W 52–53).

## Cutting metal (H 132–135; W 53–54)

- **Hand power tools**: jigsaw or reciprocating saw with metal blades; **angle grinder** with cut-off wheels, or a dedicated cut-off tool; rotary tool. Keep wheels in line with the cut, because **abrasive wheels can shatter**. Wear glasses or a face shield and a dust mask, and clamp the work (H 132). **Never grind with a thin cutting disc** (W 54). Grinders come in 110–125 mm and roughly twice that size; the big ones are powerful and take getting used to. Wear ear defenders and goggles; gloves protect against sparks. Watch where sparks go, contain them with barriers to protect others and prevent fires, and remember the work gets very hot and edges very sharp (W 54).
- **Portable band saw** (portaband) for clean cuts; dedicated metal circular saws (H 132).
- **Aluminum on the table saw** throws hot chips. Never wear gloves at a table saw, so wear heavy sleeves, use push sticks, and sandwich sheet between sacrificial plywood (H 133).
- **Abrasive chop saw**: fast, loud and sparky, less accurate. Never put an abrasive disc in a wood saw. **Cold-cut saw**: slow, with coolant, no sparks or heat distortion. **Horizontal band saw**: lowers itself through the cut (H 133). Abrasive machines are the cheapest but leave a ragged edge that needs a lot of cleaning up; cold cutters leave a much cleaner cut. Mechanical hacksaws and horizontal band saws are slow but can be left running while you mark the next piece (W 53). Big vertical metal band saws are expensive and, like wood band saws, poor at cutting long lengths to size (W 53).
- **By hand**: hacksaw (too many teeth for wood, so don't use it there) (H 133); Wilson recommends an **18 teeth-per-inch** blade, and says every shop needs one (W 53). **Tin snips**; **aviation snips**, color-coded **yellow = straight, red = left, green = right** (capacity roughly 18-gauge aluminum, 24-gauge mild steel, 26-gauge stainless); **tubing cutter** (score and tighten); **nibbler** (H 133–134). **Bolt croppers** cut flat bar and small solid sections; a **cold chisel** and hammer reach tight corners (W 53).
- **Deburr every cut tube.** The inside sliver can slice an actor's finger. Use files or a reamer (H 134). Every cutting method leaves a burr that can cut you and spoils the fit for welding, so remove it soon after cutting with a file, small grinder or hand-held belt sander (W 54).
- **Plasma cutter**: fast irregular shapes in thick plate; heat-distorted edges. **Laser cutting**: usually outsourced. **Milling machine**: precision channels and parts, essential for working weapons and mechanisms. A drill press can't substitute (H 134–135).
- **Cutting fluid** protects the metal's temper and tool life: WD-40, light oil or lithium grease for drilling, or saw-specific fluids. **Cut cast iron and brass dry** (H 135).
- **Temper**: hard steel is rigid and brittle; soft steel bends; spring temper springs back. Case-hardened steel has a hard skin over a soft core (H 135).

## Shaping (H 135–139)

- **Files**:
  - **Single-cut** for finishing, sharpening and draw-filing. **Double-cut** for fast removal.
  - Grades run bastard → second-cut → smooth.
  - Specialty files: needle files, diamond files, rifflers.
  - **Always fit a handle** (H 135–139); never hold a file by the bare tang (W 55).
  - Cut on the **push** stroke only, and don't rock the file.
  - Clean with a **file card**, and store files hanging separately so they don't dull each other.
- **Grinders**: angle grinder for freeform shaping, bench grinder for deburring. Aluminum-oxide wheels for steel; silicon carbide for nonferrous metals, stone and rubber. Flap discs leave a smoother finish. Grinding-wheel grit numbers don't correspond to sandpaper grits (H 136–137). For Wilson the angle grinder is the most useful shaping tool in a small props shop, though no precision instrument: sparks and heat discolouration make marks hard to follow (W 54–55).
- **Belt sanders for deburring** (W 55): a small one-handed belt sander deburrs faster and cleaner than a grinder, with no sparks and less noise. Hold small pieces so they **trail** the belt to avoid kicks. 40–60 grit cuts fastest; anything finer than 80 just polishes.
- **Drilling**:
  - **Center-punch** first (H 137). The pop mark stops the bit wandering and helps the chisel point start cutting (W 55–56).
  - Twist bits or metal hole saws only (H 137); Wilson specifies **HSS** bits (W 55).
  - Speed: bigger bits go slower; aluminum fast, brass medium, steel slow. Feed steadily, and **never drill steel dry** (H 137). Wilson's numbers: HSS cuts mild steel best when the bit's outside edge moves at about **30 m/min**, which works out to about **3,200 rpm for a 3 mm bit and about 800 rpm for a 12 mm bit**. Running too fast is the danger; too slow just takes longer, so a two-speed drill limits your maximum hole size. Lubricate with a **10% mix of soluble oil or cutting fluid in water**, applied often from an oilcan or bottle. Keep bits sharp: blunt bits need more pressure and overheat (W 55).
  - **Step up through pilot holes** for large bits (for example 5 mm, then 8–9 mm, then 12 mm); the intermediate hole helps control the breakthrough, the most hazardous moment with a hand drill (W 56).
  - **Clamp the work.** A bit catching at breakthrough can spin the piece into your hand (H 137). If the only option is holding work under your boot, wear steel toe caps and keep your balance; back injury risk is high. On a pillar drill, clamp the work or a vice to the table, never hold small pieces by hand, keep the clear telescopic guard on, and clear hot, sharp swarf regularly (not with bare hands) (W 56).
  - Use the side handle on hand drills (H 137); always drill two-handed (W 56).
  - Deburr new holes with a light touch of a countersink or larger bit. A large old bit reground to a 90° included angle makes a fast, easily sharpened countersink (W 56).
- **Metal lathe** for parts with axial symmetry (H 137).
- **Bending**:
  - Metal stays bent, unlike wood. Heat helps, especially with steel.
  - Use a machinist's vise and a cheater pipe for leverage, on a table that won't move.
  - Make a series of small bends for long curves. Hammer over the vise's anvil face or an anvil for sharp bends.
  - **Plywood forms and jig blocks** produce repeatable curves and rings.
  - Bending machines: Hossfeld-style universal benders, hydraulic tube benders, ring benders, **conduit benders**.
  - **Fill thin tube with sand** to prevent kinking. Kerf-bend, then weld the gaps (H 138–140).
- **Cold bending over plywood formers** (W 56–60). Steel bends far more easily red hot, but a surprising amount can be done cold, which is quicker, cheaper and safer. For strong sections, big work or high accuracy, farm the job out to someone with a **ring roller** (your steel supplier may arrange it) (W 56).
  - **Spring-back:** steel must be pulled past its elastic limit before it deforms, and springs back a little when released. So to get a semicircle you bend slightly more than a semicircle, at a tighter radius. Cut the former to the inside of the curve, bend a test piece, see how far it springs back, trim the former a little (err on the side of caution), and repeat until the bent piece matches a full-size drawing. With experience you can draw roughly the right former straight off (W 56–58).
  - **Set-up:** a strong, level bench, ideally fixed to the floor; a full-size drawing; scrap ply at least as thick as the steel (at least **twice** as thick for hoops, where the ends overlap); No. 10–12 screws (clamps get in the way); scrap blocks; goggles and gloves. Cut test blanks well over length, with burr-free ends, and trim them after bending (W 58, 60).
  - **Semicircle:** screw an 18 mm ply disc to the bench and a block that traps one end of the steel tight against it; both must resist strong sideways force. Lever a metre of stout timber against the steel and roll it round the former. For a one-off, bend round a former you know is slightly small, then pull the ends apart to fit the drawing (W 58–59).
  - **S-curve:** one former per curve, with enough overlap that the steel is trapped between them. Two people bending both halves at once stops the steel slipping. Adjust for spring-back without cutting anything from the overlap zone, and refit the formers in the same screw holes (W 59).
  - **Hoops:** either bend on an undersized former and let it spring open, or bend on a full-size former until the ends overlap, mark and cut them, force them together on the jig and weld. The weld must be strong enough to hold the trapped spring-back or the hoop won't stay round (W 59–60).
  - **Limits:** solid sections are limited by your strength and your bench's. Some hollow sections are too strong to bend, others too weak: the inside wall of thin tube ripples, and the ripple becomes a weak point where the tube buckles. All bending bulges the sides a little; if that matters, use a ring roller (W 60). Chandelier arms and furniture scrolls are typical hand-bent work (W 56–57).
- **Sheet metal** (H 140–141):
  - Cut and fold it like heavy paper; pattern it first in cardstock ([ch. 24](../4-fabrication-core/24-patterning.md)).
  - **Fold edges over**, because thin edges can't be dulled.
  - Join with folded seams, rivets, solder or brazing.
  - **Hammering** stretches and compresses sheet into shields, armor and platters.
  - A homemade **brake** (a clamp board plus a hinged bar) makes crisp bends.

## Welding

### Welding safety (H 142–144; W 62)
- **Electrocution**: keep water away; no wet gloves; inspect cable insulation (H 142–144). The voltages are low but the currents aren't: you're at risk if you give the current a better path to earth or back to the machine than it already has. Never work in damp conditions; wear leather gloves and insulating soles; don't use a damaged machine, hose or torch; never touch the tip or wire with the machine on; clamp the return lead to the work itself, not the bench, with good contact (W 62).
- **Fire**:
  - Keep an extinguisher handy and water nearby for cooling (H 142–144); Wilson adds a fire blanket, an all-steel bench, and a clean area clear of drawings and clutter (W 62).
  - **Hot metal looks exactly like cool metal** (H 142–144). The arc can reach about 4,000°C, and the weld area stays hot a long time (W 62).
  - **Fire-watch** afterward: Hart says about 30 minutes; Wilson says inspect the area for an hour (H 142–144; W 62). Never weld and leave immediately.
- **Eyes and skin**:
  - Wear a welding helmet, shade #10 standard (range #9–13; too dark is safer than too light) (H 142–144). Wilson specifies a full-face mask with an approved 11 or 12 filter (W 62).
  - **Auto-darkening helmets** reduce neck strain; faster-switching lenses are better (H 142–144). They cost much more than a flip-down mask but make life a lot easier (W 63).
  - UV burns skin, so wear leather or heavy flame-resistant cotton, **no synthetics** (they melt), and cover all skin (H 142–144), especially forearms and neck; add a leather apron for heavy work and a skullcap against spatter if needed (W 62).
  - Wear gauntlet gloves for MIG, stick and torch work, thinner gloves for TIG.
  - Use screens or curtains for bystanders and **call "Welding!" before you strike an arc** (H 142–144). Screen the arc so no one can see it, post warning notices, and keep uninvolved people out of the immediate area (W 62).
- **Fumes**:
  - Remove paint and coatings first (H 142–144), for at least 100 mm around the weld (W 64).
  - Use a fume extractor, but don't blow away the shielding gas (H 142–144). MIG fume is mostly tiny metal particles plus burnt coatings. Dip-transfer MIG on clean mild steel should be within limits, but extract at source anyway, or wear a suitable particle mask, and keep your head out of the rising plume (W 62).
  - Wear a respirator as required by the SDS.
  - **Never weld galvanized or zinc-coated steel.** It causes metal-fume fever ("welding shivers") and is carcinogenic (H 142–144). Wilson does describe tack-welding galvanized metal lath to armature framing, with extraction or protection because zinc vaporizes (W 83); see the disagreement table below.
  - Wire and filler rod have their own SDS.
  - **Flux-cored ("no-gas") machines**, popular with hire companies, burn flux and almost certainly need local exhaust ventilation (W 63).
- **Gas cylinders**: chained upright. Never strike an arc on a cylinder (H 142–144). Fit an argon regulator with a flowmeter, and back the regulator knob off until slack at the end of each session to relieve the diaphragm spring (W 63).

### Technique (H 144–148; W 60–67)
- Melt both pieces into a **puddle**, add filler, and move steadily. **Practice on scrap and get mentored.** Test joints by abusing them harder than the actors will (H 144–148). Wilson likewise urges training from someone competent plus specialist textbooks; a book's introduction is no substitute (W 62–63).
- A good bead looks like a **"stack of dimes."** Beginners move too fast and end up with lumpy, unfused beads (H 144–148).
- **Laying a bead** (W 65): hold the torch with the shroud vertical or nearly so, its end about **10 mm** above the work, supported with both hands where you can see the joint. Squeeze the trigger and hold still a fraction of a second while a puddle builds, then move slowly and steadily at a constant height, with the arc leading the weld pool. At the end, stop moving and hold a moment before releasing the trigger.
- **Stringer** (straight) vs. **weave** beads (zigzag, semicircles, loops) for wider joints and thicker metal (H 144–148). Weaving should be regular and rhythmic, like a pendulum, whenever you need a wider weld or to control the pool against gravity (W 67).
- **Penetration / joint preparation**:
  - Under 3/16": leave a 3/32–1/8" gap (H 144–148).
  - 3/16–5/8": grind a **vee** groove (H 144–148).
  - Over ½": **double vee** (H 144–148).
  - Wilson's metric version: steel up to **2 mm** can be butted; over 2 mm, leave a small gap (allow for it when cutting parts to length) or grind about 30° on each part. The rolled edges of rectangular tube help penetration. Over **5 mm**, grind a 60° included vee and leave a small root gap (W 64).
- **Groove welds** vs. **fillet welds** (fillets for tees and corners).
- Joint types: **butt, corner, lap** (weld both sides), **tee, edge, flange** (sheet), **plug** (through a drilled hole; clean but weaker).
- Positions: **flat** (easiest, so reposition the work to weld flat whenever you can), **vertical** (build a shelf from the bottom up), **horizontal** (heat the lower piece first), **overhead** (sparks fall on you) (H 144–148). For **fillet welds** with one surface flat and one vertical, tilt the torch to 45° to bisect the angle and check the weld fuses equally to both sides; with both surfaces vertical, point the torch horizontally or slightly up. Small vertical welds can go up or down; Wilson makes **larger vertical fillets from the top, weaving downward** so molten metal doesn't run down the joint (W 67). See the disagreement table.
- **Clean metal** with a grinder, sander or wire brush. The ground clamp must touch bare metal (H 144–148). New steel free of rust and grease needs no further prep, but grind or sand it for critical work (W 64).
- **Tack welds** (W 67): aim at the joint, squeeze the trigger, and as the arc starts move it to one side of the joint and then the other before releasing. A good tack is a small bridge well fused to both sides.
- **Control distortion** (welds shrink as they cool and pull angles closed):
  - Clamp the work, and **tack-weld every joint before welding fully** (H 144–148). Clamp parts together or to the bench, or weight them down; tack a whole structure and check its geometry before full welds (W 66).
  - Wilson's shortcut: in one fluent action, put a couple of tacks on one face of a joint and a full weld along the opposite face. Once the tacks are reinforced, a big structure needn't be turned over, and the tacked face stays clear for cladding with ply (W 66).
  - Draw patterns on the welding table and staple down **jig blocks** (scraps of ¾" ply) (H 144–148).
  - Let welds cool between passes and counter-weld the opposite side (H 144–148). Extra welds that balance the distribution round a tube can pull distortion back, and can be ground off later (W 66).
  - Lay a root pass, then fill passes (H 144–148).

### Processes (H 149–154)

| Process | AWS name | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **MIG** | GMAW | The props workhorse for steel. Wire-fed electrode with shielding gas (75/25 argon/CO₂ is common; pure argon for aluminum). Wire 0.023–0.030". Small 115 V machines exist; 220 V machines handle more. Voltage controls penetration. A good weld **sounds like sizzling bacon**. Keep ¼–⅜" wire stickout. Point the gun 45° to the joint and tilt 10–15° to push (H 149–154). Wilson: the easiest process to learn, more delicate than stick, with fewer fumes and less UV. Also called MAG; machines are rated by maximum amps, and about **140 A with 0.8 mm wire** covers most prop work (180 A for heavy use). Gas: argon with **5–25% CO₂** and a little oxygen (less CO₂ for thinner steel), from disposable canisters or a hired bottle, at **10–15 L/min**. In **dip transfer** (used for props) the wire touches the work, fuses like a fuse wire and re-strikes about 100 times a second: low heat, so little distortion and vertical or overhead work is possible, but fusion problems are the typical fault. Voltage is set in steps for the steel's thickness, then wire speed is adjusted for a stable arc: too slow and the wire burns back to the tip; too fast and it **stubs** against the work. Start with the speed too high and come down. As a rough guide, 1.5 mm steel wants about halfway up the voltage range on a 120–140 A machine and about a third on a 180 A one (W 60–65) |
| **Flux-core** | FCAW | Self-shielding wire with no gas tank. Works outdoors in wind; less pretty, but strong |
| **TIG** | GTAW | Tungsten electrode with hand-fed filler and argon gas (**never argon/CO₂**). Preferred for **aluminum** (needs AC) and **stainless**. Precise, clean, slow and skill-intensive. Filler rods: ER70S-2 for mild steel, ER308 for stainless, ER4043 for aluminum, ERCuSi-A for copper alloys. Keep separate tungstens and brushes for aluminum |
| **Stick** | SMAW | Formerly the cheap option. Chip the **slag** after each pass. Arc length ≤ electrode diameter; scratch start. E6013 is a good learning rod |
| **Oxy-acetylene** | OAW | Red hose acetylene, green oxygen. Light with a **flint striker**. Adjust from **carbonizing** to **neutral** (for welding) to **oxidizing** (for cutting). **Shut down oxygen first**, then acetylene, and bleed the lines at day's end. Also heats metal for bending and cuts with a cutting tip |

**Troubleshooting** (H 154; W 65–66):
- **Porosity**: arc too long, or dirty metal (H 154).
- **Spatter**: settings too high (H 154). A torch held too far away also gives a noisy, unstable, spattery arc and poor fusion (W 66).
- **Incomplete fusion**: weld sitting on top of the metal "like glue" because the heat was too low, the metal dirty, or you moved too fast (H 154). Moving too fast leaves a thin, round bead lying on the surface; moving too slowly gives a big bead with poor fusion, because the arc is aimed at the pool rather than the work (W 65).
- **Burn-through**: too hot or too slow (H 154). Current too high also gives a very wide, flat weld (W 66).
- **Torch too close** (MIG): a small, quiet arc that may go out, wire burning back to the tip, and the shroud sticking to the weld (W 66).
- Also check that the gas is on and the tank isn't empty (H 154).

Welding isn't always the best joint. **Rivets** often work better on sheet metal (H 154).

## Soldering and brazing (H 154–157)

- The filler melts and the base metal doesn't. **Soldering** is below 800°F; **brazing** is above 800°F. Brazing can join dissimilar metals.
- **Prefer lead-free solder.** Never use leaded solder on items that touch food or drink or get handled often. All solder fumes need extraction (H 155).
- **Flux** removes oxides and helps flow. Use the least aggressive flux that works, and clean off active fluxes afterward because they corrode.
- Joints must **fit tightly**, be clamped or wired, and be **perfectly clean** (fine steel wool).
- **Heat the work, not the solder.** Capillary action draws solder into a hot joint. Don't move it until it's solid (H 155–156).
- Torch soldering and brazing; **sweat soldering**, where both sides are pre-tinned (H 157).

## Finishing (H 157)

- **Dull every edge and corner.** Check carefully, because a razor-sharp edge cuts before you feel it.
- Degrease and **prime immediately**, because rust starts right away.
- **Polish** by bringing the surface to a uniform matte first, then buff with a cloth wheel and compound.

## Alloys and buying (H 157–160)

- **Mild steel** (AISI 1018/1020) covers most prop work. **1045** is stronger, for shafts. **5160** is **spring steel**, used for stage-combat blades. **Merchant bar** is the cheapest grade, fine for decorative work. **Hot-rolled** is cheaper; **cold-rolled** is more precise.
- New steel often comes with mill scale and grease. Wash it on sawhorses when it's delivered (wear gloves with cleaners).
- **Pre-primed weldable steel** saves labor. **Galvanized**: don't weld it.
- **Stainless**: 304 is most common, 316 is more corrosion-resistant, 410 is dull.
- **Aluminum**: **6061-T6** is the shop standard; 6063 for square tube; 1100 for sheet; **7075 ("aircraft")** looks like steel on film and is used for screen combat blades, but dents too easily for a stage run.
- Old circular saw blades are good tool steel for an axe that must actually split something nightly (H 159).

## For the classroom

- **Project-based welding.** Hart recommends learning welding by building a small object rather than running practice beads, so students learn "all the things you don't know you don't know" (H 9). Try a small stool, a candelabra or a wall hook.
- **Sheet-metal pattern.** Students pattern a gauntlet or footlight in cardstock, then transfer it to aluminum and fold it.
- **Weld inspection.** Students cut cross-sections through classmates' (or sample) welds and identify the defects using Hart's troubleshooting list.
- **Spring-back trials.** Students bend 6 mm round bar cold round a ply former, measure the spring-back, recut the former, and record how many tries it takes to hit a full-size semicircle drawing (W 56–59).
- **Price log.** Students phone or email two steel suppliers for quotes on three sections and start the shop's price record Wilson recommends (W 51).
