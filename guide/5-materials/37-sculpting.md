# 37. Sculpting and Sculptural Materials

> **Sources:** H ch.13 (pp. 238–254); M ch.5 (pp. 53–54), ch.10 (pp. 143–144); W ch.4 (pp. 69–84), ch.7 (pp. 125–136)
> **Companion:** [Sculpting Projects](37-sculpting-projects.md): soap carving, an oil-clay maquette, slice-method foam, hard-over-soft detail, salt dough and polymer clay, a clay helmet with pressed texture, Wilson's oil clay, modelling plaster and paper pulp recipes, reclaiming clay, a band-sawn polystyrene rough.
> **[Check current]** Product names (Chavant, Sculpey, Friendly Plastic, Foamcoat, etc.) and formulations change.

Unlike fine-art sculpture, which prizes "truth to materials," prop sculpture regularly carves one material and finishes it to look like another. All the other needs still apply — weight, cost, labor, rigidity (H 239).

## Carving vs. modeling (H 239–240)

- **Carving (subtractive):** wood, metal, rigid foams. Added material stays a distinct glued piece.
- **Modeling (additive):** clay, wax — new material merges with old. Must then be made permanent: fired (clay), cooled (thermoplastics), air-dried, or chemically cured (epoxy putty).
- Some models are **masters for molding** ([ch. 38](38-molding-and-casting.md)) or **maquettes** (small-scale 3D references) (H 239–240). Wilson splits modelling materials the same way: **temporary** ones (clay, oil clay, wax) only make originals for molding and are chosen for working properties, unhurried time and recyclability; **permanent** ones dry, set or cure tough enough for the stage, perhaps lightweight or fire-retardant, and can be modelled directly or used for details on larger work (W 69, 75). The ideal modelling material is truly plastic: easily shaped, staying where it's put without springing back, sagging or sticking to tools, a combination that clay has and few others match (W 69).
- **Machining** shares tools with carving but aims for precise, fitting parts (H 239).

**Do you actually need to sculpt?** Sometimes applying a material with inherent texture (a ridged fabric) beats carving; in very low-budget theatre even painted-on "3D" detail can meet the need (H 240).

**Talent is less important than you think.** Wilson argues that natural gift helps, but two things matter more: **frequent practice**, as in music, and a **clear picture** of what you're making, in your head and preferably on paper in front of you. Work from references and "leave sculptural improvisation to those without deadlines to meet or designers to please" (W 69). The same goes for carving: most carved sculpture is carefully scaled up from a clay or wax model, not chiselled spontaneously (W 136).

## The sculpting process (H 240–243)

1. **Reference.** Front, side, and top views at minimum (draw missing ones); many photos from all sides; real-world references even for fantasy creatures. Use a long lens, subject centered, to limit distortion (H 241). For a large carving you may get a designer's model, photos of pose and carving style, and a sketch of one detail: agree why each reference is there, and if information is missing, ask the designer or find it yourself (W 132–134).
2. **Make the reference usable:** grid-enlarge or print it full scale. Use it to compare by eye and calipers, to cut a **profile template** (a cut-out silhouette passed over the sculpture), or to build up/carve directly against (H 241).
3. **Rough the overall shape and proportions first** — don't detail an ear until both ears line up (H 242). Look at the work from different angles and from a distance, get a fresh pair of eyes to critique it, and use scaled measurements where you can (W 136).
4. **Refine with progressively gentler tools.**
5. **Detail and surface last.**

### Subtractive techniques (H 242–243; W 132–136)
- **Don't be timid with the first cut.** If it goes wrong, glue the piece back or add a new blank piece (W 132).
- Draw outer profiles on three faces; cut one profile on the band saw, **tape or pin the offcuts back on** to restore a flat face, cut the next profile (H 242–243; W 134). Wilson's order: choose the view with the most detail or "landmarks" (draw it if the references lack it, calculating a custom scale if needed), then a second view at 90° registered to the first. Cut each square through the block with a band saw, fixed hot wire or handsaw. Those two profiles achieve a lot; heads benefit from cutting a third (top) view too (W 134). If the block is too big to saw that way up, cut it in half, profile each half and glue them back; in a block glued up from several sheets, give each layer its own outline before gluing (W 134).
- **Kit-form roughs.** For something like two life-size figures entwined in a dance or fight, cut heads, torsos and limbs separately from sheets of suitable thickness (drawing round helpful colleagues), and assemble them, pinning parts temporarily with knitting needles or barbecue skewers while you judge the relationships. It's quicker and far less wasteful than carving one block (W 134–135).
- **After roughing:** mark the biggest remaining differences from the reference with a marker. Band-sawn roughs first need features that were "overshadowed" by others; then the cross-sections, which are still rectangular, must be rounded (W 135).
- **Rounding a square section** (W 135–136): beginners only nibble the corners and never escape the rectangle. Be bold: cut the square to an **octagon** (for a true octagon, the first 45° cuts take off about 30% of each face's width from each corner), then to 16 sides, then finish with sandpaper. For legs or trees, work out key cross-sections and how they change; try ideas on scrap foam or clay. Use the knife or brush as if drawing.
- Big blocks: handsaws, or a **hot-wire cutter** for polystyrene (long two-person bows exist) (H 242–243). Polystyrene cutting tools are covered in [ch. 32](32-foams-and-rubbers.md).
- Dry-assemble glued-up blocks, draw, separate, rough-cut, then glue.
- **Slice method:** cut front and side profiles from plywood, add top-profile "slices" on a frame, fill between them with pre-roughed foam, and carve flush to the slices — "connect the dots." Hart's three-foot garden gnome for *Broke-ology* was built this way (H 243–244).

### Additive techniques (H 243–245; W 69–83)
- Build up from small balls of material rather than one giant lump (H 243–245).
- **Modelling is almost always supported**: by an armature, a ply board (for a mask or other flat-backed piece), or even half a dozen nails part-driven into a chair back to give modelled detail a purchase (W 69). Paint or varnish wooden armatures, and plaster life casts, so clay will stick (W 72, 81).
- **Armatures** for delicate forms — armature wire (spiral-wrapped with fine wire so clay grips), wood, pipe, rod; joined with epoxy putty, welding, or lashing ([ch. 26](../4-fabrication-core/26-structure-and-armatures.md)).
- Bulk out fast with crumpled **foil** or paper bound with tape or wire (H 243–245). Scrumpled foil bound or skewered with wire is fireproof and absorbs some shrinkage; paper balls taped to wood or wire suit paper laminates and pulp; roughly carved polystyrene suits every material **except polyester putty and oven-baked materials** (W 81). Full armature guidance is in [ch. 26](../4-fabrication-core/26-structure-and-armatures.md).
- **Open vs. skinned armatures:** an open, skeletal armature is quick and fine for an original you'll mold (the extra clay doesn't matter); skinned armatures and cores take longer but save weight, which matters when modelling directly for the stage (W 81).

### Hard + soft strategies (H 245–246)
- Rough form in a **hard** material, details in a **soft** one (a wood bird head with self-hardening putty details).
- Or bulk in **soft, fast** material and skin with **hard** material for crisp carving (Bondo over XPS foam, carved when hard).
- Cure/fire in **stages** so handling doesn't wreck finished areas.
- **Waste-mold and recast** a soft sculpt in a hard material to "freeze" progress, carve crisp details, and polish smoother than clay allows.
- Mix materials freely — a "cast-iron" bench ornament built from resin castings, upholstery fringe, Ethafoam, and yarn (H 246).

## Materials

| Material | Character | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Oil clay** (plastilina; "Chavant," "Plasteline," "Plasticine") | Never dries; consistent for days; hardness from bubblegum to wood-like; heat to soften | For masters, not final props. Smooth with mineral spirits. **Sulfur-based clays inhibit silicone** — use **sulfur-free** (assume sulfur unless labeled; many shops ban sulfur clay) (H 249–250). Wilson: dry clay combined with oil and molten wax; ranges from soft Plasticine to industrial styling clays plastic at 50–60°C but hard enough to drill or machine at room temperature. Less plastic than natural clay but cleaner and more controlled. Soften it right through before starting (radiator, drying cupboard, low oven); long gentle heat (hair dryer, fan heater) softens deep, short fierce heat (heat gun) only the surface; let work cool or even freeze it before carving detail. Lubricate with talc, oil, petroleum jelly or soap in moderation; lighter fuel works as solvent, bonding agent and tool cleaner. Sulphur clays also inhibit some **urethane** rubbers. Cheap to make in bulk ([recipe](37-sculpting-projects.md#recipe-cards)) (W 74–75) |
| **Water/ceramic clays** | Kiln-fired 1200–2300°F | Rare in props except **breakable** items (air-dried or low-fired); air-dry clays look similar without a kiln (H 250). Wilson treats unfired natural clay as a vital **intermediary** for originals: cheap, reusable, unbeatable at hand scale and the only affordable choice for large work (which needs heavy steel and timber armatures), but it dries, shrinks and cracks, so it never goes onstage, and it's poor for very small pieces or fine detail (oil clay is better) (W 69–70). Buy in sealed 25 kg bags; cut with stretched fishing line; **talc** is the best lubricant (dry, temporary), grease also works, water makes it muddy. Cover work with damp muslin and polythene over breaks, return waste to the bag, and add disinfectant against mould. Let a layer firm up to stop sagging or to survive molding. Shellac seals clay for finer surfaces (W 70–73). Recycling: [projects](37-sculpting-projects.md#reclaiming-clay-w-7374) |
| **Polymer clay** (Sculpey, Fimo…) | PVC-based, oven-cured | Small items, jewelry, details (martini olives). **Dedicated oven — never food again** (H 250). Wilson (W 76): condition by warming and kneading; build in even layers over a foil core; build bulk in stages, baking each. Follow the maker's schedule, usually **15–30 min per 6 mm of thickness at 130°C, never hotter**; check the oven in several places with a thermometer. Some contain **phthalate plasticizers**: don't eat or drink while handling, and don't use it for food or drink props, pipes or cigarette holders. Your oven size limits the work |
| **Air-drying clay** | Natural clay with fibre (often nylon) to reduce cracking | Some harden further with an additive, a painted liquid or baking. Work stays delicate and is slightly less plastic; best when economy matters most (W 76) |
| **Epoxy putty** (e.g. Milliput) | Two sticks mixed equally; very hard and strong in a few hours | Repairs and small objects. Warm it (radiator, hair dryer) to soften and speed the set; chill it to firm it and extend working time considerably; or let it part-harden first. Water lubricates without spoiling adhesion (W 76). Modelling it onto turned wood made an elaborate candlestick (W 77). See also ch. 38 (H 282) |
| **Modelling plaster** | Plaster slowed with a retarder and thickened | Reasonably strong, easy, economical at large scale. Retard with trisodium citrate, vinegar or lemon juice. Thicken by using **alpha gypsum** (needs less water, so mixes thicker and sags less), **colloidal silica** (as little as 3% of the dry plaster's weight), damp paper pulp (stronger but less detail) or damp sawdust/polystyrene dust (texture). Proprietary thixotropes for polymer plasters don't thicken enough and are hazardous on bare hands. Also coats polystyrene ([recipe](37-sculpting-projects.md#recipe-cards)) (W 76–78). A breakaway clock original was built from plastic mouldings and modelled plaster on wood (W 77) |
| **Paper pulp** | Pulp plus paste or glue; light and very strong when dry | Traditional; many recipes (whiting, sawdust, linseed oil). Drawbacks: **weeks** to dry at large scale (keep the modelled layer thin over a close-fitting core covered first in paper and glue; add a biocide against mould) and **flocculation** into clumps, which a starch binder such as wallpaper paste plus a filler powder minimizes. Fill small drying cracks. Buy dry pulp in sacks (paperclay, or cellulose insulation, which comes fire-retardant) or make it ([recipe](37-sculpting-projects.md#recipe-cards)) (W 78–79) |
| **Fire cement** | Hearth repair paste, hardened by baking; fire-resistant to about 1,250°C | A prop made from it can hold a real fire or sit in one (a bull modelled over a foil shell survived a real fire). Gloves or barrier cream; thin layers on expanded metal or foil, baking each hard. Bake about 30 min per cm at about 100°C, then 30–60 min at 250°C (hotter in a kiln). Colour with pigment in thinned cement (test: some colours change when heated); fire metal powders on in a water/cement paste and polish with wire wool (W 79–80). See [ch. 48](../7-effects/48-flame-smoke-and-sound.md) for flame safety |
| **Polyester (body) filler** | Heavily filled polyester with a tube of catalyst | Hard to model but sets fast and carves and sands soon after: good for quick repairs. Use slightly less catalyst than recommended unless you work fast. Make your own from laminating resin, 0.5–1% MEKP and a cheap filler (W 80–81) |
| **Baker's clay / salt dough** | Flour, salt, water | **Nontoxic**; great for faux baked goods. Air-dry 2–3 days or bake low (150–350°F; browning = too hot); seal with shellac, varnish, or latex paint (H 250–251). *[Check: Hart's printed ratio appears garbled — test a recipe first.]* |
| **PCL** (Friendly Plastic, InstaMorph, Polymorph) | Softens in boiling water (~140°F), hand-shapeable | Jewelry to mask scale; machining heats and deforms it (H 251). Wilson finds it springy, so it won't take fine detail, but fast for forming and altering basic shapes, tough, and reheatable; it can be modelled over clay (a wicker-like donkey's head) and was used to fix the parts of a moose head together (W 12, 81, 120) |
| **Wax** (jeweler's carving wax, microcrystalline, etc.) | Carves, sands, and drills finely; heat-smoothable | Close-up film details; masters for casting (H 251–252). Wilson: soft waxes model, hard ones carve, machine or turn; most mold materials release from wax without release agent; wax can be cast into molds for shapes and details, or melted out of plaster waste molds (lost wax). Melt it, then let it cool until it just sets, when it's most malleable; keep a pan of molten wax on very low heat, pour puddles onto a cool surface and scrape them up as they set; or paint molten wax onto a core in layers (thickest build just before it sets). Heated tools model detail; a flame played over the surface blends details and removes tool marks. **Molten wax is flammable**: melt it in a water bath, never over direct heat. Pure waxes are harder to work than clay, so use them where their properties matter (W 75) |
| **XPS foam** (blue/pink "Styrofoam") | Light, fast to carve, glue seams vanish | Denser, stronger and much finer than EPS, so it takes more detail (W 125). Dissolved by most solvents and spray paints; degraded by heat. **Fragile — must be coated** ([ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md#coating-foam-h-315317-w-136141)). Glue with **water-based contact cement** ("green glue"); PU glue for foam-to-wood (hard glue line resists carving); two-part foaming PU as a carvable foam-to-foam glue — clamp it, it expands (H 252–253) |
| **EPS bead foam** | Huge blocks for big props; free packaging foam is less dense | (H 253). Carving it avoids lengthy casting and keeps big forms light, but it's too weak for the stage without a coating (W 125). Grades, tools and glues are in [ch. 32](32-foams-and-rubbers.md) |
| *"Styrofoam" trap* | The trademark also covers brittle **floral/craft** foam | Floral foam was carved into fake toast and sealed with yellow PVA (H 253) |
| **Rigid urethane foam** | Denser, finer detail than polystyrene | Blocks from taxidermy suppliers, or cast your own from leftover A-B foam (H 253–254) |
| **Spray foam** | Adds carvable bulk to irregular armatures (foam sprayed on a real skeleton became a corpse in *The Bacchae*) | Air-cures — apply in thin layers or the center never cures. **Isocyanates** off-gas until fully cured; no odor warning; dust masks useless; use local exhaust (H 254) |
| **Basswood** | Fine, controllable grain | Ideal for learning wood carving; balsa is too mushy (H 254) |

### Polystyrene safety (H 243; M 143–144; W 131, 136)
Hot-wiring or hot-knifing polystyrene releases **styrene** and related vapors — eye and respiratory irritant, nervous-system effects (dizziness means inadequate ventilation), IARC "possibly carcinogenic." Carving and rasping make dust. Use ventilation (local exhaust for big hot-wire rigs), an organic-vapor respirator per the SDS for hot cutting, and a particulate mask and goggles for rasping (H 243; M 143–144). Wilson adds: use the lowest wire temperature that cuts and **never let the wire glow**; switch off at the end of each cut and pick off the bits of foam that would burn on the wire; treat a respirator as a last resort, not a substitute for ventilation; polystyrene dust is light, clings by static and settles slowly, so vacuum often. **Carving knives** cause serious injuries: cut away from your body and other hand, keep spare knives in a tray, and don't carve when tired, ill or distracted (W 131, 136).

For food: Model Magic, Creative Paperclay, salt dough (baker's clay) and moldable thermoplastic pellets as fake produce, fish, crusts and butter, see [ch. 41](41-prop-food.md#modeling-compounds-ff-8-12-43-149) (FF).

**Foam clay** (EVA mixed with air-dry clay) sculpts horns, spikes, skulls and details onto EVA props and fills seams. It expands slightly as it dries unless heat-set straight after sculpting (KF 27; [ch. 33](33-eva-foam.md#foam-clay-kf-6-27-28-39-47)).

## For the classroom

- **Soap carving warm-up:** Hart's bar-of-soap polar bear (H 9) teaches subtractive thinking in an hour.
- **Slice-method sculpture:** Students build a 2–3 ft foam figure using plywood profiles and slices, then coat it ([ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md)).
- **Oil-clay master:** Students sculpt a relief panel or small object in sulfur-free clay as the master for their molding-and-casting project ([ch. 38](38-molding-and-casting.md)).
- **Profile carving.** From a photo of an animal, students draw a side and a top view, band-saw them from extruded polystyrene with the offcuts taped back between cuts, then round the sections boldly via octagon and 16-gon (W 133–136).
- **Temporary vs. permanent.** Students model the same small relief in oil clay, epoxy putty and modelling plaster, then compare working time, detail, cure and what each would be used for (W 69–78).
- **Food sculpture:** Students make a convincing plate of fake food using baker's clay, polymer clay, foam, and paint — and explain their material choices.
