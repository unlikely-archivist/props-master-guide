# 33. EVA Foam Props

> **Sources:** KF (whole book, pp. 2–47). Especially pp. 4–9 (materials, tools and safety), 10–13 (blueprints and scale), 14–19 (basic techniques, PVC cores, magnet joints), 24–29 (textures, foam clay, filling and priming). Also H 53–55, 163–167, 172, 179–181, 236–237; FF 15.
> **Companion:** [EVA Foam Build Walkthroughs](33-eva-foam-projects.md) condenses KF's five worked builds (sword, shield, claws, spear, short swords and scabbards) with materials, sequence and timings.
> **[Check current]** KF was self-published in 2019 and names specific products: Flexbond, Flexipaint, Hexflex, PlastiDip, GummiDip, Kwikseal, Flexifiller, Foam Werks cutters, Liquitex, Vallejo Model Air and Game Air, Schmincke PrimAcryl, Lukas varnish and Frog Tape. It also gives one price (a rotary tool for about $30 / €25, KF 8), links to YouTube videos and downloadable blueprints, and describes convention weapon rules in general terms. All of these change. **Check each product's current data sheet and SDS**, and each event's current rules.

Svetlana Quindt (Kamui Cosplay) wrote KF for **cosplayers**: people building a costume weapon or prop for themselves to carry at conventions, in photos and on competition stages. It's a short, photo-led book for beginners. Its argument is that **EVA foam plus a few cheap tools** can make large props that are light, affordable and convincing once primed and painted (KF 2, 4).

This chapter pulls KF's methods together by technique. The guide already covers EVA foam briefly: as a material in [ch. 32](32-foams-and-rubbers.md#rubbers-and-foams-h-179181-h-236), and as a patterned sheet in [ch. 24](../4-fabrication-core/24-patterning.md#foam-patterning-h-236237). KF goes much further, into cores, multi-part joints, carving, texturing, filling, priming and paint. It doesn't mention theatre or film. Notes on adapting its methods for a production shop are marked **[Added best practice]** near the end.

KF draws one distinction worth keeping. Armor is shaped on one person's body, but **a prop can be held by anybody**, so it calls for a different approach: you plan it from reference and drawings rather than fitting it on yourself (KF 2).

## Why EVA foam (KF 4)

- **Cheap and versatile.** KF's examples include wood-look staffs, "metal" swords, claws of scale and bone, cel-shaded wigs, feathers and wings, and full-body monster suits (KF 4).
- **Many forms.** Sheets in several densities and thicknesses, foam clay, spheres, pipes, and pre-cut dowels and bevels (KF 4).
- **Easy to find.** Even without a cosplay supplier nearby, hardware-store floor puzzle mats will do for small parts (KF 4). But see the fume warning under [Materials](#materials-kf-67), and the sheet-size note in [ch. 33 build walkthroughs](33-eva-foam-projects.md#2-hylian-shield-kf-3236).
- **Lighter and cheaper** than Worbla, leather, clay, silicone or resin. KF says a two-metre great sword can cost "only a few dollars" in foam (KF 4) **[Check current]**.

## Materials (KF 6–7)

| Material | What KF says |
|---|---|
| **EVA foam sheets** | Keep **2, 5 and 10 mm** in both **high and low density**. Stacking those three covers any thickness you need. **High density** suits sturdy, smooth props such as rifles, staffs and swords: it wobbles less, a rotary tool slips less on it, and it can be hand-sanded. It is also heavier and dearer. **Low density** suits organic shapes with a lot of heat shaping, carving and sanding (KF 6, 20, 34). **Cheap puzzle mats may release toxic fumes when heated** (KF 6). KF orders jumbo sheets of 1 × 0.5 m (KF 33) |
| **Foam clay** | Lightweight EVA mixed with air-dry modelling clay. Dries soft and semi-flexible, and can still be cut, sanded and heat-shaped. Used for sculpted details, and for filling and blending seams (KF 6, 27). See [Foam clay](#foam-clay-kf-6-27-28-39-47) |
| **Dowels and bevels** | Factory-made EVA rods, half-rounds, triangles and rectangles. KF uses round ones for gun barrels, rivets and screws. Others twist them into root-like forms. They can be heat-shaped, sanded and cut like sheet (KF 6) |
| **Cores** | Wooden poles, fiberglass rods, metal rods or PVC pipe. Look for PVC sizes that slide into each other (KF 7). See [Cores](#cores-and-pvc-pipe-kf-7-1617-21-46) |
| **Contact cement** | KF's main foam adhesive. See [Basic techniques](#basic-techniques-kf-1415). Decant it into small **HDPE squeeze bottles**: less fume and more precise. Keep **super glue** on hand too (KF 7) |
| **Fillers and primers** | Use **flexible** primers on flexible foam (KF names Flexbond and PlastiDip) and flexible gap fillers (Kwikseal, Flexifiller). Local equivalents work equally well (KF 7). See [Filling and priming](#filling-and-priming-kf-2829-36-40-47) |
| **Paint** | **Flexible paint is key.** "Stay far away from spray paints", especially on shiny blades, because most crack when the foam is pressed. KF uses acrylics: Liquitex heavy-body for brushing, Vallejo Model Air and Game Air for airbrush (KF 7) |

## Tools (KF 8–9)

- **Rotary tool** (KF uses a Dremel). "One of your most important pieces of equipment." A rubber drum with a replaceable sanding sleeve makes bevels, carves textures and ornaments, and rounds or deepens areas. Other bits drill, cut and polish. KF suggests a **flexible-shaft attachment** for control and to spare your wrist on long sanding sessions (KF 8).
- **Knives.** A box cutter (KF sharpens hers rather than replacing blades; see below), a smaller precision box cutter, an X-Acto-type hobby knife, and small **curved and straight scissors** (KF 8). KF often prefers small scissors to a blade for cutting fine 2 mm details (KF 35, 47).
- **Dedicated foam cutters** (KF names Foam Werks) cut accurate angles, bevels, holes and circles. Cut circles still need cleaning up with the rotary tool (KF 8).
- **Marking.** Metal ruler, triangle, tape measure, markers, and a **silver marker for black foam** (KF 8).
- **Heat gun.** Optional for shaping, but **you need it for heat-sealing** before priming (KF 9).
- **Soldering iron.** An old, cheap one for burning in textures (and for wiring lights) (KF 9).
- **Hot glue gun.** "Usually the best solution when something doesn't want to stick" (KF 9).
- **Vacuum hose.** Sand directly over it to catch dust at the source (KF 9).

## Safety (KF 9, 15, 16, 22, 24, 29, 36, 40)

KF repeats its safety notes throughout the book.

- **Sanding dust.** Rotary-sanding EVA throws fine particles onto skin, hair and clothes and into lungs, including those of family members and pets. Wear **respiratory protection and closed safety glasses**, plus **ear protection** for long sessions and **gloves** against slips. **Sand over a vacuum hose** (KF 9). A photo of dust-covered hands after "only a few minutes" of sanding makes the point (KF 22).
- **Burning foam.** Burning textures in with a soldering iron "creates toxic fumes". Wear respiratory protection and place a fan to blow the smoke away from you and out of a window (KF 24). On a large piece KF pulled the fumes into a spray booth (or a fan, a vacuum cleaner and an open window) while wearing a respirator (KF 40).
- **Heating PVC** releases toxic fumes. Don't burn it. Work in a well-ventilated area **or** wear a respirator (KF 16). Hart's advice on heated plastic fumes differs; see [Where the sources disagree](#where-the-sources-disagree).
- **Heated puzzle mats** may release toxic fumes (KF 6).
- **Spray rubber primers** (PlastiDip and similar) are flammable and toxic. Use a well-ventilated area or go outside, and wear a high-quality respirator and rubber gloves (KF 29). KF sprays in a home-built spray booth (KF 36).
- **Hand contact.** KF leaves the insides of hand grips **unprimed and unpainted** to keep sweaty skin away from primer, paint and varnish (KF 40).

KF doesn't say what kind of respirator to use. For NIOSH ratings, cartridge types, fit testing and the hierarchy of controls, see [ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md#respiratory-protection-h-3132-3941). **[Added best practice]** In a school or professional shop, check the SDS for each foam, primer and solvent, and follow your EHS office's respirator programme.

## From reference to blueprint (KF 10–13)

KF's builds all start from a full-size **blueprint**: a clean line drawing traced from reference, printed at real size, then cut apart into patterns (KF 10–12). KF credits the blueprint drawings to Benni, Quindt's husband, who also paints most of the builds.

**Gather reference** (KF 10–11, 23, 42, 44):
- You need **at least a clean front view**. Side and top views help.
- Sources: image search, artbooks, screenshots, 3D models, action figures, a games console's photo mode, the developer's official **cosplay reference kit**, and **filmed turnarounds** of the prop in-game (KF 10, 42).
- **Rotatable references** (3D models, action figures) catch mistakes a flat drawing hides (KF 23).
- "Time spent searching for reference is time well spent" (KF 10). KF's spear still needed rework because one screenshot was missed (KF 43–44; [ch. 33 build walkthroughs](33-eva-foam-projects.md#4-aloys-spear-kf-4244)).
- **Look for an existing blueprint first.** Another maker may already have drawn it (KF 11).

**Trace it in vector software** (KF 10–11). KF uses Inkscape (free) or Adobe Illustrator (paid subscription):
1. Place the reference on a new canvas, in the bottom layer, at about **25% opacity**, and **lock** the layer.
2. On a new layer, trace the **main shapes** with the pen tool at **2 pt**. Split complex props into parts: the sword's blade, cross-guard wings and handle were traced separately.
3. Switch to **1 pt** lines for **details**: every area to be sanded or carved, such as how far a blade bevel runs, and the edges to follow when sanding the guard.
4. Add a **side view** for thickness, lined up with the front view using guide lines. A few millimetres of error don't matter as long as the main shapes and proportions are right (KF 11).

**Remove perspective distortion.** Screenshots, photos and 3D renders can show parts tilted (a grip leaning away from the viewer). Work out the true shape from several references and draw that, not the distorted one (KF 12). Compare Hart on lens distortion in [ch. 23](../4-fabrication-core/23-drawings-measuring-and-layout.md#getting-measurements-from-photos-and-objects-h-8183).

**Print large** using your printer's option to tile across several sheets (KF 12). KF prints **two copies**: one to keep whole for reference, one to cut up (KF 20, 44).

### Choosing the size (KF 13, 38)

Fictional characters often have impossible proportions, so the right size is a judgement call (KF 13):
- **Scale to the holder's body.** A sword that looks right on a 1.74 m person will look small on someone 1.90 m tall (KF 13).
- **Scale to transport.** Foam makes a 3 m scythe easy to lift, but not easy to fit in luggage or a small car (KF 13).
- **Paper dummy test.** Print the blueprint at real size, hold it in front of a mirror, and rescale and reprint until it looks right. For the Master Sword KF guessed 90–110 cm from screenshots, printed several sizes and built at **100 cm** (KF 13, 20).
- **Photo method.** Photograph yourself, then place the reference next to your image on the computer at matching scale. This saves paper (KF 13).
- **Props too fragile to hold as paper.** For a bow, KF lay on the floor miming the draw and chose a size from overhead photos (KF 13). For the claws, paper prints were **taped to the arm**, photographed, and compared with the game character (KF 38).

These are the same moves as Hart's proportion-first approach and cardboard mock-ups ([ch. 23](../4-fabrication-core/23-drawings-measuring-and-layout.md#mock-ups-and-patterns-h-9597)).

## Basic techniques (KF 14–15)

**Cutting** (KF 14):
- Hold the blade **at 90°** to the sheet and pull it through in **one stroke**.
- Tilting the blade leaves bumps. A dull blade tears the foam. Tight curves and circles are hard.
- Clean up any cut edge with the rotary tool's sanding drum. KF always does this.
- You can cut a **45° bevel** directly, but KF prefers to sand bevels because cut ones are rarely clean enough.

**Keeping the blade sharp** (KF 8, 14). EVA, especially high density, dulls a box cutter within a few cuts. Instead of snapping or replacing the blade, KF sharpens it every few cuts on a grinding stone or **1200-grit** sandpaper, held almost flat, and gets a year from one blade. Hart's approach differs; see [Where the sources disagree](#where-the-sources-disagree).

**Sanding bevels** (KF 15):
1. Mark the area to remove.
2. Carve most of it away with the knife first. This is faster and makes less dust.
3. Finish with the sanding drum in **fast, steady passes in the same direction the tip rotates**.
4. On high-density foam, smooth remaining bumps by hand with **120-grit** paper (KF 21, 46). Low density is too soft for this (KF 21).

KF admits a small sanding drum won't give a perfectly flat bevel; a belt sander would do better (KF 21).

**Gluing with contact cement** (KF 7, 15):
1. Apply a **very thin, even** layer to **both** surfaces. Scrape off excess with a foam scrap and make sure every bit is covered.
2. Wait until it's **dry to the touch**: 1–15 minutes depending on the brand.
3. Press the parts together.
4. Sand the joint to blend the layers.

"The thinner and more even your glue application, the stronger the bond and the fewer visible seams" (KF 15). Compare the contact-cement entry in [ch. 25](../4-fabrication-core/25-adhesives-and-fasteners.md#adhesive-families). Hart's foam patterning uses hot glue instead; see [Where the sources disagree](#where-the-sources-disagree).

**First project.** Practise these techniques on a small dagger or knife before a large weapon (KF 15).

## Cores and PVC pipe (KF 7, 16–17, 21, 46)

"Almost every foam prop needs a core" (KF 7). Foam alone is wobbly, even high-density foam (KF 21).

### Blade cores (KF 21, 46)
1. Draw a centre line on each of the two blade layers.
2. Cut a **V-groove** along it: knife at 45°, one cut from each side. Do both layers. (On the short swords KF sanded the groove instead, KF 46.)
3. Coat the grooves and faces with contact cement, lay in the rod, and close the layers over it.
4. Let the rod **run past the blade** so the grip can be built onto it.

**Match the core to the length.** KF used a thin aluminium rod in a 100 cm sword and it wobbled; she says she should have used steel or fiberglass, and that bigger, longer props need more support (KF 21). The same aluminium rod was fine in short swords (KF 46). Wood, PVC and fiberglass (GFK) rods also work: anything thin enough that doesn't break easily (KF 21). Compare Hart on rigid and flexible structure in [ch. 26](../4-fabrication-core/26-structure-and-armatures.md#rigid-vs-flexible-h-190191).

To join a grip to a thin rod, KF wrapped the rod's exposed end in two halves of a round foam dowel and slid a tight PVC pipe over that, giving a solid base for the grip details (KF 22).

### Heat-bending PVC pipe (KF 16)
1. Draw the curve full-size on paper as a guide.
2. Heat the section to be bent: a long area for a broad curve, a short one for a tight angle.
3. **Keep the heat gun moving and don't stop to check.** PVC cools very fast.
4. As soon as it becomes slightly wobbly, bend it to the drawing. Reheat and retry as needed, without burning it.
5. Pull the pipe slightly lengthwise as you bend to avoid wrinkles. Heated PVC deforms easily.
6. For bends near **90°**, **fill the pipe with sand and tape the ends** so it doesn't kink.

Heated PVC gives off toxic fumes; see [Safety](#safety-kf-9-15-16-22-24-29-36-40). For general thermoplastic forming, see [ch. 31](31-plastics.md#heat-forming-h-172174).

### Wrapping pipe in foam (KF 17)
A bare PVC pipe is slippery and the wrong diameter. A foam skin adjusts the thickness, takes carved texture, and gives other foam parts something to glue to.
1. Roll a piece of foam around the pipe and mark the wrap, **slightly long**. Cut it and check the fit. 5 and 10 mm foam is tricky; thicker is very hard.
2. Coat the pipe and the foam with contact cement, including the edges that will meet.
3. Lay the pipe on a marked centre line and roll it up, squeezing the seam closed.
4. Sand the seam smooth.

Stack sheets (for example 5 mm plus 2 mm) to reach an exact barrel or hilt diameter (KF 17).

## Making props come apart (KF 18–19, 42)

Transport is "one of the biggest challenges" with big props, so KF splits them (KF 18). Its method uses **nested PVC pipes and magnets**:

1. Choose two pipes that slide together without a big gap. The larger is the prop's base. The smaller is a short hidden connector.
2. Cut the base pipe to full length, then cut it where the joint will be easy to hide. A hand saw or scroll saw will do.
3. Slide the connector into one half and **super-glue** the last few centimetres permanently.
4. Slide the other half on (not glued). Drill **at least two holes** through both layers, one front and one back.
5. Separate them. Tape over each hole in the connector from the inside, fill it with super glue and set a **neodymium magnet** in it.
6. Cover those magnets with thin tape to keep glue off them. Slide the outer half back on, set the **counter-magnets** the right way round so they **attract**, and super-glue them.
7. To separate: twist the halves sideways, then pull. They snap back together with an audible "plopp" (KF 19).

KF's magnets are round, "7 mm radius and 2 mm high", chosen to match the drill bit (KF 18). Measure your own magnets against your bit.

**Where to split.** Look for natural breaks: a sword's parry bar, an area covered in fur, or a change of material (KF 19). On the Zinogre blades the joint sits where the scaly blade meets the metal grip, covered with foam layers (KF 19). On Aloy's spear it is just under the round plates mid-staff (KF 42). Some designs simply have a visible seam, and KF says that's fine (KF 19).

**Other ways to split a prop** (KF 19): plastic screw couplings from the plumbing aisle, collapsible plastic mop handles, or wooden staffs with screw joints.

Compare the offstage-path rule in [ch. 22](../4-fabrication-core/22-the-build-method.md#size-weight-balance) and magnets as fasteners in [ch. 25](../4-fabrication-core/25-adhesives-and-fasteners.md#mechanical-fasteners-h-7377-w-2830).

## Building volume and holding curves (KF 20–23, 32–35, 43, 46)

- **Laminate to thickness.** Two layers of 10 mm high-density foam make a 20 mm blade (KF 20). The sword guard wings were two 10 mm layers and one 5 mm layer (KF 22).
- **Cut the pattern apart progressively.** Trace the outline, then cut the paper into smaller pieces to mark each bevel line and the placement of each detail (KF 21–22, 34, 47).
- **Rough with the knife, finish with the drum.** This is used on every carved part (KF 21, 22, 34, 39).
- **Build curves into the seam.** For the shield, KF cut each half's centre edge on a slight curve, not straight. Forcing the curved edges together domed the shield automatically (KF 32–33).
- **Lock a curve by laminating.** On large pieces, heat shaping alone relaxes over time. KF heat-curved two layers and glued them together so the shape set "in stone — or foam" (KF 33). She used the same trick on the spear's curved "armor" plates after heat shaping didn't hold (KF 43).
- **Hollow parts.** For a scabbard, cut out the centre of the inner layers and cap the ends with solid pieces, leaving space for the blade. Foam drags at first. Priming and painting help, and you can **widen** it by gently heating it with a PVC rod inside (KF 46).
- **Risky cuts first.** KF cut pyramid-shaped holes into a shield border (three 45° cuts, then pull out the plug) only *after* sanding the border, and admits she should have done the risky step first (KF 34).
- **Fine details** in 2 mm foam, cut with small curved or nail scissors (KF 35, 47).
- **Big spikes** from four long 5 mm triangles glued "pyramid style", left hollow (KF 39).
- **Keep checking reference while building.** Hold cut parts against the blueprint, photos of others' builds, and any 3D model or figure (KF 23). Details take most of the build time: "see the crafting process more as a creative meditation than as a race" (KF 23).

## Textures (KF 24–25, 39, 43–44)

| Texture | KF's method |
|---|---|
| **Wood grain** | Draw long, slightly wavy parallel lines with knots from reference. Carve them with a sanding drum or grinding-stone bit. Keep it irregular, because "nature is chaotic". Then heat-seal, prime and paint (KF 24). The spear staff got a 2 mm foam skin carved this way (KF 42) |
| **Burned scales** | Draw the scales, set a soldering iron to **maximum**, and melt them in. Best on **low-density** foam. Fumes are toxic (KF 24). The claws got a final 2 mm skin burned with scales (KF 40) |
| **Carved lines on high density** | A soldering iron melts high-density foam less, so you're less likely to burn through to a PVC core underneath (KF 43) |
| **Fake leather** | Crumple tinfoil, heat the foam, and press the foil into it while hot. Sandpaper, metal or 3D-printed stamps also work. **Do this last**: reheating a textured area makes it smooth itself out (KF 24) |
| **Hammered metal** | Sand small dents all over with a round-ended grinding stone, then heat-clean the lint (KF 25) |
| **Layered scales** | Cut and glue dozens of individual foam scales, overlapping. Slow, but good for monster skin (KF 25) |
| **Scratches and grown marks** | Carve scratches into each spike and claw with the rotary tool so they look naturally grown (KF 39) |
| **Basket weave** | Weave strips of 2 mm foam (KF 44) |

KF's axe example combines two textures: a wood-grain grip (foam-wrapped PVC) and a hammered-metal head. Texture also made painting easier (KF 25).

A brushed primer such as Flexbond **fills small textures**; fake-leather wrinkles can disappear under it (KF 29). Compare Hart's three sources of texture (intrinsic, added, removed) in [ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md#texture-h-311315).

## Foam clay (KF 6, 27, 28, 39, 47)

Foam clay solves shapes that are hard to pattern from flat sheet: skulls, animal heads, horns, spikes (KF 27).

- **Working it.** Soft and ready to use from the tub. Draw the shape on the base, wet the surface (this helps it stick), and shape with fingers or modelling tools (KF 27).
- **Water** dissolves it. Mixed with water it becomes a gel for filling seams. The surface dries quickly, so keep water at hand (KF 27).
- **Curing** takes a few hours to a few days depending on thickness. Let air reach every side and turn the piece every few hours (KF 27).
- **It expands slightly as it dries**, which can blur sculpted detail and enlarge the piece. To stop this, **heat it gently with a heat gun** straight after sculpting, until it looks less shiny and slightly lighter (KF 27).
- **Once dry** it is soft and semi-flexible (it still cracks if bent too far), and can be sanded, cut and heat-shaped like EVA. It isn't very robust (KF 27).
- **Uses in KF:** spikes, horns, skulls, a full demon head; filling deep holes and blending parts together (the claws' finger joints, the base of every spike) (KF 27, 28, 39); "too three-dimensional" details on the short swords (KF 47). Others press it into silicone molds for flexible casts (KF 27; [ch. 38](38-molding-and-casting.md)).

KF made over 825 spikes for the full Nergigante costume, most in foam clay (KF 39). Compare the sculpting materials table in [ch. 37](37-sculpting.md#materials).

## Heat sealing (KF 9, 23, 28, 35)

Before priming, pass a heat gun gently over the finished foam. **Heat sealing** closes the pores, smooths the surface, and melts loose fuzz left by sanding. Pick off remaining bits with tweezers or trim them with small scissors (KF 23). KF heat-seals sanded details before gluing them on, then seals the whole prop again: "never a bad idea to heat seal a little bit too much than not enough" (KF 35). **Seams can open up during heat sealing**, so check and fill afterwards (KF 28).

## Filling and priming (KF 28–29, 36, 40, 47)

Painting raw foam gives a rough, fuzzy finish. KF compares it to frosting raw cake batter (KF 28). A primer:
- smooths the surface;
- stops the foam soaking up paint like a sponge;
- adds durability and makes repairs easy (wrinkles heat out) (KF 28).

### Fill first (KF 28)
| Gap | Filler | How |
|---|---|---|
| Seams, small rough spots | **Kwikseal** (KF likens it to a water-based silicone) | Smear on with a finger, then blend with a drop of water. It shrinks a little as it dries, so give it **two or three passes**, drying between them |
| Larger, deeper holes | **Foam clay** | Mix with a little water, wet the area too, and smear it in. More water blends better but lengthens drying (hours to days). Sand with fine grit when dry |

### Primers (KF 29)
| | **Flexbond** (brush-on) | **PlastiDip** (spray-on rubber) |
|---|---|---|
| Character | Affordable, long-lasting. Dries semi-transparent and very flexible. **Smooths** rough surfaces | Faster to apply, more expensive. From the automotive trade. **Doesn't smooth** anything: sanded areas stay rough under it |
| Method | Thin with a few drops of water. Brush gently over everything with a fine flat brush | Warm the can in hot tap water. Spray evenly from all sides at about **20 cm**. You want a thick, shiny coat with tiny popping bubbles |
| Drying | **15–25 min** per coat | About **15 min** per coat |
| Coats | Three (each adds smoothness and durability) | Three (one plus two more) |
| Watch for | Fills fine textures. Brush marks and drips: lift drips with water on a fingertip. Use soft flat brushes or make-up brushes (KF 30) | Flammable and toxic. Ventilate, respirator, rubber gloves |
| Alternatives KF names | Flexipaint, Hexflex | GummiDip (KF's preference in Germany: easier and cheaper) |

**Combining them.** On large props KF sprays PlastiDip over everything for speed, then brushes Flexbond only on sanded areas so they smooth out (shield, KF 36). On the claws she did it the other way round: three coats of Flexbond on sanded areas first, then three of PlastiDip over everything (KF 40). See the build cards in [ch. 33 build walkthroughs](33-eva-foam-projects.md).

FF also lists FlexBond as a primer for foam food props (FF 15). Compare the foam-coating options in [ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md#coating-foam-h-315317-w-136141) and the primer families in [ch. 28](../4-fabrication-core/28-painting-and-finishing.md#priming-h-333334).

## Painting and sealing (KF 7, 30, 36, 40, 44, 47)

KF leaves detailed painting to Quindt's separate painting books (KF 30), but the builds show a consistent approach:

- **Only flexible paint on flexible foam.** Most spray paints and lacquers crack or wrinkle under pressure and can ruin the prop (KF 7, 30).
- **Acrylics by brush.** The Master Sword was hand-painted in acrylics (a liquid silver for the blade, heavy-body blues, purples, teals and golds) to show that simple tools are enough. It took longer than airbrushing and showed some brush strokes (KF 30).
- **Airbrush plus masking.** On the shield: silver on the raised border and ornaments, a darker gunmetal in the corners, then **masking tape** over the silver and a blue base with darker shadows. Peeling the tape left clean silver-to-blue edges. Small details (the emblem, the triangles) were brush-painted, because masking them would have been slower (KF 36).
- **Oil-paint wipe-back.** On the claws: airbrush a flat base and **seal it with spray varnish**. Smear **dark brown oil paint** over the whole surface. Oil dries slowly, so there's time to wipe most of it off with paper towels and a little turpentine. Dark stays in the scales and recesses, giving depth (KF 40). Compare Hart's shadow washes in [ch. 28](../4-fabrication-core/28-painting-and-finishing.md#overpainting-h-335339).
- **Highlights**: a few brighter lines (KF 36).
- **Seal** with a **flexible spray varnish**. KF uses a satin-gloss one. It makes the colours pop and protects the paint from water and scratches (KF 30, 36, 40).
- **Finishing additions** such as feathers, beads and cord go on after paint (KF 44).

**[Added best practice]** Solvent-soaked rags (turpentine, oil paint) are a fire hazard. Dispose of them as your shop's fire rules require ([ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md#fire-safety-h-4244-m-149151)).

## Time, effort and perfectionism (KF 22, 35–36, 38, 44, 47)

- KF's **shield** took about **6 h** to build, **2 h** to prime and **3–4 h** to paint: "a project that can be done in only a day" (KF 36).
- The **short swords** took about a day to construct (KF 47).
- The **sword guard** took longer than any other part, and several attempts (KF 22).
- KF often improvises and restarts: "most of the time I do not have even the slightest idea of what I'm doing" (KF 38).
- **Know when to stop.** KF skipped back-of-shield details nobody would see (KF 35), and left an omission found after priming: "at some point you just have to 'let it gooo'" (KF 44).
- **Share credit.** Learn from other makers online, and tag those whose tutorials helped you (KF 25).

For planning labor and turnaround on a production, see [ch. 8](../2-admin/08-budgets-and-paperwork.md).

## Convention and competition rules (KF 5)

KF's only discussion of weapon safety concerns where a prop may be carried:
- **Every convention has weapon rules**, and its weapon check decides whether a prop may come onto the floor, or even into outside areas. Approved props usually get a coloured tag. Refused ones can often be stored until you leave (KF 5).
- **Almost no events allow functional weapons**: working bows, real swords, or projectiles including arrows. Some ban **hard materials** (wood, metal, even solid plastic). Others limit **dimensions** or require props to be made inoperable (KF 5).
- **Check the rules before you start building.** Events publish them in advance. Some competitions also restrict materials or size (KF 5).
- **Crowd safety.** Large props, wings and sweeping pieces endanger other people on a crowded floor and make the day harder for the wearer (KF 5).

Theatre and film have their own, stricter weapons rules; see [ch. 42](../6-weapons/42-weapons-safety-and-policy.md#what-the-sources-say-m-147149-h-7). **[Check current]** Event rules change from year to year.

## Adapting KF's methods for a production shop

KF is written for one maker building for themselves. The points below aren't in KF. **[Added best practice]**
- **Durability over a run.** A cosplay prop is carried for a day or a weekend; a show prop may be handled eight times a week for months. Test primer and paint flexibility against the actual handling, and plan touch-up.
- **Stage combat.** A foam-wrapped rod is still a rod. Any contact use needs the fight director's approval, and core ends must be fully embedded and padded ([ch. 42](../6-weapons/42-weapons-safety-and-policy.md#what-the-sources-say-m-147149-h-7); [ch. 26](../4-fabrication-core/26-structure-and-armatures.md#rigid-vs-flexible-h-190191)).
- **Magnet joints** that pull apart easily for travel may also separate when the prop is swung. For an onstage prop that gets swung, use a positive lock or a permanent joint, and keep the split for load-in only.
- **Flame retardancy.** Check EVA foam, foam clay and coatings with your fire marshal and the SDS before they go in front of an audience ([ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md#flame-retardancy-h-320-w-1718)).
- **Reference.** KF copies existing video-game props. In production, the designer's drawings replace the game reference, but KF's tracing, scale-testing and pattern-cutting workflow carries over directly ([ch. 6](../2-admin/06-script-breakdown-and-prop-list.md); [ch. 23](../4-fabrication-core/23-drawings-measuring-and-layout.md)).

## Where the sources disagree

| Topic | KF says | Other source says |
|---|---|---|
| Main adhesive for EVA foam | Contact cement is "the most reliable glue for all things foam" and the best choice for a strong bond. Hot glue is for when something won't stick (KF 7, 9, 15) | EVA foam is patterned like paper and joined with hot glue; hot glue bonds it because it's the same polymer (H 179–181, 236–237) |
| Keeping a knife sharp for foam | Sharpen a box-cutter blade on a stone or 1200-grit paper every few cuts instead of replacing it (KF 8, 14) | A snap knife gives a long fresh edge for foam; extend it fully and add a drop of sewing-machine oil (H 53–55, 181) |
| Protection when heating PVC | Work in a well-ventilated area **or** wear a respirator (KF 16) | Heated thermoplastic fumes are a mix no respirator cartridge is rated for. Use minimum heat and extract the fumes (H 172). PVC fumes are very hazardous when heated (H 163–167) |

## For the classroom

- **Blueprint from a bad reference.** Give students a distorted screenshot or a photo of an object taken at an angle. Each traces a clean front and side view in Inkscape, correcting the perspective, then prints it tiled at full size and defends the scale with a paper-dummy photo ([ch. 23](../4-fabrication-core/23-drawings-measuring-and-layout.md)).
- **Bevel and blade drill.** Each student makes a 30 cm practice dagger (KF 15): two laminated layers around a rod core, a sanded bevel, hand-sanding, heat-sealing. Compare high- and low-density versions for wobble and finish.
- **Texture sampler.** On one 2 mm and one 10 mm sheet, students make wood grain, burned scales, foil leather, hammered metal and basket weave. Then they brush-prime half of each panel with three coats of flexible primer and note which textures survive.
- **Primer shoot-out.** Prime identical sanded foam strips with a brush-on flexible primer, a spray rubber primer and a combination. Paint them all the same way, then bend, scratch and flex-test them. Record time, cost and smoothness. Tie to [ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md#coating-foam-h-315317-w-136141).
- **Travel joint.** Teams take a 1.8 m PVC-cored staff and design a joint so it fits a given car boot or airline limit. One team uses KF's magnet joint (KF 18–19), another a plumbing screw coupling, a third a design of their own. Swing-test all three and discuss which would be safe onstage.
- **Adhesive comparison.** Bond EVA to EVA with contact cement and with hot glue, then peel-test and flex-test the joints. Use the results to discuss the sources' different recommendations, without declaring a winner.
