# 36. Shells and Laminates

> **Sources:** H ch.15 (pp. 291–306)
> **[Check current]** Thermoplastic sheet brands (Wonderflex, Fosshape, Varaform, Formfast) and water-based resin products change; check availability and current data sheets.

A **shell** gets its shape from something else, such as a form, a mold or a skeleton. It's made by wrapping, covering or laminating material onto or into that shape (H 292). There are two families:

1. **Flexible sheets made rigid**: activated by water or heat, then hardened in place.
2. **Composites (laminates)**: a fibrous reinforcement embedded in a hardened polymer matrix, built up in overlapping layers into a seamless skin.

## Water- and heat-activated sheets

| Material | How it works | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Buckram** (millinery) | Starch-stiffened fabric. Soak in warm water, drape and press into the form, let dry stiff | Nontoxic and re-wettable. Sew **millinery wire** into the edges of large shapes. Stiffen further with more layers or PVA. Parts of the Tin Man costume in the 1939 *Wizard of Oz* were buckram (H 292) |
| **Fosshape** | Heat-activated nonwoven (about 100–130°F; steamer or heat gun). More heat makes it stiffer | **Shrinks a lot**, so cut patterns oversize. Can be pinned over forms, or sewn and also sewn to other fabrics as a hidden stiffener (H 292–293) |
| **Vegetable-tanned leather** | Soak 15 min to 12 hr, drape over a positive form, pin in trim areas (steel pins stain), leave slack for valleys, work with a **sticketta** (rounded wooden tool) for 20–60 min until it holds. Hammer details while drying, then burnish | Dries shoe-stiff. **Breathes**, which makes it comfortable for masks and armor. **Cuir bouilli**: dip briefly (≤2 min) in boiling water, or bake low (120–175°F), for harder, more brittle leather. Can be pressed between two forms (H 293) |
| **Industrial (pressed) felt** (≥50% wool) | Soak in PVA (diluted up to 1 part water to 2 parts glue), wring out, mold like wet leather, pin, dry for days (it shrinks and stiffens). Finish with PVA, shellac or varnish. Brush on resin instead of PVA for speed | The traditional fake for cast iron and armor; breastplates; trunk hardware. Wool fibers spread the glue evenly; synthetic felt won't work (H 293–295) |
| **Celastic → Formfast** | Rigid sheet softened in MEK or acetone, draped, self-adhering, dries hard | Unmatched drape and heat resistance (the coachmen statues from *Into the Woods* stayed in use for 25 years). **Very heavy solvent exposure**: long chemical gloves, ventilation, respirator, booth or outdoors, no unprotected bystanders (H 295) |
| **Thermoplastic sheets**: Wonderflex, Fosshape, Varaform (ex-Hexcelite) | Soften at about 150–170°F (hot water, heat gun, oven). Shape freehand, over a form or in a mold. **They self-adhere when hot**, so you can laminate 2–3 layers for stiffness. Cold water or ice packs freeze the shape | Masks, mascot heads, armor, giant puppets, all lighter than fiberglass. Wear thin leather gloves against burns. Foil or lecithin works as a release. Fill seams with flexible fillers (acrylic caulk, flexible coatings) and avoid rigid topcoats. Fine under stage lights; softens in a hot car (H 295–297) |

## Composites

Papier-mâché, cloth-mâché, fiberglass and carbon fiber all follow one idea: **overlapping strips of reinforcement saturated with a liquid polymer harden into a continuous skin** (H 297). Like casts, they need releases ([ch. 38](38-molding-and-casting.md#release-agents-h-265267)). They shrink slightly as they dry, so they release themselves from undercut-free negative molds. On positive forms, pop them loose while still green and set them back on to finish curing.

### Papier-mâché (H 297–301)

Papier-mâché has been a mainstay for centuries: most props before about 1950 were papier-mâché, wood and fabric, and some of the birds in Hitchcock's *The Birds* were mâché. Build it over chicken wire, balloons or crumpled foil, or lay it up inside molds. It also works as a **coating** that gives a uniform, paintable surface.

**Binders:**
- **Wheat paste**: about 2 parts water to 1 part gluten (all-purpose) flour. Whisk part of the water with the flour, boil the rest, stir in the slurry, boil 3–5 min and cool. Refrigerated, it keeps 2–3 days.
- **Wallpaper paste**: buy craft versions, because traditional pastes contained rat poison and some modern ones contain pesticides.
- **PVA white glue**: strong and flexible; thin with water. **Carpenter's glue** is stiffer and sands better. **Mod Podge**.
- Acrylic media and gesso, polyurethane floor finishes, theatrical emulsions (Sculpt or Coat, Crystal Gel, Flex Glue).

**Papers:**
- **Newsprint** is the classic. **Tear along the grain** for straight strips. **Tear rather than cut**, so the ragged edges blend, and tear off factory edges.
- **Kraft paper** for interior strength.
- **Tissue** for smooth outer layers, or for translucent props lit from inside.
- **Paper pulp** (blended paper plus binder) is a sculptable clay for features and a final skim coat.
- Use **unglazed** paper; coated office paper won't absorb. Newsprint ink shows through unless you paint over it.

**Technique:**
- **Alternate the direction of each layer**, as plywood does.
- **In a mold**, reverse the order: start with a **water coat** of fine paper soaked in water only, which acts as the release, in small pieces with minimal overlap (or a pulp beauty coat over a release). Then add fine paper, then newsprint or kraft for strength.
- **Two-part molds**: lay up each half, trim to the parting line, demold, join with hot glue or tape, then bridge the seam with strips that cross it.
- Embed **stiff wire** between layers for structure.
- **Instant papier-mâché** powders: soak for hours or overnight (keeps about a week refrigerated), mix in a sealed bag to avoid dust, apply in thin layers.

**Drying:**
- Use as little water and as thin a build as strength allows.
- Air-dry until merely damp, then use low oven heat (100–150°F) or heat lamps. Drying too fast too early cracks it. Fans help.
- **Waterproof** with shellac, lacquer, linseed oil, or acrylic or polyurethane emulsions.

A well-sealed mâché prop can **outlast fiberglass** because it flexes under impact instead of shattering (H 299). Hart's example: a papier-mâché bathtub with an Ethafoam rim, finished to look like hammered copper (H 300).

### Cloth-mâché (H 301)
Muslin or cheesecloth in glue. With a flexible binder it has more give than paper, which is good for impacts. Cut strips **on the bias** for strength and alternate layer directions. Cheesecloth makes a thin coat that keeps detail ([ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md)).

### Fiberglass (GRP) (H 301–306)

Glass fibers are flawless, strong and flexible. They don't rot or rust, they're dimensionally stable, and they don't absorb water.

**Reinforcement forms:**
- **Woven cloth**: strongest and priciest.
- **Mat**: random fibers; cheap, weaker, uses a lot of resin, harder to form on curves.
- **Chopped strand mat (CSM)**: strong in all directions. Its binder dissolves only in **polyester** resin, so epoxy and water-based resins won't wet it out.
- **Surfacing veil/tissue**: hides the weave pattern under a gel coat.
- **Woven roving**: coarse, strong and cheap; needs covering layers.
- **Mat tape**: for seams and edges.
- **Never use fiberglass insulation**: it's full of defects and has formaldehyde binders.

**Why US theatre shops largely moved away from it** (H 302): resin toxicity and cost, glass fibers working into skin and clothes, hazardous sanding dust, and resin-coated strand ends sharp as needles.

**Resins:**
- **Water-based** systems (Aqua-Resin, Aqua Form type) are far less toxic and **recommended for beginners**. Many commercial shops have switched to them (H 303). The glass dust from sanding is still hazardous.
- **Polyester** is the traditional "fiberglass resin." **Epoxy** is pricier and more water-resistant (outdoor or submerged props). Polyurethane is rarely used for laminating.
- **Gel coat**: a polyester surface coat that stays tacky in air unless it's a wax-type gel coat or covered with film. Tackiness inside a mold is useful because the next layer bonds to it.
- Polyester needs **60–85°F and under 60% relative humidity**. Moisture trapped between layers causes **delamination** later. Don't lay up fiberglass in the rain.

**Safety** (H 304; see [ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md)):
- Uncured resin sensitizes, and allergy can strike after years.
- Wear nitrile gloves, not latex, and protect your forearms. Barrier cream only *with* gloves.
- Splash goggles and coveralls, so you don't track dust home.
- Styrene vapor from polyester affects the nervous system and is a possible carcinogen.
- Use disposable brushes and sticks; seal and remove resin trash.
- Mix in flexible **polyethylene** bowls, because other plastics may dissolve in polyester.

**Open-mold layup** (H 303–305):
1. Release (PVA). **Petroleum jelly inhibits polyester's surface cure.**
2. **Impression coat**: thickened resin, or gel coat with polyester.
3. Pre-cut pieces no bigger than about a sheet of paper, with **gloves on even while cutting**, because glass shards get into skin. Use many small pieces on complex shapes.
4. Lay pieces overlapping and wet them out until they turn **transparent**. Work out air with a stippling brush or a ribbed roller. Don't break big bubbles into many small ones.
5. Lay the next layer **crosswise over the previous seams** (mark the weave direction on the cloth to check). Don't slide pieces once placed.
6. Two layers suffice for hand props. Add mat, CSM or roving for thickness, and veil under the first cloth to hide the weave.
7. Finish with a **flow coat** (wax gel coat, or cover with polypropylene or cellophane film, not polystyrene).
8. Demold at the green stage. For multi-part molds, trim to the parting line and bridge the seams with wet strips.
9. Over a **form**, reverse the order. Polyester still needs resin on both sides.

**Finishing:**
- Shape before full cure.
- **Sanding dust is extremely hazardous** (glass plus uncured resin), and dust that settles can be stirred up months later. Use source extraction and a respirator.
- It's often better to skim with body filler and sand that instead.
- **Protruding strands are daggers.** Trim with heavy shears, a hacksaw or a grinder (with extraction), and seal edges with heavily catalyzed resin (H 305–306).

### Carbon fiber and prepregs (H 306)
Carbon fiber is costly. It's usually laid up in epoxy, and wet-out is hard to see, so weak spots are easy to miss. It needs **vacuum bagging** to reach its full performance, which is overkill for most props. **Prepregs** need vacuum bagging plus oven curing at about 300°F (never in a food oven).

## For the classroom

- **Papier-mâché before fiberglass.** Hart's progression (H 9): every student makes a papier-mâché mask or helmet over a form with an alternating-layer strategy, then seals and paints it.
- **Thermoplastic mask.** Students form a Wonderflex or Varaform mask over a face form, laminate two layers, fill seams with flexible filler and paint.
- **Leather molding.** Wet-form a vegetable-tanned leather mask or bracer over a carved form with a sticketta.
- **Comparison test.** Impact-test a papier-mâché panel, a thermoplastic panel and (if your shop is equipped) a water-based-resin fiberglass panel, and discuss rigid vs. flexible failure ([ch. 26](../4-fabrication-core/26-structure-and-armatures.md#rigid-vs-flexible-h-190191)).
