# 36. Shells and Laminates

> **Sources:** H ch.15 (pp. 291–306); W ch.6 (pp. 105–121), ch.7 (pp. 139–141)
> **Companion:** [Shells and Laminates Projects](36-shells-and-laminates-projects.md): wheat-paste and binder recipes, a papier-mâché bowl or mask, a buckram crown, cloth-mâché, a water-based composite shell, a polyester GRP cast in a plaster waste mold, an epoxy and chopped-glass cast in a silicone mold.
> **[Check current]** Thermoplastic sheet brands (Wonderflex, Fosshape, Varaform, Formfast) and water-based resin products change; check availability and current data sheets. W's resin, filler and core-mat advice is British and from 2003.

A **shell** gets its shape from something else, such as a form, a mold or a skeleton. It's made by wrapping, covering or laminating material onto or into that shape (H 292). There are two families:

1. **Flexible sheets made rigid**: activated by water or heat, then hardened in place.
2. **Composites (laminates)**: a fibrous reinforcement embedded in a hardened polymer matrix, built up in overlapping layers into a seamless skin (H 292).

Wilson treats casting and laminating together because they share materials and techniques. "Laminating" means combining a glue or resin with fibrous reinforcement, in a mold or over a core. His groups: **GRP** (glass-reinforced plastic), resin/filler pastes, **plaster-based** materials (poured or laminated with glass or natural fibre), and "low-tech" composites such as papier-mâché (W 105–106).

## Water- and heat-activated sheets

| Material | How it works | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Buckram** (millinery) | Starch-stiffened fabric. Soak in warm water, drape and press into the form, let dry stiff | Nontoxic and re-wettable. Sew **millinery wire** into the edges of large shapes. Stiffen further with more layers or PVA. Parts of the Tin Man costume in the 1939 *Wizard of Oz* were buckram (H 292) |
| **Fosshape** | Heat-activated nonwoven (about 100–130°F; steamer or heat gun). More heat makes it stiffer | **Shrinks a lot**, so cut patterns oversize. Can be pinned over forms, or sewn and also sewn to other fabrics as a hidden stiffener (H 292–293) |
| **Vegetable-tanned leather** | Soak 15 min to 12 hr, drape over a positive form, pin in trim areas (steel pins stain), leave slack for valleys, work with a **sticketta** (rounded wooden tool) for 20–60 min until it holds. Hammer details while drying, then burnish | Dries shoe-stiff. **Breathes**, which makes it comfortable for masks and armor. **Cuir bouilli**: dip briefly (≤2 min) in boiling water, or bake low (120–175°F), for harder, more brittle leather. Can be pressed between two forms (H 293) |
| **Industrial (pressed) felt** (≥50% wool) | Soak in PVA (diluted up to 1 part water to 2 parts glue), wring out, mold like wet leather, pin, dry for days (it shrinks and stiffens). Finish with PVA, shellac or varnish. Brush on resin instead of PVA for speed | The traditional fake for cast iron and armor; breastplates; trunk hardware. Wool fibers spread the glue evenly; synthetic felt won't work (H 293–295) |
| **Celastic → Formfast** | Rigid sheet softened in MEK or acetone, draped, self-adhering, dries hard | Unmatched drape and heat resistance (the coachmen statues from *Into the Woods* stayed in use for 25 years). **Very heavy solvent exposure**: long chemical gloves, ventilation, respirator, booth or outdoors, no unprotected bystanders (H 295). Wilson describes Celastic as felt-like fabric impregnated with cellulose cement, once many puppet and mask makers' favourite, but fading because of the solvent and harder to get by 2003; "resincloth" is the old generic term (W 119, 181) |
| **Thermoplastic sheets**: Wonderflex, Fosshape, Varaform (ex-Hexcelite) | Soften at about 150–170°F (hot water, heat gun, oven). Shape freehand, over a form or in a mold. **They self-adhere when hot**, so you can laminate 2–3 layers for stiffness. Cold water or ice packs freeze the shape | Masks, mascot heads, armor, giant puppets, all lighter than fiberglass. Wear thin leather gloves against burns. Foil or lecithin works as a release. Fill seams with flexible fillers (acrylic caulk, flexible coatings) and avoid rigid topcoats. Fine under stage lights; softens in a hot car (H 295–297) |

## Composites

Papier-mâché, cloth-mâché, fiberglass and carbon fiber all follow one idea: **overlapping strips of reinforcement saturated with a liquid polymer harden into a continuous skin** (H 297). Like casts, they need releases ([ch. 38](38-molding-and-casting.md#release-agents-h-265267)). They shrink slightly as they dry, so they release themselves from undercut-free negative molds. On positive forms, pop them loose while still green and set them back on to finish curing.

### Papier-mâché (H 297–301)

Papier-mâché has been a mainstay for centuries: most props before about 1950 were papier-mâché, wood and fabric, and some of the birds in Hitchcock's *The Birds* were mâché. Build it over chicken wire, balloons or crumpled foil, or lay it up inside molds. It also works as a **coating** that gives a uniform, paintable surface (H 297–301). The term covers both a **laminate** of torn paper and glue and a **dough** of paper pulp and glue, either in molds or over a former (W 119). Laminated paper is cheap and quick but slow to dry when thick; lightweight paper laminates are delicate but remain an important **mask** material, since a mask is fairly easy to protect (W 119). Pulp is much stronger but takes more preparation and longer drying; it's good for small casts or for picking up detail in a big mold before backing with paper laminate (W 119). Making pulp: [ch. 37](37-sculpting.md).

**Binders:**
- **Wheat paste**: about 2 parts water to 1 part gluten (all-purpose) flour. Whisk part of the water with the flour, boil the rest, stir in the slurry, boil 3–5 min and cool. Refrigerated, it keeps 2–3 days.
- **Wallpaper paste**: buy craft versions, because traditional pastes contained rat poison and some modern ones contain pesticides.
- **PVA white glue**: strong and flexible; thin with water. **Carpenter's glue** is stiffer and sands better. **Mod Podge**.
- Acrylic media and gesso, polyurethane floor finishes, theatrical emulsions (Sculpt or Coat, Crystal Gel, Flex Glue) (H 297–301).
- Almost any water-based glue works; Wilson finds a mix of **strong size and wallpaper paste** cheap, easy and particularly tough (W 119).

**Papers:**
- **Newsprint** is the classic. **Tear along the grain** for straight strips. **Tear rather than cut**, so the ragged edges blend, and tear off factory edges.
- **Kraft paper** for interior strength (H 297–301). For stronger, heavier laminates on larger objects Wilson also uses sugar paper and printmaker's blotting paper (W 119).
- **Tissue** for smooth outer layers, or for translucent props lit from inside.
- **Paper pulp** (blended paper plus binder) is a sculptable clay for features and a final skim coat.
- Use **unglazed** paper; coated office paper won't absorb. Newsprint ink shows through unless you paint over it.

**Technique:**
- **Alternate the direction of each layer**, as plywood does.
- **In a mold**, reverse the order: start with a **water coat** of fine paper soaked in water only, which acts as the release, in small pieces with minimal overlap (or a pulp beauty coat over a release). Then add fine paper, then newsprint or kraft for strength (H 297–301). A glue-free first layer releases from untreated plaster; otherwise use sealed, oiled plaster molds or very firm silicone (W 119).
- **Over a clay original** (masks), seal the clay with shellac or cling film, or let it go leather-hard, to speed drying (W 119).
- **Shrinkage:** paper casts shrink as they dry, a solid pulp cast far more than a laminate dried layer by layer (W 119).
- **Two-part molds**: lay up each half, trim to the parting line, demold, join with hot glue or tape, then bridge the seam with strips that cross it.
- Embed **stiff wire** between layers for structure.
- **Instant papier-mâché** powders: soak for hours or overnight (keeps about a week refrigerated), mix in a sealed bag to avoid dust, apply in thin layers.

**Drying:**
- Use as little water and as thin a build as strength allows.
- Air-dry until merely damp, then use low oven heat (100–150°F) or heat lamps. Drying too fast too early cracks it. Fans help.
- **Waterproof** with shellac, lacquer, linseed oil, or acrylic or polyurethane emulsions.

A well-sealed mâché prop can **outlast fiberglass** because it flexes under impact instead of shattering (H 299). Hart's example: a papier-mâché bathtub with an Ethafoam rim, finished to look like hammered copper (H 300).

### Cloth-mâché (H 301)
Muslin or cheesecloth in glue. With a flexible binder it has more give than paper, which is good for impacts. Cut strips **on the bias** for strength and alternate layer directions. Cheesecloth makes a thin coat that keeps detail ([ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md)).

### Fiberglass (GRP) (H 301–306)

Glass fibers are flawless, strong and flexible. They don't rot or rust, they're dimensionally stable, and they don't absorb water (H 301–306). GRP combines the adhesive power of resin, which is hard but brittle on its own, with the tensile strength of glass to make a strong, light material that takes complex shapes. Because boat, surfboard and display work is often small-scale, materials and processes suit minimal equipment. In props it ranges from small casts to covering masonry, sculptural and landscape pieces and making **armour**; it casts well in silicone and plaster waste molds, takes metal and mineral powders, and goes over polystyrene or clay (W 106–107). The process: a **gel coat** (coloured, filled or thickened resin) in a mold becomes the outside surface; over a core or "plug," a final **top coat** does the same job; glass layers build strength, sometimes separated by filled resin or core material for stiffness (W 107). Wilson laminated epoxy and glass cloth straight over a clay original, digging the clay out through the neck hole (W 105).

**Reinforcement forms:**
- **Woven cloth**: strongest and priciest.
- **Mat**: random fibers; cheap, weaker, uses a lot of resin, harder to form on curves.
- **Chopped strand mat (CSM)**: strong in all directions. Its binder dissolves only in **polyester** resin, so epoxy and water-based resins won't wet it out (H 301–306). The fibres (about 0.01 mm thick) are chopped to about 50 mm and bonded into a random mat; the styrene in polyester dissolves the binder, and rolling or stippling spreads the fibres. Weights are by area: **300 g/m²** conforms best to complex shapes; **600–900 g/m²** works on plain areas but won't go into detail or round sharp corners. Each layer needs about **twice its weight in resin** (W 107).
- **Glass cloth** weights of 135–200 g/m² in plain or twill weave are most useful; also sold as tape. Tightly woven cloth is harder to wet out than CSM but needs only about **its own weight in resin**, making a stronger laminate, and its fibres move less, so thickness and surface are more even (W 107).
- **Chopped strands** (about 6 mm) and **milled fibres** (about 1 mm) mix straight into resin as a fairly strong paste to spread in a mold or over a core (W 108).
- **Surfacing veil/tissue**: hides the weave pattern under a gel coat (H 301–306). Surface mat and tissue aren't structural, but on delicate work or some polystyrene covering they may be all you need (W 107).
- **Core materials** stiffen thin, flexible laminates by bulking them between two layers of reinforcement. Resin/filler paste is heavy and hard to keep even; thin polyurethane foam is very light but awkward on complex shapes; felt-like **core mat** drapes easily and suits light, rigid, even casts such as **armour** (W 110).
- **Woven roving**: coarse, strong and cheap; needs covering layers.
- **Mat tape**: for seams and edges.
- **Never use fiberglass insulation**: it's full of defects and has formaldehyde binders.

**Why US theatre shops largely moved away from it** (H 302): resin toxicity and cost, glass fibers working into skin and clothes, hazardous sanding dust, and resin-coated strand ends sharp as needles. Wilson, writing in Britain in 2003, reports that despite the styrene hazards many prop makers still preferred polyester GRP to epoxy, because it costs a fraction as much and works faster with CSM (W 108, 141); see the disagreement table.

**Resins:**
- **Water-based** systems (Aqua-Resin, Aqua Form type) are far less toxic and **recommended for beginners**. Many commercial shops have switched to them (H 303). The glass dust from sanding is still hazardous.
- **Polyester** is the traditional "fiberglass resin." **Epoxy** is pricier and more water-resistant (outdoor or submerged props). Polyurethane is rarely used for laminating (H 301–306).
- **Polyester in detail** (W 108–109): cheap, available, easy, but gives off **styrene** until set; use enclosure and extraction or work outdoors (occasional small jobs in a large airy space with an organic-vapour respirator, if no one else is exposed). Styrene **dissolves expanded and extruded polystyrene**, so seal foam completely with several coats of paint or a layer of aluminium foil first. It's catalysed with **MEKP at 0.5–3%**; set time also depends on the **accelerator** in the resin, which reacts with the catalyst to make heat (unaccelerated resin sets only with applied heat). DIY resins come pre-accelerated for their purpose (more for thin layers, less for casting), so don't mix them up. If you buy accelerator separately, mix it thoroughly into the resin before adding catalyst, and **never let neat accelerator and catalyst touch**. Grades: laminating resin (clear syrup; fire-retardant versions are whitish), **gel coat** (thixotropic, "like mayonnaise"), **top coat** (contains wax so it cures tack-free), casting resin (less accelerator). A small shop can make do with general-purpose resin, adding fumed silica to thicken and wax-in-styrene to cut tack, though it won't perform as well as formulated products.
- **Epoxy in detail** (W 109): about four times the price of polyester but much stronger, giving tougher, lighter laminates; bonds powerfully to other materials and hardware; takes fillers (colloidal silica, talc, marble dust) to make modelling pastes over the laminate. Fixed ratios (3:1, 5:1) that can't be varied, with slow and fast hardeners; metering pumps are essential for many small batches. Hardeners are corrosive and toxic by skin contact; avoid it completely. Small jobs in a large workshop with a good through-draught; bigger jobs may need local exhaust. Epoxy won't dissolve CSM binder, so use glass cloth; epoxy mixed with chopped strands mats itself into thin layers but is weaker than cloth laminate.
- **Gel coat**: a polyester surface coat that stays tacky in air unless it's a wax-type gel coat or covered with film. Tackiness inside a mold is useful because the next layer bonds to it.
- Polyester needs **60–85°F and under 60% relative humidity**. Moisture trapped between layers causes **delamination** later. Don't lay up fiberglass in the rain (H 301–306).

**Fillers and pigments** (W 109–110):
- Cheap quarry fillers (talc, gypsum, china clay, slate powder, marble dust) make resin pastes and putties for texture, detail and fairing, or add colour. Dense slate and marble resist chipping on fine detail; all fillers reduce shrinkage cracking in solid casts; talc or clay paste can be the core of a "sandwich" laminate. **Most contain free silicates, harmful to inhale.**
- **Fumed silica** (colloidal silica, Cab-o-sil) makes resin fully thixotropic with almost no weight and drags less on tools; it's amorphous, not crystalline, but very light, so don't breathe it. **Glass microspheres** make a weak, easily sanded paste.
- **Metal powders** (brass, copper, aluminium) in a gel or top coat polish up after curing with steel wool, wet-and-dry paper and metal polish ("cold casting"). Use at least **equal parts filler to resin by volume**; mixes based on laminating resin are thick enough for gel coats; polyester then needs more catalyst (ask the supplier). Some gel-coat resins don't suit metal powders, so use laminating resin (W 110–111). Wilson's cauldron had graphite and aluminium powder in an epoxy gel coat, rubbed with steel wool to a deep metallic finish (W 109).
- Pigment pastes (opaque and translucent) for polyester, fewer for epoxy; dry pigments (chemistry less predictable), mica, pearl pigments, glitter and bronze powders also work. Keep pigment to **no more than 10% by weight** to avoid upsetting the cure.

**Tools and supplies** (W 110–111): little expensive kit beyond fume extraction. Catalyst by syringe or a supplier's dispenser. Wooden sticks; **plastic teaspoons for epoxy only** (polyester dissolves them). Flexible plastic pots and buckets so cured waste breaks out into the bin; waxed paper cups for either resin, polystyrene cups for epoxy only; many food tubs won't survive polyester. Laminating brushes are cheap enough to throw away after epoxy (it needs lots of acetone to clean); polyester brushes wash out in acetone or plenty of cheap washing-up liquid and warm water, but don't last. **Ribbed metal rollers** consolidate glass and remove bubbles. Knife, scissors or a disc cutter for glass; strong craft scissors to trim part-cured (green) laminate; a hacksaw, small angle grinder with cutting disc, or power sander for cured work, with a dust mask and extraction.

**Safety** (H 304; W 114; see [ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md)):
- Uncured resin sensitizes, and allergy can strike after years.
- Wear nitrile gloves, not latex, and protect your forearms. Barrier cream only *with* gloves.
- Splash goggles and coveralls, so you don't track dust home.
- Styrene vapor from polyester affects the nervous system and is a possible carcinogen (H 304). The safe level is only just above the point where you can smell it, so **if you can smell it strongly, you're probably at risk**; use low-emission resin where possible, and local exhaust with a fresh-air supply or an organic-vapour respirator (W 114).
- **MEKP** is highly corrosive and can cause severe burns; avoid skin contact and its vapour (W 114).
- Use disposable brushes and sticks; seal and remove resin trash.
- Mix in flexible **polyethylene** bowls, because other plastics may dissolve in polyester (H 304).
- Beware contaminated gloves and resin on container sides, not just the work. Keep the **safety data sheet** for every product where it can be found in an accident (W 114).

**Open-mold layup** (H 303–305):
1. Release (PVA). **Petroleum jelly inhibits polyester's surface cure** (H 303–305). For a plaster waste mold, Wilson uses a water-based film-forming release (sodium alginate) or soft wax on a dry mold (W 111); see the disagreement table on grease.
2. **Impression coat**: thickened resin, or gel coat with polyester (H 303–305). Let the gel coat gel; fillet sharp corners with talc-thickened laminating resin so glass doesn't bridge them (W 111).
3. Pre-cut pieces no bigger than about a sheet of paper, with **gloves on even while cutting**, because glass shards get into skin. Use many small pieces on complex shapes.
4. Lay pieces overlapping and wet them out until they turn **transparent**. Work out air with a stippling brush or a ribbed roller. Don't break big bubbles into many small ones.
5. Lay the next layer **crosswise over the previous seams** (mark the weave direction on the cloth to check). Don't slide pieces once placed.
6. Two layers suffice for hand props. Add mat, CSM or roving for thickness, and veil under the first cloth to hide the weave.
7. Finish with a **flow coat** (wax gel coat, or cover with polypropylene or cellophane film, not polystyrene) (H 303–305). Wilson's flow coat is laminating resin with pigment and a little silica; for a very smooth back, spread heavily thickened paste and cover it with polypropylene sheet (W 111, 113).
8. Demold at the green stage. For multi-part molds, trim to the parting line and bridge the seams with wet strips (H 303–305). Break a plaster waste mold away under **running water**, which also washes off the parting film (W 112).
9. Over a **form**, reverse the order. Polyester still needs resin on both sides.

**Finishing:**
- Shape before full cure.
- **Sanding dust is extremely hazardous** (glass plus uncured resin), and dust that settles can be stirred up months later. Use source extraction and a respirator.
- It's often better to skim with body filler and sand that instead.
- **Protruding strands are daggers.** Trim with heavy shears, a hacksaw or a grinder (with extraction), and seal edges with heavily catalyzed resin (H 305–306). If the back of a laminate may be handled, finish it with a thick flow coat or a layer of lightweight cloth; wear gloves to demold untrimmed work and trim it soon. Dust from freshly cured resin may still contain toxic compounds (W 114).

### Epoxy and chopped glass (W 112–115)

A fast alternative to laying up cloth: resin and glass are mixed into a paste and applied together. Brush a slightly thickened, pigmented layer into the mold first to pick up detail and hide the reinforcement. Mix resin and hardener, then stir in chopped strands until the mix forms **soggy, wafer-like mats** on the stick: just enough resin to hold the glass (too much drains to low points; too little leaves it fluffy and unmanageable). Spread it round the mold; using a different pigment from the gel coat shows where coverage is thin. The cured **spikes of glass are razor-sharp**, so this isn't a process for masks that will be worn, and the cast must be filed or belt-sanded before it's handled without gloves (W 112–115). Steps: [project 6](36-shells-and-laminates-projects.md#project-6-epoxy-and-chopped-glass-in-a-silicone-mould-w-112115).

### Polymer plaster laminates (W 116–117, 139–141)

Polymer solutions used in place of plain water make **polymer plaster**: much stronger than ordinary plaster and acceptable onstage as small solid casts or as a glass-reinforced laminate. It has none of GRP's flexibility and nothing like its strength-to-weight ratio, so treat it not as a water-based substitute for GRP but as a useful alternative **where weight isn't critical** (W 116).
- Buy a complete system, or buy the liquid polymer alone and mix it with any **alpha gypsum** plaster, which is far cheaper; a system's ancillary products may justify its premium (W 117).
- Retard it with trisodium citrate; thicken with colloidal silica or proprietary thickeners; colour with dry pigments or pastes; model or texture before it sets (suitably filled and retarded) and sand or machine it after (W 117).
- Reinforce laminates with plasterer's jute scrim or multiaxial open-weave glass cloth; for extra strength, sandwich two layers of glass round a filled core layer (W 117).
- Laminates cover polystyrene, and can be over-modelled with polymer plaster thickened with colloidal silica or dry paper pulp. Talc while modelling stops sticky hands and gives smooth, shiny surfaces (W 117). More on plaster coatings for foam in [ch. 32](32-foams-and-rubbers.md) and [ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md).

### Carbon fiber and prepregs (H 306)
Carbon fiber is costly. It's usually laid up in epoxy, and wet-out is hard to see, so weak spots are easy to miss. It needs **vacuum bagging** to reach its full performance, which is overkill for most props. **Prepregs** need vacuum bagging plus oven curing at about 300°F (never in a food oven).

## For the classroom

- **Papier-mâché before fiberglass.** Hart's progression (H 9): every student makes a papier-mâché mask or helmet over a form with an alternating-layer strategy, then seals and paints it.
- **Thermoplastic mask.** Students form a Wonderflex or Varaform mask over a face form, laminate two layers, fill seams with flexible filler and paint.
- **Leather molding.** Wet-form a vegetable-tanned leather mask or bracer over a carved form with a sticketta.
- **Glass-to-resin ratios.** Students weigh 300 g/m² CSM and 200 g/m² cloth samples, wet them out with a water-based resin, and weigh again to test Wilson's "twice its weight" and "its own weight" rules (W 107).
- **Comparison test.** Impact-test a papier-mâché panel, a thermoplastic panel and (if your shop is equipped) a water-based-resin fiberglass panel, and discuss rigid vs. flexible failure ([ch. 26](../4-fabrication-core/26-structure-and-armatures.md#rigid-vs-flexible-h-190191)).
