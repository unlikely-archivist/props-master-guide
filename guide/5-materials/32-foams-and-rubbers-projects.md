# Foam Projects (projects for ch. 32)

Starter projects for [ch. 32](32-foams-and-rubbers.md). EVA foam builds have their own file: [EVA Foam Build Walkthroughs](33-eva-foam-projects.md). Food built from foam is in the [Prop Food Recipe Cards](41-prop-food-recipes.md). Steps marked **[General knowledge]** aren't from the guide's sources.

> **Safety:** hot-wiring or hot-knifing polystyrene releases styrene vapour; rasping makes dust. Ventilate, use an organic-vapour respirator per the SDS for hot cutting, and a particulate mask and goggles for rasping (H 243; M 143–144).

## Project 1: a carved and coated XPS rock or gravestone

**Teaches:** laminating, carving, hard-coating, painting.
1. **Laminate** 1" or 2" XPS sheets into a block with water-based contact cement ("green glue") or wood glue. Don't use solvent sprays: they dissolve XPS (H 252–253; FF 26–29, 157).
2. Clamp with cauls or a strap over boards so the clamps don't dent the foam (FF 11, 125, 157).
3. Draw the outline on two faces. Rough-cut with a hot-wire cutter, band saw or pull saw ([ch. 37](37-sculpting.md#subtractive-techniques-h-242243-w-132136)).
4. Shape with a Surform or rasp; a rasped face hides glue lines (FF 146).
5. **Coat it** so it survives handling: scenic dope or joint compound with PVA for a hard shell, or cheesecloth-mâché for strength ([ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md#coating-foam-h-315317-w-136141); [ch. 27 recipes](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture-recipes.md)).
6. Base-coat, wash and drybrush to look like stone ([ch. 28 recipes](../4-fabrication-core/28-painting-and-finishing-recipes.md)).

## Project 2: a slice-method foam figure (H 243–244)

1. Cut front and side profiles from plywood and set them on a frame.
2. Add top-profile "slices" between them.
3. Fill the spaces with pre-roughed foam blocks.
4. Carve flush to the slices ("connect the dots").
5. Coat and paint. Hart built a three-foot garden gnome this way (H 243–244).

## Project 3: backer-rod molding and trim (H 179–181)

1. Halve polyethylene **backer rod** lengthwise with a sharp blade.
2. Glue it to a frame or picture surround as curved molding (H 179–181).
3. Prime with a flexible primer and paint ([ch. 28](../4-fabrication-core/28-painting-and-finishing.md)).

## Project 4: an upholstered foam cushion (H 212–217; W 145–147)

1. Trace the base onto upholstery foam and cut it with an electric carving knife, held vertical (H 212–217), or a knife-bladed band saw (W 145). For a crowned, less slab-like cushion, Wilson glues together two pieces of foam with bevelled edges (W 146–147).
2. Glue a layer of polyester wadding (batting) over the top with a light spray of contact adhesive, or wrap it right round and glue or stitch the edges (W 147).
3. Cover it: see the boxed-cushion and drop-seat projects in [Fabric and Upholstery Projects](34-fabric-and-upholstery-projects.md).

## Project 5: a homemade hot-wire bow (W 130–131)

**Teaches:** resistance heating; hot-wire safety. Build under supervision.
1. Get **nichrome** resistance wire of 24–20 SWG (0.4–0.8 mm); polystyrene suppliers may give offcuts (W 130).
2. Power it at **low voltage**: a 6 or 12 V battery, or a transformer giving a range of low voltages at up to 10 A (W 130).
3. Stretch the wire between two insulated handles or across a bow. With a fixed voltage, adjust the wire **length** until it just cuts; longer wire runs cooler (W 130–131).
4. For a fixed table cutter, tension the wire with a spring or weight so it can lengthen as it heats (W 130).
5. Test on scrap at the **lowest temperature that cuts**. The wire must never glow (W 130–131).
6. In use: ventilate (local exhaust or a fan); switch off as each cut breaks through and pick off burnt bits; wear rigger gloves against burns and shock (W 131).

## Project 6: turning polystyrene (W 128–130)

**Teaches:** large turned forms (columns, finials, urns) in foam.
1. Use a lathe that runs slowly (500–600 rpm at most) or a simple homemade one; foam is light, so the lathe can be simple (W 128).
2. Mount the stock: glue it to a ply disc on the faceplate; or glue a ply disc to each end; or, for small pieces, use two discs faced with **coarse sandpaper**, one indented for the drive spur and one punched for the tailstock centre, gripped by tailstock pressure (W 128).
3. If it must slide over a steel tube, bore the hole first, push a slightly longer piece of the tube through, and locate its ends in holes in the discs (W 128–130).
4. Turn with a sharp tool on a solid rest, or move a fixed router or hot wire against the slowly hand-turned work for repeat pieces (W 128).
5. Coat the turning (scrim, plaster, epoxy and silica) and sand or turn the coating while it's still on the lathe (W 128, 141).
6. Extract dust, wear a P2 mask, and plan the clean-up, which can take longer than the turning (W 130, 136).

## Quick reference

**Upholstery foam grades** (W 145): density/hardness, e.g. 33/180 firm seat, 24/080 soft back, 50/135 dense soft seat. Chipfoam 5 lb or 8 lb for arms and edges.

**EPS densities** (W 125): standard 12–15 kg/m³; heavy/extra-heavy 18–24 kg/m³ for fine detail and thin sections. Specify FRA grade.

**Which glue on which foam** (H 179–181, 252–253; FF 26–29; KF 7, 15):

| Foam | Good glues | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| XPS / EPS | Water-based contact cement, PVA, wood glue, low-temperature hot glue | Solvent sprays, solvent contact cement, polyester resin |
| EVA | Contact cement (KF's choice); hot glue (Hart's choice) | — (see the [disagreement](33-eva-foam.md#where-the-sources-disagree)) |
| Upholstery foam | Contact or barge cement, foam spray adhesive | — |
| Polyethylene (backer rod, Ethafoam) | Hot glue, contact cement | Most water-based glues won't grip |
