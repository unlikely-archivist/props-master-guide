## Rubbers and foams (H 179–181; H 236)

- **Elastomer** is the technical word for rubbery polymers.
- **Open-cell foam** is sponge-like and absorbent. **Closed-cell foam** is rigid, stable and waterproof.

| Material | Uses |
|---|---|
| **Latex** | Liquid for casting, coating and makeup effects. Vulcanized into rubber. **Allergy risk** |
| **Neoprene, nitrile** | Casting (neoprene); chemical gloves |
| **EPDM** (pond liner, roofing rubber) | Durable elastic parts, padding |
| **Polyethylene foam**: Ethafoam backer rod, pool noodles, yoga mats | **Halved backer rod makes curved molding.** Thermoformable |
| **EVA foam** ("craft foam", floor mats) | **Lightweight armor, masks, character heads.** Heat-formable. **Hot glue bonds it** (the same polymer). Pattern it like paper, beveling convex joints ([ch. 24](../4-fabrication-core/24-patterning.md)). KF uses contact cement as its main EVA adhesive instead (KF 15; [ch. 33](33-eva-foam.md#where-the-sources-disagree)) |
| **Soft polyurethane foam** ("polyfoam") | Upholstery, puppets (H 179–181). Often loosely called "foam rubber," because the first upholstery foam was foam latex, still used at the top end but rarely onstage because of price (W 143–144) |
| **Chipfoam / "recon"** | Offcuts of new polyurethane foam shredded and bonded under pressure into dense blocks; firm enough for upholstered arms and stiff edges (W 144–145) |
| **Plastazote** | Strong, flexible closed-cell foam in various colours and thicknesses, for puppets, structural costumes and padding (W 180) |
| **Rigid polyurethane foam** | Fine-detail carving. More toxic. Taxidermy suppliers stock it (H 179–181). Can be cast as blanks to carve or turn, or to back-fill vacuum formings; flexible PU casting foams suit puppets and prosthetics ([ch. 38](38-molding-and-casting.md)) (W 116) |

**Cutting foam**: band saw for dense foam; a **knife-edged blade** for soft foam (coarse teeth can grab and drag soft foam into the blade); a foam cutter; or a fully extended snap knife with a drop of sewing-machine oil on the blade (H 181). For upholstery foam Wilson rates, in order (W 145): an **electric foam cutter** with two reciprocating blades (Bosch's model cuts foam up to 300 mm thick, but is expensive) **[Verified 2026]**; a **band saw with a straight knife band** and an extension table for whole sheets (an ordinary blade of six or more teeth per inch also works, except on light trimming cuts, where thin foam can be dragged into the throat plate); jigsaw knife blades for thinner foam, with firm support clear of the blade; an **electric carving knife**, if you can keep its scalloped blades sharp; and, at a fraction of the price, a plain 9–10" kitchen carving knife kept razor-sharp with a steel or disc sharpener.

For food props made from XPS, upholstery foam, backer rod and expanding urethane foam (FlexFoam-iT!), see [ch. 41](41-prop-food.md) (FF).

For building whole props from EVA foam (densities and thicknesses, PVC and rod cores, carving, textures, foam clay, heat sealing and flexible primers), see [ch. 33](33-eva-foam.md) (KF).

## Rigid foams for carving (H 243, 252–254; FF 13, 26–29, 145–157)

| Foam | Character | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **XPS** (extruded polystyrene; pink or blue insulation board) | Fine, even cells; carves cleanly; glue seams vanish | Dissolved by most solvents and spray paints. Fragile, so **coat it**. Glue with water-based contact cement ("green glue"), wood glue or PVA; avoid solvent sprays (H 252–253; FF 26–29, 157). Often called by one maker's trade name, Styrofoam; used almost only for insulation, so sizes are limited (Wilson: 2,440 × 610 mm sheets up to 125 mm thick) and it costs three to four times as much as EPS. Density, surface and fire-retardancy vary by brand, so ask the supplier (W 125–126) |
| **EPS** (expanded polystyrene; bead foam; US "beadfoam") | Coarser beads; comes in huge blocks; packaging foam is less dense | Good for big sculptures; beads tear out when carved (H 253). See [Polystyrene in detail](#polystyrene-in-detail-w-125132) below |
| **Floral and craft foam** | Brittle; often sold as "Styrofoam" | Not the same as XPS; crumbles (H 253) |
| **Rigid urethane foam** | Denser; holds finer detail | Taxidermy suppliers; or cast your own from A-B foam (H 253–254) |
| **Spray foam** | Fills irregular shapes over armatures | Cures from the outside, so apply in thin layers. **Isocyanates**: use local exhaust (H 254) |

Full carving methods are in [ch. 37](37-sculpting.md). Laminating tables for XPS are in [ch. 41](41-prop-food.md#rigid-foam-xps-ff-13-and-chs-4-17-22-23-27-30-39-41).

**Safety** (H 243; M 143–144; W 131, 136): hot-wiring or hot-knifing polystyrene releases styrene vapour; rasping makes dust. Ventilate (local exhaust for big hot-wire rigs), use an organic-vapour respirator per the SDS for hot cutting, and a particulate mask and goggles for rasping ([ch. 37](37-sculpting.md#polystyrene-safety-h-243-m-143144-w-131-136)). Sanding, turning, routing and power-sawing make huge amounts of light, static-clinging dust: extract it (which protects passers-by too), wear a disposable P2 mask, and vacuum often (W 136).

## Polystyrene in detail (W 125–132)

**Buying EPS** (W 125): made for packaging and construction (insulation, concrete shuttering) in blocks ("billets") of 2,440 × 1,220 × 610 mm (8' × 4' × 2'), sometimes bigger. Suppliers hot-wire-slice it to your thickness (sheets are often bowed) and may cut cylinders and spheres. **Densities:** "standard" is 12–15 kg/m³, adequate for most stage use; heavy or extra-heavy (18 or 24 kg/m³) is stronger and takes finer detail, useful for thin sections; lighter grades are cheaper but usually too fragile to carve. Always specify the **fire-retardant additive (FRA)** grade (about 10% more), even for foam that never goes onstage, because you don't want untreated foam lying around the shop. **[Check current]**

**Cutting and shaping tools** (W 126–128):
- **Handsaws** are fast but messy: a well-set panel saw for straight cuts, a bow saw for curves; square cuts take practice.
- **Band saws** quickly cut 8' × 4' sheets down and rough out shapes, cleanly and square, with intricate curves; they need a big throat (about 600 mm) for all but small work.
- A **bench saw** cuts thinner sheets straight but makes far more dust than a band saw; an **electric jigsaw** with extra-long blades cuts and shapes (blade type hardly matters).
- **Knives**, kept sharp: a plain, narrow 150–200 mm kitchen knife carves; a wider one removes bulk fast by stabbing and twisting out lumps; long, thin, flexible knives cut curves and concave surfaces.
- A **wire brush** removes material fast (and messily); it's good for sketching flowing lines, and because it can't make detail it keeps you on the basic forms.
- **Sandpaper**: coarse to remove material at small scale, finer to smooth and refine. Keep it clear of loose beads and use gentle pressure to minimize breakout.
- **Disc and belt sanders** are fast but need extraction at source, and the surface heat can be a problem; a **router** leaves a crisp surface but also needs extraction.
- A **hot-air gun** wafted over carving levels tool marks, melts away fine sanding dust and forms a tough crust; it also textures (for example, masonry), but only enough to shrink the surface slightly, since more melting releases unacceptable styrene. Each tool leaves its own texture (knife, wire brush, hot wire, band saw) (W 127–128).

**Hot wires** (W 130–132): a current heats a resistance wire that melts through the foam. You can buy them; many people make their own.
- **Nickel-chrome (nichrome)** wire is more efficient and longer-lasting than steel; polystyrene suppliers may give you offcuts. 24–20 SWG (0.4–0.8 mm) suits general use.
- Power it at **low voltage** for safety: a 6 or 12 V car or motorbike battery, or a transformer giving a range of voltages in that region at up to 10 A. With a fixed voltage, find the wire length that reaches cutting temperature.
- **Use the lowest temperature that cuts; never let the wire glow.**
- Freehand: a wire between two handles for large blocks (don't loop it, which shortens it and can overload the supply). Fixed: the foam is moved through a stretched wire, tensioned so it can lengthen as it heats.
- **Profiled wires** of 2–4 mm, bent to a moulding's profile, need much more wattage (a bigger transformer) and skilled bending to avoid cold spots.
- **Hot-wire safety:** switch off as each cut breaks through and remove the bits of foam that would burn on the hot wire; ventilate (local exhaust or a fan); a particulate-and-organic-vapour respirator only as a last resort; rigger gloves protect against both burns and shock (W 131).

**Turning polystyrene** (W 128–130): fast, and it can make sophisticated hollow forms and very large turnings; coatings can be applied, sanded or turned on the lathe. Wood lathes are often too small and too fast, so much work happens on simple **custom-built lathes** (the foam is light and speeds are slow); a wood lathe with a bottom speed of 500–600 rpm at most, ideally with outboard turning, still helps. Two approaches: a geared-down motor (or an energetic assistant) spins the work against a hand tool on a rest; or a fixed powered tool (router, hot wire) cuts while the work is turned slowly by hand, which is slower but suits an unpowered lathe and production runs. Mounting: glue the foam to a ply disc on the faceplate; glue ply discs to each end; or, for small work, use two discs faced with **coarse sandpaper** gripped by tailstock pressure. To bore a turning to slide over a steel tube, bore it first, put a slightly longer piece of the tube through it, and locate the tube ends in the discs. **The mess is enormous**: cleaning up can take longer than the turning (W 128–130). Steps: [project 6](32-foams-and-rubbers-projects.md#project-6-turning-polystyrene-w-128130).

**Joining polystyrene** (W 132):
- Use **contact adhesives made for polystyrene**; many contact glues have solvents that dissolve it. Look for adhesive dissolved in hexane rather than toluene, or ask the supplier. They need a close fit: a thin, even coat on both faces, 5–10 minutes to flash off, then an instant bond with no repositioning. Keep glue lines thin where you'll carve or hot-wire through them; the thicker, cheaper glues from insulation suppliers can stop a hot wire or knife.
- **Latex glues** work as contact adhesives but dry much slower and are worse for wires and knives. **Epoxy** gives very strong bonds but is slow and inconvenient; kept thin it can be carved through.
- **Rigid PU "expanding foam"** is the only effective **gap-filling** glue: an aerosol from builders' merchants, or two liquids from boat chandlers. It bonds well and carves once cured, though it's denser than the polystyrene. It releases **isocyanates** while curing and when carved: extraction, ventilation or at least an organic-vapour respirator.
- **Spray mount / photo mount** gives weak, easily broken bonds, handy for test-assembling work before permanent gluing.
- **Glues to avoid:** DIY "polystyrene cement" is for sticking tiles to ceilings (useless for props, except perhaps as a coating); model-shop "polystyrene cement" is for solid polystyrene kits and simply eats holes in EPS.

## Coating foam (H 315–317; W 136–141)

Bare foam dents, tears and dissolves under many paints. Choose a coating by what the prop must survive: flexible coats for soft foam, hard sandable shells for rigid foam, laminates for strength. **Polyester resin dissolves polystyrene**; epoxy and polyurethane don't (H 317). Wilson's four groups are scrimming and papering, proprietary coatings, plaster-based mixtures, and GRP; they range from a paint-friendly skin to fire resistance, great strength or over-modelled detail (W 136–137). To use polyester over polystyrene, seal the foam completely with several coats of paint or aluminium foil (W 108, 141). The full table is in ch. 27. The full table is in [ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md#coating-foam-h-315317). For EVA foam, see heat sealing and flexible primers in [ch. 33](33-eva-foam.md#filling-and-priming-kf-2829-36-40-47).

## Working with foam

- **Density matters.** Foams are sold by density (weight per volume). Higher density is stronger, heavier and holds detail better; lower density is lighter and cheaper but crushes and tears more. EPS grades run from standard 12–15 kg/m³ to extra-heavy 24 kg/m³, the denser grades taking finer detail and thin sections (W 125). **Upholstery foam** is graded by **density** (kg/m³, typically 24 light to 50 heavy; this governs durability) and **hardness** (newtons, from about 50 very soft to 230 very firm; this governs feel), written density/hardness: **33/180** is a medium-weight firm seat foam, **24/080** a light, soft back foam, **50/135** a dense but soft top-quality seat foam. Denser foams cost more, especially soft grades. **Chipfoam** is sold by density in pounds per cubic foot, usually 5 lb and 8 lb (about 80 and 130 kg/m³; 8 lb is gym-mat stuff), and used for arms and stiffened edges. New foam comes in sheets of about 2 × 1.5 m in standard thicknesses, or cut to size by the supplier (W 144–145).
- **Hot-wire cutters** cut polystyrene cleanly and fast, using a heated nichrome wire. Use the lowest heat that cuts and never let the wire glow (W 130–131; details above). Keep the wire moving, and never use one on polyurethane or PVC foams, which give off much more hazardous fumes **[General knowledge]**.
- **Upholstery (polyurethane) foam** is cut with an electric foam cutter, knife-bladed band saw, electric carving knife or a long, razor-sharp kitchen knife (W 145), and glued with contact adhesive, brushed (thixotropic types are tidiest) or sprayed (faster, dearer), on both faces, left a few minutes, then pressed together with no repositioning (W 146). Wrap it in polyester wadding for upholstery ([ch. 34](34-fabric-and-upholstery.md)) (W 143).
- **Puppet and soft-sculpture foams** (reticulated foam, upholstery foam) are carved with scissors and glued with contact cement, then covered with fabric or latex **[General knowledge]**.
- **Foam is flammable.** Many foams burn fast and release toxic smoke. Keep them away from heat sources, and check flame-retardancy requirements for anything going onstage ([ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md#flame-retardancy-h-320)). Specify fire-retardant grades when ordering: FRA polystyrene (W 125) and fire-retardant upholstery foam (in the UK, upholstery foam must meet BS 5852, but untreated foam is still sold for other uses) (W 143). **[Check current]**
- **Store foam flat and out of sunlight.** UV breaks down many foams, and they yellow and crumble with age **[General knowledge]**.

## For the classroom

- **Foam sampler.** Students make a sampler board of six foams (XPS, EPS, EVA, upholstery, polyethylene, rigid urethane), carving, gluing and coating a sample of each, and label what each does well.
- **Glue test.** Test five adhesives on XPS and record which melt it, which hold, and which leave a carvable seam.
- **Hot-wire build.** Under supervision, students build a low-voltage hot-wire bow from nichrome and a variable transformer, find the lowest cutting temperature, and write the shop's hot-wire safety card from Wilson's rules (W 130–131).
- **Coat comparison.** Coat four identical carved XPS rocks with different coatings, then drop-test and scratch-test them ([ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md#coating-foam-h-315317)).
