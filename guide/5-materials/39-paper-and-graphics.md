# 39. Graphics and Paper Props

> **Sources:** H ch.18 (pp. 352–360); M ch.11 (p. 158)
> **Companion:** [Paper and Graphics Projects](39-paper-and-graphics-projects.md): aged parchment, a period letter and newspaper, a vintage label, a fake book, waterslide decals.
> **[Check current]** Printer technology and specialty papers change fast. Copyright and trademark law is jurisdiction-specific, so take your institution's guidance, not general advice.

Letters, newspapers, labels, signs, books, money, tickets, maps and posters all count as graphics. The same techniques also serve faux finishes, such as a printed marble pattern adhered to a surface (H 353).

## Content: what's written on it (H 353)

- **Film and TV close-ups need "real" text.** Include every word the actors speak, and fill the rest with period or location research. Check the writing technology too: a letter before the 1840s was usually folded and wax-sealed rather than put in an envelope, and quill, steel nib, fountain pen, typewriter and ballpoint each leave a different trace ([Appendix E §5](../../appendices/E-period-props-timeline.md#5-writing-and-paper)).
- **Stage props that will never be read closely** can use illegible script, repeated blocks of text, or **lorem ipsum** filler.
- **Never write anything "funny" or "clever."** Even disciplined actors get distracted or break character when they spot it.
- **Ask the actor.** Some want their lines on the page they "read"; others want nothing resembling their lines.

### Handwriting (H 353)
Computer "handwriting" fonts look mechanical because the letters repeat and the spacing is uniform. **Calligraphy and period handwriting are worth learning.** Write the document by hand, then scan or copy it for multiples and backups.

## Copyright and trademarks (H 355)

Reproducing photographs, artwork, text, logos or brands often requires **permission or clearance**, even when the item is named in the script. Hart's cautionary example: a regional theatre reproduced a California high school's logo on giant banners. The school found out from hundreds of miles away, sent a cease-and-desist letter, and the theatre had to reprint everything at great expense. **Don't guess.** Take the question to your production manager or legal office (H 355). (Mussman makes the same point about reproducing images from reference books, M 80.)

The same applies to **downloaded 3D-model files**. B asks students to respect the Creative Commons terms on designs they download and adapt (B 48–49). Licence terms vary by design, so read each one ([ch. 40](40-3d-printing.md#level-2-candlestick-phone-b-4859)) **[Check current]**.

## Analog reproduction (H 353–355)

- **Stencils**: cut from thick paper, then stippled or sprayed. Good for a non-handmade look on surfaces that won't go through a printer, and for repeated patterns. Many found objects work as stencils.
- **Screen printing**: mesh with blocked-out areas. It handles "island" shapes that a stencil can't, but takes more preparation.
- **Stamps** (mirror-image designs; rubber, or carved from anything, potato included). Custom stamps can be ordered from your artwork. **Blocks** (wood or linoleum) can be stamped or run in a **press**.
- **Rubbings / frottage**: rub graphite or chalk over paper laid on a texture or carving, or burnish paper or fabric over a block.

## Digital graphics (H 355–357)

- Graphics software lets you assemble, age and preview documents.
- **Printers**: what matters is paper size, paper type and process, not photo quality. Several specialized printers can beat one jack-of-all-trades.
  - **Inkjet**: affordable and detailed, with optional specialty inks (white, metallic). **The ink is water-soluble and smears if the print gets wet.**
  - **Laser/toner**: fast, cheap for text, **waterproof**, prints on **acetate and transparency film**. Large-format color laser printers are expensive.
  - **Wide-format "plotters"** (rolls about 40" wide) often live in scene shops. Use them for newspapers and printed "oil paintings."
  - **Print shops** handle oversized jobs, unusual stock and large quantities.
- **Scanners** make flat, evenly lit, proportionally accurate copies. They're also how hand-made paper props become digital files.
- **Fake books**: wrap printed covers on real books. If the actor flips through it, the interior must match: no color photos in a period text, and poetry shouldn't look like prose (H 356).

### Putting graphics on 3D objects (H 357)
- **Decals** (waterslide paper): usually **laser-printed**, because inkjet ink washes off in the soak. They give a mass-produced look.
- **Stickers**: any printed paper or film plus spray adhesive or contact cement.
- **Découpage**: layered glued papers.
- **Toner transfer**: soak a laser print or photocopy in acetone and press it face down. The image transfers in reverse onto fabric and other surfaces. Ventilate and wear gloves.
- **Example**: a "vintage" rubber-cement jar made by scanning a modern label, altering it in software, printing it and adhering it to a real jar (H 359).

## Aging paper (H 357–360; M 158)

Start by thinking about the document's history ([ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md#distressing-and-aging-h-317319-m-158159)). Then:

- **Tea or coffee bath** in a rimmed baking sheet, or paint it on with a tea bag or rag. Dry by air, in an oven (~200°F; paper ignites near 451°F and smolders before that), in a microwave, or with a hair dryer or heat gun (H 357–358).
- Mussman's **parchment method**:
  1. Crumple the paper, dampen it and tear the edges.
  2. Flatten it and soak it in strong tea.
  3. Dry it and iron it flat.

  A quicker alternative is to spritz it with Design Master Glossy Wood Tone (M 158).
- **Selective effects**:
  - rub used coffee grounds or tea leaves on
  - sprinkle **instant-coffee crystals** for "blooms"
  - cover loosely with wax paper or plastic while drying for mottling
  - crumple and iron (H 358–360)
- **Lemon juice + heat** browns paper wherever the juice touches. **Walnut ink crystals** and **soy sauce** also stain (H 360).
- **Inks may run** in these baths. You can age the paper first and print afterward, but the writing may then look newer than the paper, and curled paper jams printers (H 360).
- **Don't burn the edges** unless the document was actually in a fire. For rough edges, tear them (a deckle edge helps) or rub the wet edges with your finger (H 360).
- Cut paper cleanly with a craft knife against a straightedge, or a paper cutter (H 360).

## Paper-prop practicalities

- **Paper props are often consumables.** Letters torn nightly need multiples: performances plus rehearsals, dress and previews (M 8; [ch. 1](../1-intro/01-what-is-a-prop.md)).
- **Waterproof** labels on bottles washed nightly with clear packing tape, or print them on a laser printer (H 72).
- **Prop money**: check current law before producing realistic currency **[check current; jurisdiction-specific]**.

## For the classroom

- **Letter-writing assignment.** Students hand-letter a period letter in an appropriate script, age it convincingly, and present the document's "history." Peers critique believability.
- **Newspaper build.** Students design a period newspaper front page, real headline included, print it on newsprint, and fold and age it for a scene where it's read onstage.
- **Clearance exercise.** Give students a script calling for a famous brand of soda and a real university logo. They draft the clearance email and prepare a generic alternative.
- **Label-to-object.** Scan a modern product label, "period-ize" it in software, print and apply it, then distress the bottle to match.
