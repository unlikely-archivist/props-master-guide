## Real or fake? (FF 3–5; M 7–8; H 6)

FF's answer is "it depends" (FF 4). Fake food wins when:
- **The run is long.** Building once is cheaper than buying perishables nightly for weeks or months (FF 4; H 6).
- **The food is dressing, or the eating is mimed.** No crew time goes into nightly cooking (FF 4).
- **It sits under lights or on a film set for hours.** Real food spoils, attracts insects and breaks continuity between takes and shooting days (FF 5).
- **You need control.** A home-built piece can be tweaked in colour and shape during the build. A mass-produced fake can't, and commercial fake food is expensive and seldom the right form (FF 4, 33).
- **It will come back.** The goose for an annual *A Christmas Carol* can be pulled from storage and freshened (FF 4). Fake food also moves between shows: breakfast plates built for *The Spitfire Grill* suit *Bus Stop*, *Diner* or *Waitress* (FF 5).

Real or edible food wins when the run is very short, an actor actually has to eat, or build time is extremely tight. Drinks are the usual case (FF 4). The two can combine: see the edible inset cake below and [ch. 47](../7-effects/47-eating-drinking-and-smoking.md#food-and-drink-m-78-156157-h-6).

**Case study: the snapped beans** (FF 4–5). Stage One's touring *Diary of Anne Frank* needed green beans snapped onstage at every performance. Fresh beans were beyond the budget and the tour schedule, so the crew **cast beans in green wax**. Wax beans snap with a good sound, and the broken pieces are remelted into the next batch. The tour carried the molds and a toaster oven and recast at any venue where it stayed two days or more. It's a good example of the needs/means method from [ch. 22](../4-fabrication-core/22-the-build-method.md): a consumable effect solved with a renewable fake.

**Storage** (FF 4, 22). Let the piece dry completely. Bag it, press out the air and seal it, then store it in a plastic bin with a tight lid. Kept this way, pieces last for years. The book reports wax hot chocolate and Encapso K lemonade still looking new after five years (FF 12, 17). Record stored food in the inventory like any other stock ([ch. 13](../2-admin/13-strike-storage-and-records.md)).

## Principles (FF 3–5 and throughout)

1. **Research the real thing first.** Buy it or photograph it. A food prop is convincing when its **colour, shape, texture and sheen** are right (FF 4). Every recipe in FF begins with "match your research".
2. **Realism comes from layers of translucent glaze** over a base, not one opaque coat (FF 130, 157). This is Hart's overpainting method ([ch. 28](../4-fabrication-core/28-painting-and-finishing.md#overpainting-h-335339)) applied to food: browning, juices and caramelisation are all built from thin transparent layers.
3. **Prime foam before painting it.** Paint applied straight to foam dries brittle and flakes off when handled (FF 15). Skip the primer only for pieces that are never touched (FF 125).
4. **Shop kitchenware never goes back to real food.** Label pans, spoons, cups and graters ("WAX ONLY") and store them apart (FF 8–10).
5. **Cast when you need several.** One silicone mold of a real wing, shrimp, rib bone, corn cob or bread roll gives as many copies as the show needs (FF 37–41, 85–89).
6. **Upgrade bought fakes.** Plastic lemon slices and acrylic ice cubes save time, but they usually look fake because the finish is wrong (too glossy, too dull, too smooth). Re-texture and re-tone them (FF 175).
7. **Repurpose.** A foam ham from *The Best Christmas Pageant Ever* was sliced into roast beef (FF 145).
8. **Sample kits stretch far.** One Smooth-On trial kit (under $35 in 2018) often covered several dishes. One 1-oz Rosco paint sample kit ($70–$100 in 2018) covers almost the whole book (FF 4, 15–17) **[check current]**.
9. **Scale to the production.** A stylised show may need oversized food (FF 51). Detail level depends on the distance to the audience (FF 179). Compare Hart's needs-and-means questions ([ch. 22](../4-fabrication-core/22-the-build-method.md)).

## Safety specific to food props (FF 19 and every recipe)

The general rules are in [ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md). FF adds the following:
- **Re-read the SDS periodically.** Companies change formulas, so one check isn't permanent clearance (FF 27 and throughout).
- **Rules for melting wax** (repeated in every wax recipe):
  - never pour wax down a drain
  - never leave it melting unattended
  - keep it away from open flame
  - monitor it with a candle thermometer
  - **never heat gel or creme wax above 220°F**
  - use a potholder, because filled glassware gets very hot
  - keep an **ABC extinguisher** nearby (FF 33, 172)
  This adds to the fire guidance in [ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md#fire-safety-h-4244-m-149151).
- **Gloves.** Nitrile is the default. **Use vinyl, not latex, with Dragon Skin and Encapso K**, because latex inhibits their cure (FF 86, 175). This is the same cure-inhibition problem Hart describes for silicones ([ch. 38](38-molding-and-casting.md#flexible)).
- **Exothermic casts.** Smooth-Cast 300 gets hot. Smooth-Cast 320 gets *extremely* hot and gives off visible fumes (FF 40, 219).
- **Ammonia in liquid latex.** Castin' Craft Mold Builder contains ammonia; Kangaroo brand doesn't. Ventilate either way (FF 14–15, 124).
- **Allergies.** Ask the whole company about food allergies and sensitivities (FF 19). This matters most when an edible piece sits inside a fake one.
- **Sources differ on urethane hazards.** FF treats FlexFoam-iT! and Smooth-Cast as beginner-friendly trial kits, needing gloves, safety glasses and ventilation (FF 26–30, 38). Hart calls urethanes the most toxic common casting material: isocyanates can't be smelled and can sensitise, and he calls for local exhaust or supplied air ([ch. 38](38-molding-and-casting.md#rigid); H 278–280).

## Materials playbook

### Salt dough (FF 20–22; H 250–251 "baker's clay")

Flour, salt, water, and usually oil and alum, hardened by baking or microwaving. Use it for **crusts, fried foods, dumplings and mashed potato**.

| Version | Flour | Salt | Water | Oil | Alum | Method → result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional (FF 21) | 2 c | 1 c | ¾ c | — | — | Bowl-mixed, kneaded, baked at 350°F. Cracks more easily |
| V1 (FF 21) | 2 c | 2 c | 1 c | 2 tbsp | 2 tbsp | Cold-mixed. Oil and alum resist cracking |
| V2 stovetop (FF 21) | 2 c | 2 c | 1 c | 2 tbsp | 2 tbsp | Stirred over low heat about 8 min, to mashed-potato consistency. **Less sticky.** Extra salt plus oil gives a **pockmarked "fried" skin** (FF 43) |
| V3 low-salt (FF 22) | 2 c | 1 c | 1 c | 2 tbsp | 2 tbsp | Stovetop. Stays soft and flexible longer while kneading (FF 142, 190) |
| Wonton (FF 65) | 2 c | reduced | — | 2 tbsp | 4 tbsp alum or cream of tartar | Microwave-dried. Boiled look |

- **Alum preserves the dough.** Cream of tartar can substitute. Leave it out if the dough will be sealed right away (FF 145).
- **Baking temperature controls puffing.** At 350°F for 20–25 min the dough puffs and bubbles. At 250°F for 15 min (FF 202) or **200°F for 60 min** (FF 190) it barely puffs. Take it out when it turns white and dry.
- **Baking the underside of a crust.** Shellac the cooled crust, invert it over the upturned pan, and bake it again (FF 192, 203).
- **Microwave "boiled" finish.** Wrap the piece in a damp towel and microwave 15 s. Re-wet it and microwave 30 s more (FF 67).
- **Fried texture.** Brush amber shellac on fresh dough and seal it in a zip bag for 24 hr. The shellac reacts with the salt and moisture and turns granular (FF 44).
- **Shelf life.** Unbaked: about 3 days airtight. Baked and properly stored: at least a year (FF 22).

### Candle wax (FF 12, 33, and every wax recipe)

Yaley **Gel Wax** is translucent and pourable, and has a realistic weight. Yaley **Creme Wax** is opaque, has more structure, traps fewer bubbles and sets faster. Both are odourless and long-lasting (FF 12, 33).

| Blend | Result | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Gel only | Translucent liquid look | Soy sauce, syrup, gravy, beer, coffee, cocoa, caramel, egg yolk, cherry filling, custard (FF 46, 83, 147, 166–173) |
| 2 gel : 1 creme | Softer; can be cut with an ordinary knife | Swiss cheese, diced tomato, quiche (FF 36, 151, 153) |
| 1 gel : 1 creme | Firmer | Cheddar, gouda, tomato slice, gelatin mold (FF 35–36, 120, 216) |
| Creme only | Opaque solid; grates well | Shredded cheddar, cast corn (FF 98, 102) |
| Soy candle beads | Melts low (about 110°F; pour at 115–120°F; max 150°F). Already white; vanilla scent available | Icing drizzle (FF 86–89) |

- **More creme sets faster but is harder to cut** (FF 33).
- **Temperatures.** Melts at about 130°F. Pour at 160–170°F. Maximum 220°F. Heating to **about 215°F traps bubbles**, which is useful for beer (FF 166).
- **Tinting.** Shave in concentrated candle dye, or crayon on a budget. Test the colour by pouring a sample onto a ceramic dish (FF 12, 102, 147).
- **Molds.** Ceramic dishes, cake pans, muffin tins, plastic Easter eggs and gelatin molds, sprayed with Universal Mold Release. Don't move the mold until the wax has set. A freezer speeds small pieces (FF 34–36, 81, 110, 216).
- **Cutting.** Use a hot knife on wax paper (lift the pieces before the drips set), a snap blade warmed under hot water, or drill bits (Swiss cheese holes) (FF 35–36, 111).
- **Suspending things.** Wax holds inclusions in place: marshmallows, plastic grapes as cherries, seed beads as tomato seeds, wood shavings as coconut or horseradish, paper spinach (FF 57, 151, 155, 170, 203, 212).
- **Seal the wax before putting foam on it.** Use clear shellac or water-based polyurethane, or the dye migrates into urethane foam (FF 82, 167, 172).
- **Unmolding.** Dip the mold in *warm* (not hot) water for about 10 s. Moisten the plate so the piece can be slid into position (FF 216).
- **Cleanup.** Let gel wax cool completely; it peels out of the pan (FF 94).

### Rigid foam (XPS) (FF 13, and chs. 4, 17, 22, 23, 27, 30, 39, 41)

Pink (Owens Corning Foamular) or blue (Dow Styrofoam) insulation board. Use **1" sheet** for carving (FF 145). It's the base for **bread, roasts, ham, turkey, salami, potatoes, cakes and filler blocks**. See Hart's general guidance on polystyrene and safety ([ch. 31](31-plastics.md), [ch. 37](37-sculpting.md#polystyrene-safety-h-243-m-143144)).

**Laminating** (FF 26–29, 95, 130, 157, 195):

| Adhesive | FF's notes |
|---|---|
| Titebond II | Fast tack. Shape after 1 hr; full cure 8 hr (24–36 hr for large stacks) |
| Elmer's Glue-All | Cheap. Use plenty and clamp hard |
| Rosco FlexBond | Stays flexible |
| 3M Fastbond 30NF water-based contact cement ("green glue") | Coat both faces, dry about 30 min until no longer glossy, assemble within 4 hr. **Preferred:** cleans up with water and doesn't attack foam. Wrap brushes and rollers in plastic between uses |
| 3M Super 77 *Classic* or **78 Polystyrene** spray | Spray both faces and let tack 30 s. **The reformulated Super 77 contains acetone and dissolves XPS** (FF 157, 195) **[check current]** |

- Use **cauls** (lauan, Masonite) or a ratchet strap over wood blocks so the clamps don't dent the foam (FF 11, 125, 157).
- Clear aerosol tips by inverting the can after each use (FF 157).
- These match Hart's adhesive families ([ch. 25](../4-fabrication-core/25-adhesives-and-fasteners.md#adhesive-families)).

**Shaping.** Rough out with a band saw, jig saw or Japanese pull saw. Shape with a Surform or rasp, a Dremel for detail, and a sander for smoothing (FF 10–14). **A rasped face looks like cut meat and hides the glue lines** (FF 146). **Pink foam can stand in for a base coat on red meat.** An earth-umber wash over pink reads as roast beef (FF 130, 146).

**Primers** (FF 15, 125): Rosco FoamCoat (can be sanded once dry), FlexCoat, CrystalGel, Sculpt or Coat, FlexBond or Fastbond. Compare the foam-coating table in [ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md#coating-foam-h-315317).

### Other foams (FF 13, 119, 124, 128)
- **Upholstery (flexible polyurethane) foam,** split to ½": sandwich bread (FF 119–120).
- **Backer rod (polyethylene):** kielbasa-style sausage, primed with FlexCoat. For short links, stuff foam or batting into pantyhose (FF 124, 128).

### Molding and casting with Smooth-On (FF 17–18, 37–41, 54–58, 85–89, 99–105, 218–222)

FF uses Smooth-On products throughout because most mix **by volume with no scale**, pour easily and come as trial kits (FF preface, 17). The methods are Hart's ([ch. 38](38-molding-and-casting.md)). These are FF's specific numbers.

| Product | Role | Mix | Working time | Demold | FF uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| OOMOO 25 | Silicone mold | 1:1 (pink + blue = purple) | 15 min | 75 min | Shrimp, pineapple rings |
| OOMOO 30 | Silicone mold | 1:1 | 30 min | 6 hr | Wings, corn, rib bones |
| Dragon Skin 10 Fast | Strong, stretchy silicone | 1:1, stir 3 min | about 8 min | 75 min (faster above 73°F) | Bread roll |
| Smooth-Cast 300 | Rigid urethane cast | 1:1 | 3 min | 15 min | Wings (12–15 per trial kit), rib bones (24+), shrimp |
| Smooth-Cast 320 | Ultra-low-viscosity cast | 1:1 | about 3 min | 10–15 min (full cure 4–6 hr) | Pineapple rings |

**FF's block-mold routine** (FF 38–40, 56, 101–103):
1. Wash off any sauce.
2. **Seal the organic original** with Krylon Crystal Clear acrylic or shellac (FF 38–39). Hart says the opposite about shellac: none under silicone, because it inhibits cure (H 267).
3. Hot-glue it into a foam-core box **1–2" larger** than the object. Seal the seams with hot glue and tape.
4. Release with **Ease Release 200** and let it dry 5–10 min.
5. Pour at the lowest point to **½–1" over** the top.
6. Cut the box apart at the corners. Make **relief cuts** if needed, but as few as possible for wax casts, which leak through them.
7. **Close the mold with medical tape.** Masking tape and Scotch tape failed.
8. Re-release (twice for Smooth-Cast) and cast. Tint the resin with Silc Pig dropped from an eyedropper (FF 104).

**Originals.** Use grocery items (frozen shrimp, corn, dried pineapple) or restaurant leftovers (rib bones). **Use a plastic lobster, not a real one.** Cooked lobster shell is too fragile to mold (FF 138).

**Release matrix** (FF 17–18, 26, 72, 77, 175). Compare [ch. 38's release table](38-molding-and-casting.md#release-agents-h-265267).

| Situation | Release |
|---|---|
| Silicone molds; general casting | Ease Release 200 |
| Urethane foam (FlexFoam-iT!) | **Ease Release 2831**, or Universal *followed by* 2831. **Never Universal or a silicone release alone: it collapses the foam** |
| Wax into ceramic or metal | Universal Mold Release |
| Encapso K into glass you want back | Petroleum jelly or Ease Release 200 |
| Teflon bakeware with foam | None needed |

### Expanding urethane foam: FlexFoam-iT! (FF 18, 26–31, 71–89, 165–167, 171–173, 184–186, 218–222)

| | FlexFoam-iT! III | FlexFoam-iT! V |
|---|---|---|
| Ratio by volume | **1A : 2B** | **1A : 1B** |
| Expansion | about 15× (lowest density) | about 11× |
| Pot life | about 35 s | about 50 s (FF 77 also says 90 s) |
| Cure | Tacky at 30 min; set at 2 hr | Tacky at 15 min; demold muffins at 40 min; set at 2 hr |
| Surface | Pockmarked | Smoother |
| FF uses | Rye loaf, chocolate doughnuts, egg white, pancakes, whipped cream, upside-down cake | Plain doughnuts, muffins, sticky buns, beer head, piña colada froth |

- Pre-mix Part B with a drill mixer. Measure in identical clear cups using the moulded ridges. Mix hard for 15–30 s, scraping the sides and bottom, and **pour immediately** (FF 30, 73, 167).
- Fill bakeware only partway. Real loaf, doughnut, muffin and cake pans make ready-made molds (FF 30, 73, 77, 220).
- **Wax paper laid over the pour** gives a pocked, crumb-like crust instead of a smooth skin (FF 30).
- Spoon the foam for irregular shapes such as pancakes and fried-egg whites (FF 81–82).
- Compare Mussman's "spray foam" for bread and muffins, which expands about 3× (M 157).

### Clear liquids: Encapso K (FF 17, 65–67, 174–186)

A clear encapsulation rubber for **lemonade, martini, milk, orange juice, piña colada and broth**.
- **Mix:** 1:1. **Pot life 2 hr. Cure 24 hr.** Don't move the glass until it has cured.
- **Tint by single drops** of Silc Pig or Ignite pigment:
  - milk: 2 drops white
  - chocolate milk: 1 white + ½ brown
  - orange juice: red + yellow + white
  - pink lemonade: 1 drop fluorescent pink (FF 176, 182–183)
- **Shake Part A; don't shake Part B**, which adds bubbles (FF 175).
- **Seal painted inclusions** (olives) before pouring. Unsealed paint can stop the cure (FF 179).
- **Anchor anything that would float.** Fix the olive pick to the glass (FF 179–180).
- **Ice cubes.** Pour half the liquid, wait about 90 min, add acrylic cubes, wait about 10 min, then pour the rest (FF 176).
- Coat glassware with petroleum jelly or Ease Release 200 if you want to reuse it. Cured Encapso K can be shaved or crumbled to look like ice or broken glass (FF 17).
- **Cheaper hard-setting alternative:** FloralCraft Acrylic Water Kit, 24–48 hr (FF 178).

### Glazes and finishes (FF 12, 16, 84, 131, 160)
- **Amber shellac** is the "baked" colour. Dilute it **1 shellac : 2 denatured alcohol** and build it in thin coats. Full-strength coats look encapsulated and plastic (FF 78, 88, 204).
- **FEV.** FF's recipe is **1 part shellac : 10 parts denatured alcohol plus leather dye** (brown and red). Clear or amber shellac doesn't matter. It's used for bacon, ham rind and turkey skin (FF 84, 131, 160). Hart's starting ratio is stronger (about 4 alcohol : 1 shellac with spirit dye; [ch. 28](../4-fabrication-core/28-painting-and-finishing.md#fev-french-enamel-varnish-h-348)).
- **Design Master Glossy Wood Tone** spray is FF's universal **"oven browning"**. Mist it where real food browns: crust edges, pan contact, meat ends (FF 12).
- **Sealers:**
  - Rosco Clear Acrylic Gloss for wet looks
  - Gloss + Flat for satin (vegetables)
  - Flat for cured meat
  - **Rosco Premiere Clear** (water-based polyurethane) for hard handling (FF 16)
- **India ink.** Yellow ink in acrylic gloss is popcorn butter. Brown ink in water is a dye bath for noodles and paper vegetables. Brewed black tea worked better for noodles (FF 12, 52, 113–115).

### Modeling compounds (FF 8, 12, 43, 149)
- **Crayola Model Magic.** Light and clean; paintable after about 3 hr. Use it for small produce, olives, marshmallows, lobster meat, apple slices and ice cream. **Paint it with cotton swabs to avoid brush marks.** Reds need 2–3 coats; red Model Magic skips the painting (FF 60–62, 222).
- **Creative Paperclay.** Air-dries hard and can be sanded. Use it for the salmon fillet and egg-roll filling. Work it on plastic, not wax paper (FF 43, 149–150).
- **Moldable thermoplastic pellets** (Polly Plastics, Friendly Plastic, Adapt-it). Soften above 150°F and can be tinted with colour pellets. Use for butter pats (FF 12, 83, 97). Compare low-melt thermoplastics in [ch. 37](37-sculpting.md#materials).

### Found materials (throughout)
FF's most transferable lesson is that everyday textures stand in for food:
- **Paper:** Bounty towels and ribbed Cottonelle toilet paper become lettuce. Brown paper towel becomes potato and turkey skin. Thai Unryu paper becomes spring-roll wrappers and shredded vegetables.
- **Fabric:** silk lining becomes turkey slices, pink corduroy becomes ham, ruffle trim becomes lettuce and bacon, fringe becomes broccoli.
- **Packing peanuts** become popcorn and ground meat.
- **Wood:** sawdust becomes crust and sugar; wood shavings become coconut; balsa becomes almonds.
- **Small fillers:** seed and jewellery beads become seeds and nuts; PVC shavings or glitter become sea salt; baby powder becomes salami bloom.
- **Plastic sheeting**, heat-gunned, becomes bacon.
- **Cotton cord** becomes noodles.
- **Meat netting** dresses salami.
- **A PVC stub** becomes a ham bone.

## The finishing sequence

Every FF recipe follows roughly this order **[synthesis of FF chs. 4–44]**:
1. Form (carve, cast, sculpt or pour).
2. Seal or prime.
3. Pale base colour.
4. Texture layer (sawdust, latex, papier-mâché, caulk).
5. **Translucent glazes**: shellac, FEV, tinted gel, ink.
6. **Browning**: Wood Tone mist, sponged darker colour. Don't over-sponge; FF warns it turns texture into "visual mud" (FF 29).
7. Details (char, joint shadows, colour at tips).
8. **Sheen matched to the food.** This is the most common reason a fake reads as fake (FF 175).
9. Assemble with CrystalGel, Sculpt or Coat, low-temperature hot glue or melted gel wax.

## Fake and real together: the edible inset (FF 194–200)

When an actor eats one piece of an otherwise fake dish:
- Carve the cake from XPS and **cut away the portion to be eaten**.
- Seal it with shellac. FF notes shellac is marketed as food-safe, but **don't rely on that**.
- **Line every surface the real food will touch with overlapping plastic wrap** (FF 197).
- Frost the fake with CrystalGel and cake flour, feathered thin at the joint.
- Set in real pound cake and blend real frosting into fake.

**[Added best practice]** Treat the inset as a consumable under the rules in [ch. 47](../7-effects/47-eating-drinking-and-smoking.md#food-and-drink-m-78-156157-h-6): fresh every performance, prepared by the run crew in a clean area, with allergens checked. FF's sequel, *Volume 2: Prop Foods You Can Eat* (2025), covers edible props and hidden allergens ([Appendix C](../../appendices/C-sources-and-editions.md)).

## Where the sources disagree

| Topic | FF says | Other source says |
|---|---|---|
| Sealing an organic original before silicone | Seal with Krylon Crystal Clear acrylic *or* shellac, then Ease Release 200 (FF 38–39) | No shellac under silicone, because it inhibits cure (H 267) |
| FEV strength | 1 shellac : 10 alcohol + leather dye (FF 84) | About 1 shellac : 4 alcohol + spirit dye (H 348) |

## For the classroom

- **Real vs. fake brief.** Give three scenarios: a 6-week run with mimed breakfast, a 2-night student showcase where a character eats cake, and a 10-hour film day with a buffet. Students choose real, fake or hybrid for each, with a cost and labour estimate, and defend the choice using FF 3–5 and [ch. 8](../2-admin/08-budgets-and-paperwork.md).
- **Sheen lab.** Give each student three identical Model Magic cherry tomatoes. One is finished flat, one satin, one gloss. View them under stage light from the house and discuss which reads as food, and from which distance.
- **One material, three dishes.** Each student picks a material (gel wax, salt dough, CrystalGel and paper, or FlexFoam) and makes three different foods with it. Discuss what the material does well and where it fails.
- **Cast a snack.** In the spirit of Hart's small-complete-object approach (H 9), each student makes a one-piece OOMOO mold of a sealed real food item and pulls three casts, then finishes one for a wet look and one for a dry look.
- **Glaze ladder.** Students paint a strip of primed XPS "crust" with zero to five thin diluted-shellac glazes plus Wood Tone, then compare the result with a single full-strength coat.
- **Edible-inset safety plan.** Students write the procedure for an edible piece inside a fake dish: barrier, preparation location, who handles it, allergen check, nightly replacement and disposal. Use the effect safety plan in [Appendix B](../../appendices/B-paperwork-templates.md).
