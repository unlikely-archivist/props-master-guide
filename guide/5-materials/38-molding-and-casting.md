# 38. Molding and Casting

> **Sources:** H ch.14 (pp. 255–290); M ch.5 (p. 54); W ch.5–6 (pp. 85–123)
> **Companion:** [Molding and Casting Projects](38-molding-and-casting-projects.md): a plaster press mold, a two-piece silicone mold, a brush-up glove mold, an alginate hand, a caulk mold, a scrimmed plaster mold of a clay mask, a silicone skin mold with plaster case, an alginate face cast, a slush-cast latex mask, release and mixing references.
> **[Check current]** Product lines (Smooth-On, Por-a-Kast, Vinamold, etc.), formulations, mix ratios, and safety data change — **always follow the current manufacturer's technical data sheet and SDS.** Resin chemistry is the most hazardous work in most prop shops; do not attempt urethane or polyester casting without the ventilation and PPE described in [ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md).

## Why mold and cast? (H 256)

- **Multiples** — when building each by hand costs more, or for repeated details (rosettes); a **mold library** of ornaments lets a shop decorate huge pieces quickly (H 257).
- **A different final material** than the sculpted model.
- **Hollow skins** — masks, armor, lighter parts.
- **Replicas** of things that can't be used — body parts (an actor's real head is messy and often against Equity rules), valuables, details fixed to a building.
- **Breakables** — many cheap copies.
- **Difficult or very specific shapes**, and copies of existing objects (W 85).

**But ask whether it's necessary** — molding materials are expensive and time-consuming (H 256). Keep molds as simple as you can: the time and money in a mold should be in proportion to its usefulness (W 85).

## Decide in order (H 256–257)

1. What is the **cast** made of (from the prop's needs and your means)?
2. That limits the **mold material**, further constrained by what the **master** is made of and its **shape** (undercuts → flexible or multi-piece; through-holes → piece mold).
3. That determines **releases and sealers**.
4. Then budget, time, and what your shop is equipped (and ventilated) to handle.

Manufacturers' tech support can help if you describe the job well (H 256–257). Wilson plans the same way, working back from the result (W 85–87):
- **Shape and texture of the original.** An original with a back or base that won't be seen is far simpler: fewer mold pieces, easier filling.
- **How many casts?** For one, consider a **waste mold**: a plaster shell, alginate or plaster-bandage life mold, or an impression in clay or wax. Waste molds get round undercuts cheaply and quickly, but the original must be soft (clay, wax) so it can be dug out, and the mold may still need more than one piece.
- **What the original is made of.** A plaster mold, even in many pieces, struggles to release from something rigid like china; use a flexible mold.
- **What the cast is made of.** Multiple rigid casts (GRP, polymer plaster) need either a well-made rigid mold with release and the undercuts designed out by dividing it, or a flexible mold. Rigid molds suit only small runs or very simple shapes, and even then release relies on some **spring**: a thin GRP cast flexes out of a rigid mold that a thick one would never leave.
- **The casting process.** A hollow GRP cast needs easy access to the inside of the mold; a solid cast needs only a pour hole and maybe an air vent. Very runny materials flow through smaller openings into finer detail.

**Learn on cheap, forgiving materials first** — plaster, alginate, latex, papier-mâché — before silicones and resins, whose chemistry punishes mistakes (H 257).

## Vocabulary (H 258–259, 263, 270)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Master** | The original being copied |
| **Mold / negative** | The material surrounding the master, holding a cavity of its shape |
| **Cast / positive** | The copy (most materials shrink slightly — usually imperceptible) |
| **Press mold** | Clay-like material pushed in by hand |
| **Master mold** | A mold used to make more working molds (molds wear out) |
| **Release agent** | Prevents sticking (cast to mold, mold piece to mold piece) |
| **Sealer** | Closes porous master surfaces before releasing |
| **Mother mold / jacket** | Rigid shell supporting a flexible mold |
| **Waste mold** | Destroyed to free the cast — fast, seamless, one use |
| **Piece mold** | Multiple parts, reusable; needed for undercuts |
| **Undercut** | A recess that locks a rigid cast in a rigid mold. Wilson names three kinds: a feature larger than the opening it must pass through; two unrelated planes in different parts of the object that together lock it (easily overlooked); and rough texture, effectively many tiny undercuts (W 86). Three ways round them: a waste mold, a piece mold (cheap material, much skill), or a flexible mold, the usual professional answer (W 86) |
| **Parting line / flashing** | Where mold pieces meet / the thin fin of material that leaks there |
| **Registration keys** | Bumps and sockets that align mold pieces |
| **Sprue / gate / vent** | Pour channel / passage to the cavity / air escape |
| **Beauty (impression) coat** | First thin coat that captures detail without bubbles |

**Golden rule:** cast **rigid materials in flexible molds** and **flexible materials in rigid molds** — rigid-in-rigid with any undercut traps the cast (H 267). Demold resins at the **"green" stage** (holds shape, still slightly flexible) and let them finish curing outside the mold.

## Mold types (H 260–265)

- **Box (bucket) mold:** walls around the master; pour over. Never pour under the master (hydraulic lift, trapped air) (H 260–265). Wilson's **block mold** for small, one-sided originals: fix the object to a baseboard, fill gaps at its edges with clay or wax, build a wall (clay, ply, lino, even Lego) sealed to the board, release if needed, and pour silicone or urethane until it's covered. A fully 3D original can be suspended in the rubber, then the cured block cut open with a scalpel far enough to free it, with an **ingate** and **risers** carved into a cut face (W 92–94).
- **Two-piece box mold:** embed the master halfway in an oil-clay bed at the seam line, carve **registration keys**, pour half one; remove clay, flip, apply release (*silicone sticks to silicone!*), pour half two (H 260–265). Alternatively, suspend the original in the first pour up to the parting line; cut shallow conical holes as keys (W 94).
- **Holding piece molds closed:** rubber bands alone distort a soft mold and may not stop leaks. Back each half with ply to spread the pressure of bands, ties or gentle clamps, or push the mold back into the rigid container it was poured in (W 94).
- **Brush-up mold:** thin beauty coat, then thicker coats (colored layers help judge thickness); one piece (**glove mold**) or divided with **fences** (clay walls perpendicular to the surface with carved keys, metal shims, or stiff card for non-water materials). Support with a **mother mold** — uses far less costly rubber than a box mold (H 260–265). Wilson calls this the commonest skin-mold method in props: thixotropic rubber (thickened with an additive or bought thick) brushed or spatula'd on, then a rigid case built directly over it. It's fast but gives less control over the skin's thickness. Thickened rubbers still sag a little. Build **urethanes** in two or more layers, each part-cured, brushing the first well into the detail; some silicones go on in one layer if you keep scooping the rubber back into place. Urethane has better tear strength; silicone can be reinforced with open-weave fabric (W 95). Step-by-step: [project 7](38-molding-and-casting-projects.md#project-7-a-silicone-skin-mold-with-a-plaster-case-w-9597).
- **Matrix mold:** cover the master in clay (~½" for silicone), build the shell over it, remove the clay, pour rubber into the gap — combines economy and convenience (H 260–265). Wilson calls this **shell molding** (derived from traditional gelatin molding): the most laborious method, but with full control of the skin's thickness and outer shape, so best kept for long production runs. Runners, risers and re-registering skin and case take a lot of planning (W 95).
- **Multi-piece molds:** plan seams where flashing is easy to trim and away from focal surfaces; often easier to **cut the master into parts**, mold each simply, and assemble the casts (H 260–265).
- **Multi-piece plaster molds** (W 90–91): the **clay-wall method** builds a clay or wax wall on each dividing line with round registration dents (a marble or screwdriver end), plasters right up the wall, then peels the wall and soaps the plaster face before the next piece: slow, but accurate and long-lasting. The **fence method** pushes overlapping card, plastic or brass shim 3–4 cm high into a clay original along the dividing lines (slight zigzags help registration), plasters all the pieces at once (don't bridge the fence with scrim), and scrapes back to expose the fence as the plaster sets: fast, mostly for waste molds. To open either kind, make the edges strong, set small clay wedges along the seams to leave **lever slots**, and flood water in as you prise.

## Release agents (H 265–267)

| Release | Good with |
|---|---|
| **Green soap / castile / oil soap** | Plaster-on-plaster, gypsum, papier-mâché |
| **Petroleum jelly** (thin it, or heat-level brush marks) | PU rubber, epoxy, polyester, silicone, plaster, papier-mâché |
| **Waxes** (paste, aerosol) | Epoxy, PU foam, polyester; pair with PVA |
| **PVA (polyvinyl *alcohol*) film release** — *not* white glue | Epoxy, polyester, PU |
| **Lecithin** (some food-safe sprays) | Epoxy, urethanes, polyester; paintable afterward |
| **Silicone spray** | Effective but transfers to casts (paint won't stick) and **contaminates the whole shop** |
| **Cornstarch/baking soda dust, soapy water** | Breaks surface tension for fine detail |
| **Plastic film / foil** | Crude release where detail doesn't matter |

**Resin in a plaster mold** (H 266, 279): seal the dry plaster (shellac/lacquer/paint) → wax → dust with cornstarch → PVA.
**No release for absorption casting** in plaster — it clogs the pores (H 268–269). When making the plaster mold itself, petroleum jelly or wax on the original can migrate into the plaster face and stop it absorbing; soap doesn't affect porosity (W 117).
Silicone molds rarely need release, but light wax extends their life (H 268, 283–284). Silicone is **self-releasing** from most originals and casts; latex releases from plaster; GRP and other adhesive composites in rigid molds most often need release, usually a single layer of grease or wax, though polyester cast from polyester molds needs a proprietary release system. If unsure, test first (W 92–93). On clay originals, **soap destroys fine detail**; use an aerosol wax instead (W 87). Resin thickened with fumed silica can stick to silicone molds unless you use a silicone-free release (W 110).
Release builds up and transfers to casts — clean casts (dish soap; acetone for silicone release) before painting; skip release every few pulls (H 266–267).

**Sealers** for porous masters (plaster, stone, wood): shellac, paste wax, thinned petroleum jelly, paint. **No shellac under silicone** (inhibits cure) (H 267).

> **Conflict with FF.** *The Fake Food Cookbook* seals real-food masters (wings, shrimp, corn, rib bones) with Krylon Crystal Clear acrylic *or* shellac, then Ease Release 200, before pouring OOMOO (FF 38–39). Hart says the opposite: no shellac under silicone (H 267). FF's full food-casting workflow and Smooth-On numbers are in [ch. 41](41-prop-food.md#molding-and-casting-with-smooth-on-ff-1718-3741-5458-8589-99105-218222).

**Two-part mixing:** measure each part in its own cup, combine in a third; tare containers when weighing; mix exact ratios (H 267, 284).

## Casting methods (H 269–271)

- **Gravity casting:** pour and let it flow.
- **Slush casting:** swirl to coat the walls → hollow; brush material up the walls until it stops running; **roto-casting** machines spin capped molds for even walls.
- **Absorption (slip) casting:** a **dry plaster** mold absorbs water so a skin forms on the walls; pour out the excess at the desired thickness. For slip clay, casting latex, neoprene. (Dry plaster is room-temperature and light; damp plaster is cold and heavy.) (H 269–271) With natural latex, a fresh, still-damp mold works too; absorption just takes longer. Molds of even wall thickness give the most even casts. Natural latex gives tough, flexible casts with great detail and undercuts but shrinks a great deal and sags under its own weight; synthetic compounds give thinner, more rigid casts and are harder to find (W 117). Procedure: [project 9](38-molding-and-casting-projects.md#project-9-a-slush-cast-latex-mask-w-117119).
- **Fighting bubbles:** sprue to the highest point; vents exiting as high as the sprue; keep the stirrer submerged; tap the cup, skim foam; **pour a thin, high stream to one spot** and let it flow; vibrate the filled mold; tilt to free trapped air; **vacuum-degas** viscous silicones/urethanes.

## Materials

### Rigid
**Plaster (gypsum)** (H 271–274) — economical, intuitive, low-toxicity; uniquely absorbent.
- Grades vary enormously — USG's range spans ~2,000 psi molding plasters at ~70:100 water:plaster to ~10,000 psi gypsum cements (Hydro-Stone) at ~32:100. Soft plasters for waste molds; mid-strength for working molds; very hard for castings and vacuum-form bucks.
- **Mixing:** fresh, dry plaster; dust protection; water 70–100°F; flexible plastic bucket; **sift plaster into water, let it slake 1–2 min**, mix 2–5 min without whipping in air; tap and skim. The "islands" method is quick but gives inconsistent properties.
- Smear a beauty coat by hand, then pour. Reinforce big molds with wire or rebar (H 271–274). Thin-walled molds are reinforced with **jute scrim** (plasterer's scrim) bedded in wet plaster (W 87–89).
- **Wilson's practice** (W 87): use **casting plaster / plaster of Paris** (wall plasters set too slowly). Test new plaster's set time on a small batch first. About **1 part water to 2–2½ parts plaster by volume**; water in first, plaster sprinkled in to settle. The longer and harder it's mixed, the stronger it sets. **Speed it up** with hot water, salt (up to 10 g per litre) or both; **slow it down** with a dash of trisodium citrate solution (a few ml doubles the set of a litre, so measure carefully) or glue size. Don't mix until you're ready, abandon a batch that starts going off, and wash hands and tools in a bucket, scraping the settled sludge into the bin.
- **Drying:** a mold needs at least 48 hours at room temperature to dry; an oven or drying cupboard is faster but uneven heating can crack it (W 90). Stronger, lighter rigid alternatives to plaster are GRP and polymer-modified plasters such as Jesmonite (W 87).
- **Plaster safety** (W 91): bags weigh up to 35 kg and work gets heavy fast, so lift safely. Avoid dust: goggles and a dust mask when moving open bags or measuring. Always protect your eyes from wet plaster; rinse with plenty of water after contact. Wet plaster dries skin; wash it off, and use barrier cream for long contact.
- **Never pour plaster down a drain.** It's **exothermic** — large masses can burn. Demold when cool (~15–30 min); fully dry in 1–7 days before painting, gluing, or absorption casting.
- **Plaster bandages** make quick, strong mother molds (H 274).

**Polyurethane resins** (H 278–280) — fast, fine detail, enormous range (rigid plastic, rubbers, A-B foams).
- **Most toxic common casting material:** isocyanates off-gas for 24+ hours (years if mis-mixed); **no odor**, so cartridge respirators are unreliable — use a spray booth, chemical hood, glove box, or **supplied air**; cure pieces in a separate area; disposable cups and sticks, sealed and removed. Isocyanate asthma and **deaths** have occurred; sensitization can follow one exposure or decades.
- Moisture-sensitive (damp molds or humid days → tacky, bubbly casts); short shelf life (~5–6 months after opening); sticks to everything but polyethylene (H 278–280). Wilson notes they can be cast in damp molds (alginate, clay) if a slightly worse surface is acceptable, and that an inert-gas "blanket" sprayed into part-used bottles extends shelf life (W 116).
- Wilson's case for urethanes as the material of choice for small, intricate or quick solid castings: tougher (can be dropped or drilled), very fast versions, very runny (fills detail, traps fewer bubbles), often 1:1, takes metal powders and polishes well. Against: expensive, some too tough to buff to a high polish, aggressively adhesive (best in silicone molds) (W 115–116).
- Add colorants and fillers to the resin side *before* catalyst. Best cast in silicone molds.
- **Shell-and-foam trick:** rotocast a tough PU shell, fill with rigid foam → light, strong, detailed (H 278–280).
- **Urethane foams** (W 116): rigid foam back-fills vacuum formings or makes blanks to carve or turn; soft foam suits puppets, prosthetics and casts that must bend round other forms. Expansion is uncontrollable without a closed mold: put a measured amount in and close it securely before foaming starts (about **one minute** with most products). The mold must be strong and rigid, so back flexible molds fully with plaster or GRP. Use enough mix to fill the mold plus about a quarter as much again if left to rise freely; start with too little and work up. For blanks, make greased shuttering from ply (flat faces) or a roll of vinyl flooring tied with string (cylinders), standing on a greased, weighted board. Turning, sawing or sanding rigid urethane makes very fine, irritating dust that may be toxic: contain it.
- Non-flame-retardant PU is highly flammable with toxic smoke.

**Polyester resin** (H 280–282) — cheapest resin; good for clear casts; shrinks more than epoxy.
- **Don't cast with laminating resin** (formulated hot — a mass can overheat, off-gas, or **catch fire**) and don't laminate with casting resin (won't cure thin) (H 280–282). Casting resins contain less accelerator for the heat build-up in deep molds (W 109). Full detail on polyester grades, catalyst and accelerator: [ch. 36](36-shells-and-laminates.md).
- **MEKP catalyst** is explosive, corrosive, skin-absorbed. Too much catalyst = heat, cracks, fire risk; thick casts may need less.
- Styrene vapors (suspected carcinogen) — local exhaust, respirator, covered curing.
- **Auto-body filler (Bondo)** is polyester plus fillers; "Rondo" mixes it with resin. **Water-extended polyester** is cheap and bone-white but warps over years.

**Epoxy resin** (H 282) — mostly adhesive and laminating; pricier casting resin. Toxicity varies by product; >50% of exposed workers develop allergies. **Epoxy putty** models by hand, cures in ~1.5 hours, and machines when hard (H 282). Its cost makes it less likely for solid casts (W 109).

**Exotherm in the pot** (W 114): polyester, epoxy and urethane all heat as they cure, and most are meant for thin layers. A large amount left in a mixing pot can overheat, give off toxic fumes and even catch fire. If it starts, **cover the pot with cold water and take it outside**. Mix only what you need, spread any excess thin to cure, and use slower hardeners or low catalyst ratios for bulky casts.

**Polymer plaster** (W 116–117): a polymer solution replaces the mixing water, giving plaster strong enough for stage use as small solid casts or as a glass-reinforced laminate. It releases from most flexible molds without release, and from plaster waste molds with a layer of soft wax. Details in [ch. 36](36-shells-and-laminates.md).

**Plain plaster casts** are rarely used onstage because they're heavy and fragile; thin, fibre-reinforced casts made like plaster molds are a cheap option if those limits are acceptable, with a steel or timber structure built into the back of anything large (W 116). For plaster breakaways see [ch. 46](../7-effects/46-effects-and-breakaways.md).

### Clear casting (W 120–121)

Water-clear versions of all three main resins imitate glass and crystal:

| Resin | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Clear polyester** | Cheapest and most available; easiest to sand and polish to a glass-like finish; some match glass's refractive index, so open-weave glass cloth reinforces them almost invisibly | Weakest (thin-walled casts smash if dropped); highest shrinkage, so distortion and surface defects; styrene vapour |
| **Clear urethane** | Much tougher; negligible shrinkage | 3–4× the price; harder to polish; fixed ratios, so different products for small delicate and large bulky casts; diisocyanate hardener toxic in minute amounts |
| **Clear epoxy** | Like urethane in price and working | Less available; hardener causes burns, toxin absorption and dermatitis |

- Tint with the resin's own colourants, spirit-soluble dyes or dry pigments. All are low-viscosity, which minimizes trapped air (vacuum treatment removes it completely), but they only cast in molds suited to **self-levelling liquids**, which complicates molds for thin-walled or hollow objects.
- Use silicone or urethane rubber molds for multiples or to keep an original's polish; plaster waste molds for one-offs you can sand and polish. A **lost-wax** route works too: model or turn the object in wax (on a plaster-clad polystyrene core if hollow), build a plaster mold round it, melt the wax out by baking or boiling, flood with release, dry, fill with resin, and break the mold away.
- Clear polyester cures tacky where exposed to air or moisture; neat washing-up liquid removes the tack. Incompletely cured silicone molds cause tacky, crazed polyester casts, so **post-cure silicone molds in a low oven** after their room-temperature cure.
- Examples: polished clear polyester balls and tinted chandelier drops (W 107, 120); a clear urethane dome that took hours to polish because polyester was judged too fragile (W 106).

### White metals (W 121–123)

Low-melting silver-coloured alloys for costume jewellery and small metal objects. Alloys vary in price, polishability and ability to fill thin parts. Most contain **lead**; lead-free alloys are much safer.
- **Melting:** small amounts in a ladle in a blowtorch flame; larger in a small heavy saucepan on a gas or electric ring. Pour well above the melting point (an alloy melting at 240°C pours at about 300°C); check with a thermometer or by how a dipped stick of wood behaves. Skim the dross back with a spoon or bent wire before pouring.
- **Molds:** high-temperature silicone (not every silicone is suitable; check). Two-part molds with an **ingate** and **runner** that enter the cavity at its lowest point so it fills from the bottom up, and vents at the top wherever air would be trapped; pour until metal shows at the vents. Dust the mold with talc or graphite, and warm it (oven, or a couple of sacrificial casts) for fine detail. Rougher casts come from completely dry plaster molds, buried in sand in case they leak.
- **Lost-wax one-offs:** add a wax rod for the ingate and runner, wires for vents and a wax plug as a drain; encase in investment, or plaster mixed with thin clay wash instead of water (strong enough to cast into, weak enough to chip off); pull the wires, bake until all the wax is gone, plug the drain with fresh plaster, dry, and reheat near the metal's melting point before pouring.
- **Shrinkage** is considerable: make originals oversize if needed, and keep a big body of metal in the ingate and runner to reduce distortion.
- **Safety:** melt only with good ventilation (local exhaust or a suitable respirator for leaded alloys); goggles and leather gloves; molds and tools **completely dry**, because a drop of water flashing to steam can spray molten metal; castings and pots stay hot a long time, and never quench them in water; wear gloves to handle, file or polish lead alloys.

### Flexible
**Silicone rubber** (H 283–284) — the props favorite for durable flexible molds.
- Tin- or platinum-cure; brush, pour, or spray. Best release of any rubber (most casts need none). Heat-resistant (~400°F+), so it can cast **low-melt metals** (pewter, tin) (H 283–284).
- The most expensive mold material, so it's often used as a thin **skin** backed by a plaster or GRP shell. Old molds can be put through a food processor and the "meal" used to bulk out fresh rubber (W 91–92).
- Very flexible and tear-resistant → handles severe undercuts; usually needs a mother mold.
- **Cure inhibitors:** sulfur clay, latex (even latex gloves), shellac. Exact mix ratios (1:1 to 10:1); parts are colored to show complete mixing.
- Also a superb **casting** material for skin and prosthetics.
- **Budget hack — silicone-caulk molds** (H 284–285): clear 100% silicone kneaded with glycerin and a little acrylic paint, handled with soapy gloves, pressed on in layers; 24–48 hr cure; inconsistent — temporary use only.

**Alginate** (H 275, 286–289) — seaweed-based, nontoxic, skin-safe; the dental-impression material. Superb detail, but keeps shrinking — usable for only ~2 hours. Can't take moisture-sensitive resins. **The go-to for life-casting hands, feet, faces** (H 275, 286–289). Wilson: it needs a rigid backing (usually plaster bandage) and tears easily. Cast within a few hours; wrapped in a damp cloth and a plastic bag in the fridge it lasts a couple of days. Epoxy, plaster and polymer plaster cast well in it; moisture should rule out polyester and urethane, though some urethanes seem to cast happily (W 98). It's also handy as a very quick temporary mold of objects (W 92). Wilson used its shrinkage on purpose: an alginate face mold was left to shrink for days, cast, re-molded in alginate and shrunk again until it reached the size wanted for a miniature face prop, then cast in epoxy and glass (W 105).

**Polyurethane mold rubber** (W 92) — much cheaper than silicone, often with better tear strength; some are very low-viscosity (fewer trapped bubbles) and many mix 1:1. But both the original and the mold need careful release, they're moisture-sensitive (no molding straight off wet clay), and shelf lives are short.

**Polysulphide rubber** (W 92) — between urethane and silicone in price, less widely available. Can be used directly over wet clay; most originals need no release, but the mold needs release for many casting materials.

**Latex** (H 276–277) — cheap, one-part; brushed glove molds, slush/absorption casting. Up to ~20 coats, each dried (milky color gone); shrinks 10–20%; ammonia fumes; **allergies** among artisans, actors, and crew. Reinforce casts with cheesecloth (bias for stretch, grain for strength); vulcanize per product; spoils in ~1 year (cottage-cheese smell).

**Others:** **gelatin** (remeltable molds; **edible** casts in a food-safe space; melts under stage lights — great for **breakaway prosthetics** like Gloucester's eyes) (H 274–275). Wilson's cheap **gelatin mold material**: soak glue size or pearl glue overnight in cold water, pour off the excess, melt in a double boiler, and stir in antifreeze equal to the dry glue's original volume; pour hot, and it sets to a tough, slightly waxy jelly. It's runny until set (block or shell molds only), poured hot (may affect originals), and **remelts**, so hot-curing casts destroy it: one cast from a large mold, several polyester or urethane casts from small or shallow ones with a soft wax release. Store in a bag in the fridge (W 97); **agar/moulage** (body molds, reusable) (H 275); **neoprene** (absorption-cast, harder than latex, ~3/16" walls for masks; toxic while curing) (H 285); **foam latex** (makeup effects; baked; degrades over years) and two-part **silicone foam** (H 285–286); **hot-pour vinyl** (remeltable; largely replaced by silicone) (H 277). Vinyls are naturally greasy but still need release with urethane or epoxy; originals must be heat-resistant and dry; slight shrinkage; poured molds only (W 92).

**In-a-pinch casting:** Plastic Wood, patching compounds (slow and crack-prone in mass), tinted **hot glue** in a *wet* plaster mold (H 285).

**Shore hardness** (H 286): Shore OO (very soft), A (flexible), D (rigid); higher = harder; scales don't convert; hardness ≠ strength.

## Molding the human body (H 286–290)

- **Alginate** for hands, feet, faces, ears; **Flexwax** (skin-safe low-melt wax, dipped in layers to ~¼", reusable); **skin-safe silicone** for larger areas (**patch-test on the wrist first**). No release needed (H 286–290). In 2003 Wilson found skin-safe silicones very expensive and mostly used by specialists (W 99).
- **Uses** (W 97): a face cast as the base for a closely fitting mask; hands, feet and faces cast and added to carved polystyrene figures to save carving difficult detail; occasionally whole bodies.
- **Plaster bandage** (W 97–98): the cheapest and easiest life-mold material, as a mold in itself or as the rigid jacket for alginate. It captures little detail unless carefully applied, can't handle undercuts beyond slight ones around soft features, and **sticks to hair** unless heavily greased (cake bushy eyebrows in grease or mask them with fine tissue); skin needs only a light greasing. **Cut** it (tearing shakes the plaster out), float each piece briefly on warm water, run it through your fingers, and massage it into the features; overlap pieces before edges set. A face needs 3–4 layers with a thickened rim. Work as a team: one wetting, one or more applying.
- **Face mold in alginate and plaster bandage**: Wilson's full procedure, including nostril straws, communication with the model and removal, is [project 8](38-molding-and-casting-projects.md#project-8-a-life-mold-of-a-face-w-99102). The essentials: explain everything and keep talking once the eyes are covered; agree a yes/no signal; postpone if the model can't breathe freely through the nose; **put the straws in only after the mouth is covered**; keep alginate edges about 1 cm thick; and build strong bandage edges (W 99–102).
- **Hand in alginate:** container with ½" clearance; warm-water mix; actor seated, hand posed ~10 minutes; wiggle to break suction and withdraw. **Cast immediately — on site if necessary** — usually a quick plaster positive, which is then molded in silicone for permanent casts. Don't make the actor come back.
- Whole bodies: cast in parts; fill in with mannequin parts; cast only what shows (H 286–290).
- **Large life molds** (W 102–103): the model needs stamina, the mold must come apart in registered pieces quickly, and big gently curved areas of bandage are flimsy and need **timber reinforcement** just to survive removal. Alginate is impractical except for well-supported poses; mold dynamic poses in plaster bandage alone with plenty of petroleum jelly or baby oil. Models holding a pose under warm bandage gradually lose track of their muscle tension and tense up, cramp, or relax until they fall, so speed and support matter: **two teams of two plus a floating troubleshooter**, a warm private room, a castored table per team, and supports from a scaffold pole with adjustable crossbars to a padded, tilting ply board. Models can be cast naked (petroleum jelly or a soft white paraffin/mineral oil mix) or in greased clothes. **Rehearse the pose first** to plan the divisions and supports; pre-cut the wood strips and tape them on to check fit. Draw the divisions on the skin with greasepaint or lipstick. Build each seam as a **flange**: 3–4-layer bandage gathered concertina-fashion, pinched into a triangular wall along one side of the line, faced with a polished tool and oiled or soaped, then a matching wall built against it; fill each section's interior while the walls are still wet, and lash on 1" (2 cm) square timber with bandage. This is slightly slower than covering the model and cutting the mold with scissors, but the edges are stronger, mate on reassembly, and no blades come near the model. If the cast goes much below the shoulders, mold the head separately, registering it on a hard jawline (W 102–103).
- **Never encase body parts in plaster** (H 290). Plaster heats as it sets and can trap limbs: in 2007 a schoolgirl in England put both hands into a bucket of plaster; as it set it trapped them and exceeded 140°F (60°C) and she ultimately lost eight fingers (H 290). Apply plaster to skin only in thin layers with cooling time (bandages after the first layer), with petroleum jelly as release, hair protected — and only under experienced supervision.
- **Printed alternative.** B scans a face, sculpts a nose or mask on the scan, and prints it in flexible TPU or flexible PLA, attaching it with spirit gum (B 70–80; [ch. 40](40-3d-printing.md#level-4-makeup-prosthesis-or-mask-b-7080)). B also notes wax-model printers for lost-wax metal casting (B 109).

## Mold care (H 290)
Store molds in sealed tubs, unstacked; moisture, dust, sunlight, and air degrade them (H 290).

## For the classroom

- **Starter sequence (cheap → advanced):** (1) press objects into clay and pour plaster (Hart's first casting exercise, H 9); (2) two-piece plaster mold of a simple object with keys; (3) alginate hand cast → plaster positive; (4) brush-up silicone glove mold with plaster mother mold (only after silicone safety training); (5) water-based or low-toxicity casting resin.
- **Release matrix:** Students build a chart of mold/cast/release combinations from manufacturers' data sheets for materials your shop actually stocks.
- **Case discussion:** The 2007 plaster injury — what would a safe life-casting protocol look like in your program?
- **Undercut hunt.** Students examine five household objects and mark each of Wilson's three undercut types, then propose a waste, piece or flexible mold for each (W 86).
- **Life-casting rehearsal.** Before anyone is cast, teams rehearse Wilson's face procedure on a mannequin head: timing two alginate batches, the straw moment and the bandage jacket, with the "model" practising yes/no signals (W 99–102).
