# Wood Projects (projects for ch. 29)

Starter projects and quick references for [ch. 29](29-wood.md). Hart recommends learning woodworking by building one small, complete object, such as a birdhouse, rather than drilling isolated cuts (H 9). Steps marked **[General knowledge]** aren't from the guide's sources. Do the shop safety checkout in [ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md) and [ch. 21](../4-fabrication-core/21-shop-space-and-tools.md) first.

## Project 1: a birdhouse or lidded box (H 9) [General knowledge]

**Teaches:** measuring, square crosscuts, butt and rabbet joints, gluing and clamping, sanding, finishing.
1. Draw a front, side and top view with dimensions ([ch. 23](../4-fabrication-core/23-drawings-measuring-and-layout.md)) and write a **cut list**.
2. Cut the parts from ¾" pine or poplar. Label each part.
3. **Dry-fit** everything (H 118).
4. Glue long-grain to long-grain joints where possible; reinforce end-grain joints with brads or screws (H 112–113).
5. Glue both faces thinly, clamp until a small bead squeezes out, and leave clamped **at least 20 minutes**; don't stress it for 24 hours (H 111–112).
6. **Break the edges** and run your hands over the whole piece (H 121).
7. Sand, then finish ([ch. 28 recipes](../4-fabrication-core/28-painting-and-finishing-recipes.md)).

## Project 2: a rehearsal table (M 152–156)

**Teaches:** sheet goods, legs, aprons, quick sturdy construction.
1. Cut a plywood top.
2. Make legs from 4×4 or two laminated 2×4s (M 155).
3. Build a 1×4 apron under the top.
4. Join legs to the apron with glue and screws or bolted corner blocks (M 155).
5. Check it's square and level, and break all edges (H 121).

## Project 3: a "period" table with faked joinery (M 155; H 119–121)

**Teaches:** making paint-grade furniture look heavier and older than it is.
1. Build the table from butt joints, corner irons and bolted corner blocks (M 155).
2. Make the top look thick with an applied **nose** or reveal strip, or fill and paint the plywood edge (H 119–120).
3. Add surface-applied wedges or tenons that read as through-joinery (H 120).
4. Add moldings, routered on the edge of a wide board and ripped off (H 127–128).
5. Finish and break down to suit the period ([ch. 27 recipes](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture-recipes.md)).

## Project 4: a dresser with one working drawer (H 117–119)

1. Build a plain carcass box from sheet goods (H 120–121).
2. Fit **one working drawer**, built so it can move to whichever opening the director chooses (H 117–119).
3. Fix the false drawer fronts as late as possible.
4. Layer moldings and panels to add detail, watching the cumulative width (H 120–121).

## Project 5: a curved form (H 107–110; W 39–40)

Make a curved panel several ways and compare: **bending plywood** (or 1.5 mm skin ply) glued and nailed or stapled over ply formers (**stressed skin**), **kerfing** a solid board, **laminating** thin strips over a form, and **staving** (battens sandwiched between two thin skins) (H 107–110; W 39). Note how many layers each needs to hold its shape, how much it springs back off the form, and how long each took. Wilson expects cutting from solid and stressed skin to be fastest (W 40).

## Project 6: a band-sawn cabriole leg (W 33)

**Teaches:** turning a two-view drawing into a 3D part on the band saw.
1. Make a full-size template of the leg profile.
2. Trace the profile onto **two adjacent faces** of square stock, both facing the same way (W 33).
3. Cut the first profile on the band saw and **save the offcuts** (W 33).
4. Tape the offcuts back in place so the block is square again, then cut the second profile (W 33).
5. Remove the offcuts. Refine the shape with rasps, then sand (W 27, 33).

## Project 7: anti-raking a table (W 37–39)

**Teaches:** rake geometry; fitting furniture to a sloped stage.
1. Get the rake's slope and the direction of steepest slope (at right angles to any line joining two points of equal height). Find the table's intended position and angle from the director or designer, and do this step as late as possible: the table will be level only in that orientation (W 36–37).
2. On the floor or a scale drawing, draw the direction of slope and a **base line** at right angles to it (W 37).
3. Place the table, or mark its leg positions, so the **most downhill leg sits on the base line** at the intended angle (W 38).
4. Measure each leg's shortest (perpendicular) distance to the base line (W 38).
5. **Multiply each distance by the tangent of the rake angle**, or divide it by the second number of the ratio, to get the amount to cut off that leg (W 39). Example: a leg 800 mm from the base line on a 1 in 8 rake loses 100 mm.
6. Cut the legs, set the table on the rake, and check the top with a level.
7. If a leg must be lengthened instead, fit a steel-tube extension with a wooden floor plug ([ch. 29](29-wood.md#furniture-on-raked-stages-w-3639)) (W 38).

## Project 8: a turned table leg with a pommel (W 45–49)

**Teaches:** lathe set-up, roughing, sizing slots, skew work, sanding.
1. Prepare square stock; mark the centres at each end and saw shallow cuts across the headstock end so the spur bites. Mount between centres; spin by hand to check clearance (W 44–46).
2. Pencil a line round the work where the square pommel will end. Cut it in with the **skew's long point** using alternating knife and relieving cuts (W 46–47).
3. **Rough** the rest to a cylinder with the roughing gouge at low speed, working in both directions (W 46).
4. Raise the speed. Cut **sizing slots** at key points with a parting tool or bedan, stopping when calipers set to each diameter slip over (W 47).
5. Shape between the slots: plane tapers and convex curves with the **skew**, cutting downhill; scoop coves with a spindle gouge (W 47–48).
6. Add details (beads, fillets) with the skew or a form tool; use a steady or gloved hand if the work starts to whip (W 49).
7. Sand on the lathe (120–180 then 240–320 grit), tool rest moved away, wearing goggles and a mask. Apply shellac on the lathe if time allows (W 49).
8. For a second, matching leg, repeat the same slot positions and depths (W 47).

## Quick reference

**Board feet** (H 123–125): length × width × thickness, in inches, ÷ 144.

**Nominal vs. actual sizes**: see the table in [ch. 29](29-wood.md#buying-wood-h-122126) (a 2×4 is 1½ × 3½).

**Joint strength by grain** (H 112–113): end-to-end weakest; end-to-long moderate; long-to-long strongest.

**Milling rough lumber** (H 123–125): jointer one face → planer the other → jointer one edge → crosscut one end → rip to width → cut to length.

**Rake conversions** (W 37): tangent of rake angle = 1 ÷ *n* for a "1 in *n*" ratio = percentage ÷ 100. 1 in 8 = 12.5% = 7.125°; 1 in 5 = 20% = 11.3°.

**Lathe speeds** (W 42): about 300 rpm for roughing large work, up to about 2,000 rpm for slender spindles. **Glued-up turning blanks** (W 41): cure 24 h for epoxy, 48 h for PVA before turning.

**Sheet sizes** (W 22): standard 2,440 × 1,220 mm (8' × 4'); British skin ply 1,525 × 1,525 mm.
