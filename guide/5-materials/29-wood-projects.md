# Wood Projects (projects for ch. 29)

Starter projects and quick references for [ch. 29](29-wood.md). Hart recommends learning woodworking by building one small, complete object, such as a birdhouse, rather than drilling isolated cuts (H 9). Steps marked **[General knowledge]** aren't from the guide's sources. Do the shop safety checkout in [ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md) and [ch. 21](../4-fabrication-core/21-shop-space-and-tools.md) first.

## Project 1: a birdhouse or lidded box (H 9) [General knowledge]

**Teaches:** measuring, square crosscuts, butt and rabbet joints, gluing and clamping, sanding, finishing.
1. Draw a front, side and top view with dimensions ([ch. 23](../4-fabrication-core/23-drawings-measuring-and-layout.md)) and write a **cut list**.
2. Cut the parts from ¾" pine or poplar. Label each part.
3. **Dry-fit** everything (H 118).
4. Glue long-grain to long-grain joints where possible; reinforce end-grain joints with brads or screws (H 112–113).
5. Glue both faces thinly, clamp until a small bead squeezes out, and leave clamped **at least 20 minutes**; don't stress it for 24 hours (H 111–112).
6. **Break the edges** and run your hands over the whole piece (H 121).
7. Sand, then finish ([ch. 28 recipes](../4-fabrication-core/28-painting-and-finishing-recipes.md)).

## Project 2: a rehearsal table (M 152–156)

**Teaches:** sheet goods, legs, aprons, quick sturdy construction.
1. Cut a plywood top.
2. Make legs from 4×4 or two laminated 2×4s (M 155).
3. Build a 1×4 apron under the top.
4. Join legs to the apron with glue and screws or bolted corner blocks (M 155).
5. Check it's square and level, and break all edges (H 121).

## Project 3: a "period" table with faked joinery (M 155; H 119–121)

**Teaches:** making paint-grade furniture look heavier and older than it is.
1. Build the table from butt joints, corner irons and bolted corner blocks (M 155).
2. Make the top look thick with an applied **nose** or reveal strip, or fill and paint the plywood edge (H 119–120).
3. Add surface-applied wedges or tenons that read as through-joinery (H 120).
4. Add moldings, routered on the edge of a wide board and ripped off (H 127–128).
5. Finish and break down to suit the period ([ch. 27 recipes](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture-recipes.md)).

## Project 4: a dresser with one working drawer (H 117–119)

1. Build a plain carcass box from sheet goods (H 120–121).
2. Fit **one working drawer**, built so it can move to whichever opening the director chooses (H 117–119).
3. Fix the false drawer fronts as late as possible.
4. Layer moldings and panels to add detail, watching the cumulative width (H 120–121).

## Project 5: a curved form (H 107–110)

Make a curved panel three ways and compare: **bending plywood** over formers, **kerfing** a solid board, and **laminating** thin strips over a form. Note how many layers each needs to hold its shape.

## Quick reference

**Board feet** (H 123–125): length × width × thickness, in inches, ÷ 144.

**Nominal vs. actual sizes**: see the table in [ch. 29](29-wood.md#buying-wood-h-122126) (a 2×4 is 1½ × 3½).

**Joint strength by grain** (H 112–113): end-to-end weakest; end-to-long moderate; long-to-long strongest.

**Milling rough lumber** (H 123–125): jointer one face → planer the other → jointer one edge → crosscut one end → rip to width → cut to length.
