# Molding and Casting Projects (projects for ch. 38)

Starter projects and quick references for [ch. 38](38-molding-and-casting.md), cheap to advanced. Hart's first casting exercise is a plaster press mold (H 9). **Read each product's data sheet and SDS first**; polyurethanes in particular need serious ventilation (H 278–280). Steps marked **[General knowledge]** aren't from the guide's sources.

## Project 1: a plaster press mold (H 9, 271–274)

**Teaches:** plaster mixing, release, positives and negatives.
1. Roll out a slab of oil clay about 1" thick in a small box.
2. Press objects (shells, keys, buttons, leaves) into the clay to make impressions.
3. Mix plaster: water at 70–100°F in a flexible bucket; **sift plaster into the water**, let it slake 1–2 minutes, mix 2–5 minutes without whipping in air; tap and skim (H 271–274).
4. Pour into the box over the clay.
5. Demold when cool (about 15–30 minutes). The plaster now has raised positives of each object.
6. **Never pour plaster down a drain** (H 271–274).

## Project 2: a two-piece silicone box mold (H 260–265, 283–284)

**Teaches:** parting lines, registration keys, release between halves.
1. Seal a porous master ([ch. 38](38-molding-and-casting.md#release-agents-h-265267)). **No shellac under silicone** (H 267).
2. Embed the master halfway in an oil-clay bed at the seam line. Build walls around it.
3. Carve **registration keys** into the clay (H 260–265).
4. Mix silicone exactly to ratio, measuring each part in its own cup and combining in a third (H 267, 284).
5. Pour in a thin, high stream to one spot and let it flow (H 269–271).
6. When cured, flip it, remove the clay, and **apply release to the silicone**: silicone sticks to silicone (H 260–265).
7. Pour the second half. Cut a sprue and vents to the highest points (H 269–271).
8. Demold, and cast plaster, resin or more silicone in it.

## Project 3: a brush-up glove mold with a mother mold (H 260–265, 274)

**Teaches:** saving expensive rubber on big masters.
1. Brush a thin beauty coat of rubber over the master.
2. Build up thicker coats, colouring alternate layers to judge thickness.
3. Divide with clay **fences** for a multi-piece mold if needed.
4. Build a **mother mold** of plaster bandage over the rubber (H 274).
5. Demold the mother mold, then peel the rubber glove off the master.

## Project 4: casting a hand in alginate (H 286–289)

**Teaches:** life casting safely.
1. Fill a container with ½" clearance around the hand.
2. Mix alginate with warm water; the actor sits and holds the hand posed about 10 minutes.
3. The actor wiggles to break the suction and withdraws the hand.
4. **Cast immediately** (usually plaster), because alginate keeps shrinking. Don't make the actor come back (H 286–289). If you must delay, wrap the mold in a damp cloth and a plastic bag and refrigerate (W 98).
5. **Never encase body parts in plaster** (H 290).

## Project 5: a quick silicone-caulk mold (H 284–285)

For a temporary, cheap mold: knead clear 100% silicone caulk with glycerin and a little acrylic paint, handling it with soapy gloves. Press it on in layers and let it cure 24–48 hours. Results are inconsistent, so use it only for short runs (H 284–285).

## Project 6: a scrimmed plaster mold of a clay mask (W 87–90)

**Teaches:** beauty coat, scrim reinforcement, strong edges, clay removal. (Wilson's one-piece waste/working mold for a mask modelled on a board; the same layering applies to the case of the silicone skin mold in project 7.)
1. Leave at least **50 mm** of ply baseboard round the clay; paint the exposed ply with thin clay slip so plaster won't stick. Release on the clay is optional: an aerosol wax helps clean release; **soap destroys fine detail** (W 87).
2. Cut enough jute scrim to cover the work with about 50 mm overlap all round (W 88).
3. **First coat:** a small batch of plaster as thick as double cream, flicked, poured or brushed on in a thin layer that takes the detail without trapping air and keeps the scrim from showing inside. Scrape excess off the board, leaving about a 30 mm margin (W 88).
4. **Second coat:** without delay, a larger, thicker mix spread over the first. Lay scrim pieces one at a time into the **wet** plaster and work them in until fully wetted, except the overlap beyond the edge. Always put scrim into wet plaster; mix fresh as needed. (This is faster than the traditional dipping of scrim in a bowl.) (W 88)
5. As the plaster starts to go off, **turn the overlaps back** into wet plaster to make a strong edge. Scrape excess off the board as each mix sets (W 88–89).
6. One layer of scrim is usually enough for a mask; larger or flatter molds may need two. Finish with a thin smooth coat (W 89).
7. Wait for the plaster to **heat up and cool down** before disturbing it; then turn it over and pull it off the board (W 89).
8. Hollow out the clay with a loop tool or wire, stopping 1–2 cm short of the mold face; the rest should peel out in big pieces. Wash out the last bits (W 90).
9. Dry at least **48 hours** at room temperature before casting anything that needs a dry mold (W 90).

## Project 7: a silicone skin mold with a plaster case (W 95–97)

**Teaches:** brush-on thixotropic silicone; building a rigid case.
1. Roll out soft modelling clay, cut it into strips, and build a **low wall** round the original along the mold's edge (W 95).
2. Mix silicone with its catalyst and a **thixotropic agent**. Brush or spatula it over the original and up the clay wall, avoiding trapped bubbles and keeping the thickness even right to the edge. Add another layer while the first is still tacky if needed; Wilson's example is about **8 mm** thick (W 97).
3. When cured, pull the clay wall away, trim the ragged rubber edge, and **replace the wall a few centimetres further out** (W 97).
4. Apply neat plaster right to the clay wall, press in scrim, fold overlaps back into wet plaster for a neat, strong edge, and smooth another layer over so the scrim is hidden (W 97).
5. When the plaster has gone off, remove the clay wall and tidy the case edge with a knife or rasp (W 97).

## Project 8: a life mold of a face (W 99–102)

**Teaches:** working fast and safely on a person. Do this only under experienced supervision; Hart's warning about never encasing body parts in plaster applies ([ch. 38](38-molding-and-casting.md#molding-the-human-body-h-286290)) (H 290).

**Have ready before the model arrives** (W 99):
- a comfortable chair with working room around it, ideally a headrest so the face can tilt up, and a table for materials
- a towel round the neck, cover for clothes, and a hair band or swimming cap
- pre-measured alginate and water for **two batches of about 250 g mixed** (three if the neck is included), with mixing and spreading tools, and an assistant to mix
- 10–15 lengths of plaster bandage about 25 cm long, and a bowl to be filled with **hot** water just before you start (faster set)
- nostril **straws** rolled from small squares of paper and taped, 3–4 cm long, wide enough to fit securely without distorting the nose
- moisturizer or cold cream (helps alginate release from dry skin) and paper towels

**Procedure:**
1. Explain each stage and how long it takes; agree a **yes/no signal**. Check the model breathes freely through the nose; if not, **postpone**. Seat and drape them, test the straws, apply cream (W 99).
2. Mix the first batch until lump-free. Spread a big helping up each jawline from chin to ear, then the cheeks to just below the eyes and round the mouth and nose, catching it as it sags (W 99).
3. **Warn the model**, then cover the mouth: a spatula-load in the middle spread firmly into each corner to push out air, covering the top lip up to the base of the nose (W 99).
4. Have the assistant start the second batch. **Now** insert the straws (ideally the model does it with clean hands); in place earlier, they stop you covering the mouth properly. Work alginate carefully round the nostrils and straws; a plastic teaspoon handle helps (W 101).
5. With the second batch, thicken the edge at the jawline; then, after a warning, cover each eye firmly so no air is trapped in the inner corners; finish the forehead with a **thick edge**, about 1 cm, since thin edges tear and dry fast (W 99, 101).
6. As soon as the alginate stops sagging, apply 3–4 layers of plaster bandage, starting at the edges and reinforcing them; don't try to cover every patch round the nose, since the bandage only needs to cradle the alginate. Leave one wet scrap on the bowl rim to show when the plaster has set (W 100–101).
7. Wait 2–3 minutes, **talking to the model throughout** (W 101).
8. Remove the straws with a quick downward tug. Ask the model to move their face to break the vacuum while you pull the mold away near the ears, pivoting it down to free the nose. If hair is caught at the hairline or temples, ease it out by working your fingers behind the mold, and use more cream or petroleum jelly next time (W 101–102).
9. **Cast immediately** or wrap the mold in polythene to slow drying. Block the straw holes with pellets of plaster bandage before casting (W 102).

## Project 9: a slush-cast latex mask (W 117–119)

**Teaches:** absorption casting in an unsealed plaster mold.
1. Use a clean, leak-free, **unsealed** plaster mold, made with soap release rather than petroleum jelly or wax so the face still absorbs (W 117).
2. Set it firmly with its top edge level, propped with wedges of fresh clay if irregular (W 117).
3. Wearing goggles, stir the latex thoroughly in its tin and pour carefully into the mold; adjust the level just before it's full, using the latex surface as a guide. Plug any leaks with wet clay (W 117).
4. Leave it while a wall of latex builds up; the level drops slightly as the mold absorbs water, and you'll see the wall thickness at the rim (W 117).
5. At the thickness you want, **pour the latex back into the tin** in one swift, bold movement from the best-pouring point of the rim (the messiest step: goggles on). Leave the mold propped upside down on the tin to drain, or a puddle forms at the deepest point. Don't touch the deposit (W 117).
6. Leave somewhere warm overnight (longer for thick casts). Test by pressing near the bottom: any softness means wet latex under a dry skin (W 119).
7. When fully dry, dust the inside with talc so fresh surfaces don't stick together, and pull the cast out; fully dried latex withstands a lot of tugging out of undercuts (W 119).

## Quick reference: release and mixing

**Resin into a plaster mold** (H 266, 279): seal the dry plaster (shellac, lacquer or paint) → wax → dust with cornstarch → PVA release.

**No release for absorption casting** in plaster: it clogs the pores (H 268–269).

**Two-part mixing** (H 267, 284): measure each part in its own cup, combine in a third; tare containers when weighing; mix exact ratios.

**Plaster for molds** (W 87): about 1 part water to 2–2½ parts plaster by volume; faster with hot water or up to 10 g salt per litre; slower with a few ml trisodium citrate solution per litre (doubles the set) or glue size.

**Gelatin mold material** (W 97): glue size or pearl glue soaked overnight, drained, melted in a double boiler, plus antifreeze equal to the dry glue's volume. Pour hot; remelts.

**White metal** (W 123): pour about 60°C above the melting point (240°C alloy → about 300°C); molds bone dry; talc or graphite dust.

**Fighting bubbles** (H 269–271): sprue to the highest point; vents as high as the sprue; pour a thin, high stream to one spot; tap and vibrate; vacuum-degas viscous materials.

**Cure inhibitors for silicone** (H 283–284): sulfur clay, latex (even latex gloves), shellac.

**Shore hardness** (H 286): OO very soft, A flexible, D rigid. The scales don't convert.
