# 31. Plastics

> **Sources:** H ch.9 (pp. 161–181), ch.13 (pp. 252–254), ch.12 (p. 236)
> **[Check current]** Brand names, formulations and 3D-printing materials change quickly. Thermoforming temperatures are starting points; follow the manufacturer's data. Heating plastics releases toxic fumes, so ventilate.

## Vocabulary (H 162–163)

- A **polymer** is a long chain of repeating units, a "beaded necklace" of monomers.
- **Natural polymers** include latex, shellac, casein, horn, wool and cellulose.
- A **resin** is any liquid that hardens. All plastics start as resins, which become sheets, emulsions (varnish, PVA glue, acrylic paint), or casting and laminating resins.
- **Thermoplastics** soften every time they're heated and can be reshaped (acrylic, styrene, ABS, PVC, PETG, Kydex). **Thermosets** cure once and can't be re-softened (epoxy, cured casting resins).
- **Copolymers** are joined chemically (ABS). **Alloys** are blended mechanically (Kydex is acrylic plus PVC). Additives such as plasticizers, colorants, fillers and flame retardants change properties.

### Buying (H 167)
Hobby, model and craft stores carry small thin sheets. Home centers carry some acrylic and polycarbonate. **Plastics fabricators** (often hidden and poorly advertised) sell full 4'×8' sheets, cut to size cheaply. That can save hundreds of dollars over shipping large sheets. **Film** is 0.5–10 mil (1 mil = 0.001"); anything thicker is **sheet**.

## Cutting (H 168–169)

- **Thin styrene**: score with a knife and snap.
- **Acrylic under about ¼"**: **scribe and snap**. Score four or five times along a straightedge, then snap over a dowel or a table edge. Needs about 1½" of width.
- **Saws**:
  - Use fine finishing or veneer blades: triple-chip grind, 10" blades with about 80 teeth for ¼" stock or about 60 for thicker, rake 0 to +5°.
  - Band saw: 10–15 TPI. Jigsaw: ≥14 TPI on thick stock, about 32 TPI under ⅛".
  - Don't use blades already dulled by metal.
- **Prevent chatter**, which cracks and shatters plastic. Use zero-clearance inserts, clamp the work, cut close to a support, or sandwich thin sheet between plywood.
- **Feed rate**: too fast chips; too slow melts. **Chip welding** is when melted chips re-fuse the kerf behind the blade. Cool with compressed air or a plastic-safe lubricant (wax, mineral oil, soapy water).

## Drilling and machining (H 169–170)

- **Plastic drill bits** have a sharper point (60–90°) and 0–4° rake. Standard bits grab and crack. Without plastic bits, drill slowly.
- **Back the work** with sacrificial MDF so the exit stays clean. **Peck-drill** thick stock.
- Machining acrylic is like machining brass. Use detergent-water coolant only, because solvents attack the plastic.
- **Router**: sharp bits (≥½"), maximum RPM, the right feed speed, and several shallow passes.

## Joining (H 170–172)

- **Screws and bolts** can crack plastic. Drill clearance holes, use washers or flat heads, and **oversize the holes** for thermal expansion under stage lights or sun.

### Solvent welding (H 170–171)
- **Weldable**: acrylic, polycarbonate, styrene and ABS, PVC.
- **Not weldable**: polyethylene (LDPE, HDPE, UHMW), polypropylene, Teflon, acetal/Delrin, nylon.
- **Test**: dab acetone or MEK on a scrap. If it turns tacky, it can be solvent-welded.
- **Water-thin cements** need tight, clean (unpolished) joints. Apply from a **needle bottle** and let capillary action draw the cement in. Clamp snug, not crushing. Drips mar the surface.
- **Bodied cements** fill small gaps.
- **Cure**: don't handle for 24 hours. Full strength takes days, so ignore "20-minute set" claims.
- Solvents absorb through skin and harm with the first breath. Wear the correct gloves and respirator.

### Heat welding (H 171)
- Hot-air welders with matching rod (PE, PP, PVC, ABS).
- Impulse sealers for films.
- Speed-tip welders.
- **Laser-welded** acrylic joints are invisible, and fabricators offer them.

**Other options:** CA works well on acrylic; double-stick tape for face-to-face joints; contact cement and two-part adhesives for bonding plastic to other materials (H 171–172).

## Heat forming (H 172–174)

- Most thermoplastics form around 300°F. That's close to where some begin to decompose (HIPS forms at about 325°F and decomposes as low as about 390°F). The fumes are a mix no cartridge is rated for, so **use the minimum heat and extract the fumes** (H 172).
- **Starting temperatures (°F, H 172)**:
  - acrylic 240–340
  - HIPS 325–350
  - ABS 300–350
  - Kydex 350–365
  - polycarbonate 350–425
  - Sintra about 300
  - PETG 230–350
  - CAB 335–390
  - polyethylene 325–425
- **Strip heater** for straight bends. Heat the *outside* of the bend, and heat both sides for sheet over ¼". **Heat gun** for irregular shapes and rods; it can burn through in seconds. A **dedicated oven** for whole sheets, never used for food again (H 172–173).
- **Thick sharp bends**: cut a V-groove ¾ of the way through on the inside, bend, then seal it with water-thin cement (H 173).
- **Hold the shape while it cools** with jigs, formers and clamps, because the plastic springs back. Cover formers with cardstock, since hot plastic picks up texture. Drape over a pipe for a simple radius. **Press forming** uses matched male and female forms (H 173–174).

KF heat-bends **PVC pipe** for curved grips, bows and staffs with a heat gun, keeping it moving and filling the pipe with sand for tight bends (KF 16). See [ch. 33](33-eva-foam.md#heat-bending-pvc-pipe-kf-16), and its source-disagreement table on fume protection.

## Vacuum forming (H 174–175)

Heated sheet is pulled over a **buck** (positive "drape" forming) or into a female mold. The vacuum sucks it tight until it cools. Results are **one-sided, with no undercuts**.

- **Machine parts**:
  - a **platen** perforated with holes over a vacuum (even a shop vac works)
  - a **frame** that clamps the sheet and travels straight down
  - a **heater** above, or a dedicated oven
- **Details** end up on the inside and are softened by sheet thickness. Exaggerate them, or accept the soft "mass-produced" look.
- **Sheet**: HIPS is the beginner favorite. 0.06" for general use, about 0.08" for torso armor. **Tall bucks thin the sheet**, so use thicker stock for them.
- **Bucks** must survive heat and vacuum: rigid PU foam, plaster, fiberglass, wood or aluminum. Coat foam and clay bucks.
- Run the vacuum until the sheet has cooled. Dust the buck with talc and pop parts off with compressed air.
- **Webbing** at sharp corners: raise the objects, push the plastic with a stick while it's soft, keep the webbing in offcut areas, or spot-reheat with a heat gun (H 178).

## Clear plastics (H 175–179)

- **Keep the protective film on** through fabrication. Mark on it, and peel only where you heat, bend or glue.
- Remove old masking with kerosene or WD-40 and a soft plastic scraper, then wash with soap.
- **Never clean with ammonia** (glass cleaners). Use soap and water or plastic cleaners.
- **Scratches**:
  - Light ones you can't feel with a fingernail come out with plastic polish or toothpaste.
  - Deeper ones need wet-sanding through grits (about 150 → 320 → 400 → 600 → 800), then light buffing with plastic compound.
  - Polycarbonate scratches can't be sanded out.
- **Clear edges**: scrape off saw marks with a razor held perpendicular (a "dressed" edge), wet-sand up to about 600 grit, then buff with muslin and flannel wheels and fine compound. **Always wet-sand plastic.**

## 3D printing note [check current]

Hart (2013) mentions ABS extrusion printing as emerging technology (H 10, 58, 165). Today, PLA and PETG desktop printing and resin printing are common in prop shops. Resin printing brings its own skin-contact and ventilation hazards. Treat printed parts as **one more material**, chosen through the needs/means process ([ch. 22](../4-fabrication-core/22-the-build-method.md)), and finish them like any other plastic ([ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md)).

For filaments in detail (PLA, ABS, PETG, nylon and TPU temperatures, properties and ventilation), infill, finishing and troubleshooting, see [ch. 40](40-3d-printing.md#filament-and-other-materials-b-1215) (B 12–22).

## For the classroom

- **Vacuum-forming lab.** Students sculpt or assemble bucks and pull HIPS over them, learning about undercuts, webbing and detail loss firsthand.
- **Heat-bending exercise.** Students make a set of acrylic brackets to identical dimensions using a strip heater and a shop-made jig.
- **EVA armor piece.** Students pattern, cut, heat-form and hot-glue a vambrace or pauldron, then seal and paint it ([ch. 28](../4-fabrication-core/28-painting-and-finishing.md)).
- **Solvent test.** Students identify unknown plastic scraps using the acetone tack test, flexibility, and the recycling codes on the material.
