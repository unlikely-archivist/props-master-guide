# Sculpting Projects (projects for ch. 37)

Starter projects for [ch. 37](37-sculpting.md). Hart suggests learning sculpting by carving soap first (H 9). Foam-specific carving is also in [Foam Projects](32-foams-and-rubbers-projects.md). Steps marked **[General knowledge]** aren't from the guide's sources.

## Project 1: a soap carving (H 9) [General knowledge]

**Teaches:** subtractive thinking, working from profiles, roughing before detail.
1. Choose a simple subject (a fish, a bird, an animal head) and draw its front and side profiles at the size of the soap bar.
2. Transfer the profiles to two faces of the bar.
3. Rough out one profile with a knife, then the other (H 242–243).
4. **Rough the overall shape before any detail** (H 242).
5. Refine with smaller tools; add detail last.
6. Smooth with a damp finger.

## Project 2: an oil-clay maquette on an armature (H 239–245)

**Teaches:** additive modelling and armatures.
1. Gather reference: front, side and top views, and photos from all sides (H 241).
2. Bend **armature wire**, spiral-wrapped with fine wire so clay grips, and fix it to a base (H 243–244). For a life-size head, Wilson screws a 50 × 75 mm upright into an 18 mm ply base, then either screws on two crosspieces or fixes two loops of square aluminium armature wire with hose clips and packs clay between them; paint or varnish the wood so the clay sticks (W 81, 83).
3. Bulk out with crumpled foil bound with tape (H 244).
4. Build up oil clay in small balls, not one big lump (H 243).
5. Block in proportions, then refine with loop and ribbon tools, then add surface detail (H 242, 246–249).
6. Use it as a reference for a full-size build, or as a master for a mould ([Molding and Casting Projects](38-molding-and-casting-projects.md)).

## Project 3: a slice-method foam sculpture (H 243–244)

Build a 2–3 ft figure from plywood profiles and slices filled with foam, carved flush and coated. Steps are in [Foam Projects](32-foams-and-rubbers-projects.md#project-2-a-slice-method-foam-figure-h-243244).

## Project 4: hard-over-soft detail (H 245–246)

1. Carve a rough form in XPS foam.
2. Skin it with a thin layer of auto-body filler ([ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md#fillers-h-308311)).
3. Carve crisp details in the filler when it's "cheese-hard" (H 245–246).
4. Sand, prime and paint.

## Project 5: salt-dough or polymer-clay details (H 250–251)

1. Model small details (fruit, bread, jewellery, olives).
2. Salt dough: air-dry 2–3 days or bake low; browning means too hot. Seal it (H 250–251). FF's salt-dough recipes and timings are in [ch. 41](41-prop-food.md#salt-dough-ff-2022-h-250251-bakers-clay).
3. Polymer clay: bake in a **dedicated oven, never used for food again** (H 250). Bake about 15–30 min per 6 mm thickness at 130°C, no hotter, checking with an oven thermometer; don't use it for food or drink props (W 76).

## Project 6: a clay helmet with pressed texture (W 72–73)

**Teaches:** formers for geometry, fairing curves, applied detail, sealing for a mould.
1. Cut a hardboard former that sets the geometry of the brim; build a rough clay mass on it (W 72).
2. Roll a dowel gently over the surfaces to **fair** the curves (W 72).
3. Roll out clay and cut strips and shapes for applied detail (W 73).
4. Press a scrap of **lace** into the surface with the rod to suggest engraving (W 73).
5. Paint with **shellac** to seal the clay and allow finer surfaces (talc has a similar effect) (W 73). Then mould it ([Molding and Casting Projects](38-molding-and-casting-projects.md)). Note that shellac inhibits silicone, so choose the mould material accordingly (H 267).

## Project 7: a band-sawn polystyrene rough (W 133–136)

**Teaches:** turning a 3D carving into 2D cuts. Wilson's example is an armadillo in extruded polystyrene.
1. Choose the view with the most landmarks (here, the plan view) and draw it on the top of the block to scale (W 134).
2. Cut it square through the block on the band saw, keep the offcuts, and tape them back on (W 133–134).
3. Draw the side view on the side face, registered to the first, and cut it (W 133).
4. Mark the biggest differences from the reference with a marker; then round the sections boldly with a knife, square → octagon → 16 sides (W 135).
5. Smooth with sandpaper, keeping it clear of loose beads (W 127, 133).
6. Coat it before painting ([ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md)); Wilson's armadillo got polymer plaster dusted with talc and impressed with texture (W 140).

## Reclaiming clay (W 73–74)

Recycling clay saves waste and is said to improve its working; it gets harder the drier the clay (W 73).
- **Small amounts:** break or cut used clay into small pieces, picking out plaster and debris, and put them in a bucket with saved waste. Add about a cupful of water per 5 kg (if not too dry) with a little disinfectant. Leave overnight, then knead and repeatedly lift and slam it on the bench (W 73).
- **Large amounts or dry clay:** break it into buckets or a bin no more than three-quarters full, cover completely with water and fill to the top. Soak a few days, siphon or bail off the clear water, and mix to a thick slurry with a small spade. Spread on plywood trays for a few days, turning daily, until firm. Put it through a **pugmill** if possible (it's hard to get new-clay smoothness without one), otherwise knead it by hand or spade (hard work). Store in heavy polythene bags, sealed with the air pressed out (W 73–74).

## Recipe cards

| Recipe | Ingredients | Method and notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Wilson's oil clay** (W 75) | 2 kg microcrystalline wax; 700 g petroleum jelly; 800 g white mineral oil such as baby oil (about 1 L); 5 kg dry powdered clay (ball clay or white china clay) | Melt wax, jelly and oil together in a bucket **standing in a larger container of boiling water** (molten wax is flammable; never direct heat). Stir in the clay thoroughly. Turn out into plastic tubs or damp plaster molds. **Don't use paraffin wax** (big brittle crystals make it crumbly). Some beeswax with the microcrystalline gives a more flexible, less crack-prone clay at higher cost; adding a very plastic clay such as bentonite gives a softer, finer product |
| **Modelling plaster** (W 78) | 1 kg alpha plaster; 350 g water; 0–30 g trisodium citrate solution (10 parts water : 1 part citrate); 50 g colloidal silica | Working time 10 min to 2 hr depending on retarder. Halve the silica and add a little paper pulp or sawdust as a variation. Also a covering for polystyrene |
| **Paper pulp** (W 79) | Newspaper or brown paper; water; optional 20 ml bleach per litre; paste | Tear the paper small, cover with lots of water, boil and simmer 1 hr (bleach helps it break down). Rinse well to remove ink and bleach. Break it down further: add water to make a soup and beat with an electric whisk or a plaster-mixing paddle on a drill, or squeeze it out and pound it in a metal bucket with stout timber. Squeeze out as much water as possible and mix with paste (wallpaper paste plus a filler reduces clumping) |

## Quick reference

## Quick reference

**Choosing a sculpting material**: see the materials table in [ch. 37](37-sculpting.md#materials).

**Polystyrene safety** (H 243; M 143–144; W 131): hot-wiring releases styrene; ventilate and use the right respirator. Lowest wire temperature that cuts; never let it glow.

**Clay lubricants** (W 72, 74): talc is best for natural clay (dry, temporary); talc, oil, petroleum jelly or soap for oil clay, in moderation.
