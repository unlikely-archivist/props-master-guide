# 34. Fabric and Upholstery

> **Sources:** H ch.11 (pp. 198–224); M ch.5 (pp. 67–69), ch.11 (pp. 160–161)
> **[Check current]** Fabric prices ($2 to $200+ per yard, H 220), widths and brands vary. Flame-retardancy requirements are local ([ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md#fire-safety-h-4244-m-149151)).

Props uses fabric for upholstery, drapery, linens, bags, flags, tents, dead animals, and much more (H 199).

## Textile basics (H 199–200)

- **Woven**: warp (lengthwise) crossed by weft. Woven fabric has a **grain**, tears straight (plain weave tears easiest, satin hardest), barely stretches on the grain, and **stretches on the bias** (45°).
- **Knit**: interlocking loops; stretchy. Hand knitting can make seamless 3D shapes.
- **Nonwoven**: e.g., **felt** (matted fibers, up to 2" thick), used as padding against skin or between casters and furniture.
- **Selvage** is the factory edge. The crosswise grain runs selvage to selvage; the lengthwise grain runs along the bolt.
- **Right side vs. wrong side**: mark them with tape if they look alike. Designers sometimes choose the wrong side deliberately.

### Weaves and fabric types (H 222–224)
- **Plain/tabby** (chiffon, organza, taffeta; basketweave)
- **Twill** (denim, gabardine, tweed, herringbone, houndstooth): drapes better, resists wrinkles, front and back differ
- **Satin/sateen**: sheen; drapes easiest
- **Pile → nap** (velvet, velour): **directional**, so cut all pieces the same way or they'll look like different fabrics
- **Brocade** (multicolor pattern woven in, often metallic) and **damask** (pattern made by contrasting weaves): both common in upholstery and drapery
- **Lace**, **net**, braiding, crochet and macramé, which can also be glued onto rigid surfaces as texture

### Fibers (H 220–222)
| Fiber | Properties |
|---|---|
| **Cotton** (muslin, canvas, denim) | Little stretch; wrinkles; pins leave holes |
| **Linen (flax)** | Strong (stronger wet); wrinkles and can crack |
| **Jute/sisal (burlap), hemp, Manila** | Rustic sacks, natural rope |
| **Wool** (worsted vs. woolen) | Elastic; weaker wet; steam-shapeable |
| **Silk** | Strongest natural fiber; vivid dye colors; packs small (magic tricks, trick curtains). **Directors want silk flags for how they wave** |
| **Synthetics** | Polyester (also fiberfill and batting), acetate (heat-embossable), spandex, olefins, metallics, microfiber |

For period work, synthetics may not look or move like natural fibers. Consider every need: drape, movement in wind, stiffness, paintability, light blocking, strength and washability (H 220–221).

### Theatre fabrics (H 224; M 69)
- **Muslin**: cheap plain cotton for mock-ups, patterns and cloth-mâché.
- **Canvas** (plain or tighter **duck**): number grades run backward, so #12 is lighter than #4.
- **Duvetyn/commando cloth**: matte black, light-blocking. Use it to **skirt furniture** and hide casters.
- **Burlap** for rustic looks.
- **Scrim and gauze**; plaster gauze for mold jackets.
- **Bobbinet/tulle**: holds shape for underlayers.
- **Cheesecloth**, mostly for straining paint.
- **Sheers** for curtains.
- Also: brocade, chintz, felt, velour, velvet, vinyl and leatherette, ticking, batting, interfacing, rug liner, pillow forms (M 69).

## Buying fabric (H 200–201)

- Fabric's feel and drape (its **"hand"**) can't be judged online. Collect **swatches** labeled with type and price for designer approval, and photograph large patterns to show their scale and repeat.
- **Fabric math**:
  - Fabric is sold by the yard in widths of 40/54/60/72", up to 110". Recalculate if the store's width differs from what you planned.
  - Add seam allowances and hems, and respect grain and direction. You can't nest pieces like plywood.
  - **Add the pattern repeat** to each piece that must match.
  - **Railroaded** fabric runs the pattern along the length (selvages at top and bottom), useful for long sofa backs.
- **Sources**: fabric and craft stores, upholstery suppliers, theatrical suppliers (wide seamless muslin, canvas, scrim), and custom digital fabric printing.

## Cutting and sewing (H 201–212; M 160–161)

- **Cutting table**: at least 45" wide and 6–8' long, with a pinnable surface (cork, or Homosote wrapped in muslin or paper).
- Mark on the **wrong side** with tailor's chalk or pencil; some pens bleed through.
- **Fabric scissors are for fabric only.** Never borrow them for paper. Cut flat on the table. **Rotary cutters** need a self-healing mat and attention to your fingers.
- **Seam allowance** is typically ¼–⅝". Sew **right sides together**, so 3D objects are built inside-out. Pin perpendicular to the stitch line.
- **Press seams**: both sides, then open. Use a **tailor's ham** for curves.

### Seams (H 202–205)
- **Plain seam**.
- **Flat-felled**: strong, finished on both sides.
- **French**: encloses raw edges; good for sheers. **Mock French** for curves.
- **Clip and notch** allowances: trim outside corners diagonally, slit inside corners, V-notch concave curves, slit convex ones. Never cut the stitch.

### Machines (H 206–207)
- **Domestic** machines are versatile, for lighter fabrics.
- **Industrial** machines do one task fast and handle heavy layers.
- **Walking-foot** industrial machines feed top and bottom layers evenly and suit upholstery.
- Learn the parts: spool and bobbin, tension, stitch length and width, presser foot (always sew with it down), feed dogs, throat plate guides.
- **Tension**: too tight puckers or breaks the thread; too loose leaves a bird's nest underneath. **Test on a scrap of the same fabric.**

### Hand stitches (H 206–210; M 160–161)
You'll always need hand stitches: the last seam of a stuffed object, fabric attached to rigid parts, repairs onstage.
- **Running/basting**: quick joins, gathering, temporary seams.
- **Back stitch**: the strongest hand stitch, like a machine stitch.
- **Whip/overcast**: hems and raw edges.
- **Catch/cross stitch**: invisible, flexible hems.
- **Slip/ladder stitch**: invisible closure from the outside; easier with a curved needle.
- Lock off the thread with tiny perpendicular stitches.

### Edges (H 206, 210–212)
- **Hems**: rolled (napkins, delicate fabric), turned (most common), blind (nearly invisible).
- **Serging** (overlock) trims and binds an edge in one pass and can be decorative.
- Keep a **seam ripper** for mistakes.

## Upholstery: the props approach (H 212–217)

Hart covers building new upholstered pieces rather than full traditional reupholstery.

- **Build removable upholstered parts** (drop-in or slip seats, panels). The wood shop and soft-goods shop can then work in parallel, and either part can be redone later without damaging the other.
- **The sandwich**:
  1. **Plywood base**, thick enough that screws from below won't poke through.
  2. **Polyurethane foam**, traced from the base. Cut with a snap knife, an **electric knife** or a foam saw. Butt pieces tight. Glue foam with contact or barge cement, with ventilation, respirator and gloves.
  3. **Batting**: polyester ("Dacron") or cotton. Cut it, don't tear it. Wrap it to the back for a rounded edge, or stop at the edge for a crisp one.
  4. **Optional muslin layer**: holds the padding, sets the shape, and makes future reupholstery easier.
  5. **Upholstery fabric**: center the pattern; florals "grow" upward.
- **Stapling order**:
  1. Mark the centers of each side on base and fabric.
  2. Staple center front, then center back, center right, center left.
  3. Work outward toward the corners, keeping tension even.
  4. **Fold, trim and miter the corners**, keeping pleats close together, with any others underneath.
  5. Use **sub-staples** (partly driven) or pins while adjusting.
- **Staple guns**: pneumatic (strongest; fine staples for many layers), electric, hand; **long-nose** for recesses. A tack puller removes staples.
- **Cambric** dust cover underneath.
- **Visible edges** (H 215–217):
  - **gimp or trim** over the staples (glued or tacked)
  - **upholstery tacks / nailhead trim**
  - fabric-wrapped thin panels to cover clusters of staples
  - **piping/welt** (cord in a continuous bias strip, sewn with a piping foot)
- **Tufting** (button-tufted diamond, square and other patterns), **pleats** (pinch, French) and **gathering**.

## For the classroom

- **Pillowcase to cushion.** Start with Hart's pillowcase (H 9), then a boxed cushion with piping, building skills progressively.
- **Drop-in seat.** Each student upholsters a drop-in seat using the full sandwich, with mitered corners and a cambric underside.
- **Fabric math problems.** Given a sofa back, a 27" repeat and a choice of 54" or railroaded 110" goods, students calculate yardage.
- **Leather sampler.** Students saddle-stitch a small leather pouch or knife sheath.
