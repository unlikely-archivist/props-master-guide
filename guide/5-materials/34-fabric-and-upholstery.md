# 34. Fabric and Upholstery

> **Sources:** H ch.11 (pp. 198–224); M ch.5 (pp. 67–69), ch.11 (pp. 160–161); W ch.8 (pp. 142–155)
> **Companion:** [Fabric and Upholstery Projects](34-fabric-and-upholstery-projects.md): a pillowcase, a boxed cushion with piping, a recovered drop seat, deep buttoning, a bordered (feather-edged) seat, faux hide in calico.
> **[Check current]** Fabric prices ($2 to $200+ per yard, H 220), widths and brands vary. Flame-retardancy requirements are local ([ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md#fire-safety-h-4244-m-149151)).

Props uses fabric for upholstery, drapery, linens, bags, flags, tents, dead animals, and much more (H 199).

## Textile basics (H 199–200)

- **Woven**: warp (lengthwise) crossed by weft. Woven fabric has a **grain**, tears straight (plain weave tears easiest, satin hardest), barely stretches on the grain, and **stretches on the bias** (45°).
- **Knit**: interlocking loops; stretchy. Hand knitting can make seamless 3D shapes.
- **Nonwoven**: e.g., **felt** (matted fibers, up to 2" thick), used as padding against skin or between casters and furniture.
- **Selvage** is the factory edge. The crosswise grain runs selvage to selvage; the lengthwise grain runs along the bolt.
- **Right side vs. wrong side**: mark them with tape if they look alike. Designers sometimes choose the wrong side deliberately.

### Weaves and fabric types (H 222–224)
- **Plain/tabby** (chiffon, organza, taffeta; basketweave)
- **Twill** (denim, gabardine, tweed, herringbone, houndstooth): drapes better, resists wrinkles, front and back differ
- **Satin/sateen**: sheen; drapes easiest
- **Pile → nap** (velvet, velour): **directional**, so cut all pieces the same way or they'll look like different fabrics
- **Brocade** (multicolor pattern woven in, often metallic) and **damask** (pattern made by contrasting weaves): both common in upholstery and drapery
- **Lace**, **net**, braiding, crochet and macramé, which can also be glued onto rigid surfaces as texture

### Fibers (H 220–222)
| Fiber | Properties |
|---|---|
| **Cotton** (muslin, canvas, denim) | Little stretch; wrinkles; pins leave holes |
| **Linen (flax)** | Strong (stronger wet); wrinkles and can crack |
| **Jute/sisal (burlap), hemp, Manila** | Rustic sacks, natural rope |
| **Wool** (worsted vs. woolen) | Elastic; weaker wet; steam-shapeable |
| **Silk** | Strongest natural fiber; vivid dye colors; packs small (magic tricks, trick curtains). **Directors want silk flags for how they wave** |
| **Synthetics** | Polyester (also fiberfill and batting), acetate (heat-embossable), spandex, olefins, metallics, microfiber |

For period work, synthetics may not look or move like natural fibers. Consider every need: drape, movement in wind, stiffness, paintability, light blocking, strength and washability (H 220–221).

### Theatre fabrics (H 224; M 69)
- **Muslin**: cheap plain cotton for mock-ups, patterns and cloth-mâché.
- **Canvas** (plain or tighter **duck**): number grades run backward, so #12 is lighter than #4.
- **Duvetyn/commando cloth**: matte black, light-blocking. Use it to **skirt furniture** and hide casters.
- **Burlap** for rustic looks.
- **Scrim and gauze**; plaster gauze for mold jackets.
- **Bobbinet/tulle**: holds shape for underlayers.
- **Cheesecloth**, mostly for straining paint.
- **Sheers** for curtains.
- Also: brocade, chintz, felt, velour, velvet, vinyl and leatherette, ticking, batting, interfacing, rug liner, pillow forms (M 69).

## Buying fabric (H 200–201)

- Fabric's feel and drape (its **"hand"**) can't be judged online. Collect **swatches** labeled with type and price for designer approval, and photograph large patterns to show their scale and repeat.
- **Fabric math**:
  - Fabric is sold by the yard in widths of 40/54/60/72", up to 110". Recalculate if the store's width differs from what you planned.
  - Add seam allowances and hems, and respect grain and direction. You can't nest pieces like plywood.
  - **Add the pattern repeat** to each piece that must match.
  - **Railroaded** fabric runs the pattern along the length (selvages at top and bottom), useful for long sofa backs.
- **Sources**: fabric and craft stores, upholstery suppliers, theatrical suppliers (wide seamless muslin, canvas, scrim), and custom digital fabric printing.

## Cutting and sewing (H 201–212; M 160–161)

- **Cutting table**: at least 45" wide and 6–8' long, with a pinnable surface (cork, or Homosote wrapped in muslin or paper).
- Mark on the **wrong side** with tailor's chalk or pencil; some pens bleed through.
- **Fabric scissors are for fabric only.** Never borrow them for paper. Cut flat on the table. **Rotary cutters** need a self-healing mat and attention to your fingers.
- **Seam allowance** is typically ¼–⅝". Sew **right sides together**, so 3D objects are built inside-out. Pin perpendicular to the stitch line.
- **Press seams**: both sides, then open. Use a **tailor's ham** for curves.

### Seams (H 202–205)
- **Plain seam**.
- **Flat-felled**: strong, finished on both sides.
- **French**: encloses raw edges; good for sheers. **Mock French** for curves.
- **Clip and notch** allowances: trim outside corners diagonally, slit inside corners, V-notch concave curves, slit convex ones. Never cut the stitch.

### Machines (H 206–207)
- **Domestic** machines are versatile, for lighter fabrics.
- **Industrial** machines do one task fast and handle heavy layers.
- **Walking-foot** industrial machines feed top and bottom layers evenly and suit upholstery.
- Learn the parts: spool and bobbin, tension, stitch length and width, presser foot (always sew with it down), feed dogs, throat plate guides.
- **Tension**: too tight puckers or breaks the thread; too loose leaves a bird's nest underneath. **Test on a scrap of the same fabric.**

### Hand stitches (H 206–210; M 160–161)
You'll always need hand stitches: the last seam of a stuffed object, fabric attached to rigid parts, repairs onstage.
- **Running/basting**: quick joins, gathering, temporary seams.
- **Back stitch**: the strongest hand stitch, like a machine stitch.
- **Whip/overcast**: hems and raw edges.
- **Catch/cross stitch**: invisible, flexible hems.
- **Slip/ladder stitch**: invisible closure from the outside; easier with a curved needle.
- Lock off the thread with tiny perpendicular stitches.

### Edges (H 206, 210–212)
- **Hems**: rolled (napkins, delicate fabric), turned (most common), blind (nearly invisible).
- **Serging** (overlock) trims and binds an edge in one pass and can be decorative.
- Keep a **seam ripper** for mistakes.

## Upholstery: the props approach (H 212–217; W 142–155)

Hart covers building new upholstered pieces rather than full traditional reupholstery (H 212–217). A job may be as simple as re-covering a drop-in dining-chair seat or as complex as a full elaborate sofa (W 142).

**How stage upholstery differs** (W 142): physical comfort matters less, and can even be unwanted: it's common to put **plywood under sofa and armchair cushions** so actors can get up easily. It also needn't last as long. So the cover fabric may be the same as "real" upholstery, but what's underneath is much simpler: **plywood instead of webbing or springs, and foam as the main filling**, unless more elaborate techniques would really improve the look.

**The three layers** (W 143):
- **Support**, the load-bearing base: traditionally jute webbing stretched both ways under hessian; in modern work rubber webbing or steel tension springs; onstage usually **thin plywood**, for speed, firmness and a surface to glue foam to. Where the underside shows or is seen close up, dress fine jute scrim or black casement over the ply.
- **Filling**, which gives shape and depth: traditionally two layers, coarse dense fibre tied to the hessian, covered in scrim and stitched to shape, then softer cotton felt to fair the shape (sprung work adds hourglass springs under the first stuffing). Modern work, and nearly all stage upholstery, uses **polyurethane foam**, often wrapped in polyester wadding to soften the shape and stop the foam catching the cover.
- **Cover**, traditionally hard-wearing textile or real or imitation leather; onstage, where looks matter more than durability, lighter and cheaper fabrics, ideally over a **calico lining**. Buttoning and trims (gimp, piping, braid) decorate edges and hide the staples.

**Fire retardancy** (W 143): in the UK, upholstery foams and nearly all fillings must be fire-retardant to BS 5852, which most fire officers accept as equivalent to the stage standard; but untreated foam is still sold for other uses, so **specify fire-retardant foam**. Upholstery cover fabrics must comply too, but onstage you may use dress fabric or a market-stall bargain: treat anything untreated or doubtful. Trims such as braid aren't covered, so dip them in a retardant suited to their fibre. Fire-retardant calico is available. **[Check current]** See [ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md#flame-retardancy-h-320-w-1718).

- **Build removable upholstered parts** (drop-in or slip seats, panels). The wood shop and soft-goods shop can then work in parallel, and either part can be redone later without damaging the other.
- **The sandwich**:
  1. **Plywood base**, thick enough that screws from below won't poke through.
  2. **Polyurethane foam**, traced from the base. Cut with a snap knife, an **electric knife** or a foam saw. Butt pieces tight. Glue foam with contact or barge cement, with ventilation, respirator and gloves (H 212–217). Foam grades, cutters and glues are in [ch. 32](32-foams-and-rubbers.md#working-with-foam). Reinforce glue joints under tension with a strip of calico or other fabric, and staple where foam meets wood (W 146).
  3. **Batting**: polyester ("Dacron") or cotton. Cut it, don't tear it. Wrap it to the back for a rounded edge, or stop at the edge for a crisp one (H 212–217). Polyester wadding comes from about **2 oz/yd² (70 g/m²)**, a light cover for a foam seat or cushion, to **14 oz (475 g/m²)**, which takes deep buttoning or can fill a light cushion without foam. Surface-, centre- and through-bonded waddings are progressively firmer. Over foam, a very light spray of glue holds it, often not even that; glue or stitch the edges when wrapping cushions (W 147).
  4. **Optional muslin layer**: holds the padding, sets the shape, and makes future reupholstery easier (H 212–217). Wilson's equivalent is a **calico lining** used to pull the foam into shape, so the tension is off the cover and you can concentrate on neatness and pattern symmetry when covering. Use a whole piece over a seat or back, or strips glued round the foam's edges; strips suit deep-buttoned work, where an all-over lining isn't practical unless lining and cover go on together (W 147).
  5. **Upholstery fabric**: center the pattern; florals "grow" upward.
- **Shaping the foam** (W 146–147): upholstery profiles are as important to period accuracy as the woodwork, and a flat slab of new foam isn't a shape traditional upholstery has; it can look clumsy. Even without a specific reference, give surfaces at risk of looking flat a slight **crown**, or make them slightly concave to suggest upholstery collapsed with age.
  - **Build up in layers** rather than sculpting one block: a small piece of foam under the middle of a seat gives fullness; a strip of firmer foam around or under it makes a **feather edge**; a chaise scroll end is easiest in thin layers. **The top layer should always be one continuous sheet**, with bulk blocks underneath.
  - **Rolled edges:** cut the foam oversize, chamfer the edge, and glue it chamfer-side down; the chamfer's angle sets the roll's profile. Steep chamfers and hard foams create a lot of tension, so reinforce the join with a fabric strip or pull the whole piece down in calico.
  - **Round nosings:** fold foam of half the finished thickness double.
  - Measure, mark and cut accurately for consistent results.
- **Stapling order**:
  1. Mark the centers of each side on base and fabric.
  2. Staple center front, then center back, center right, center left.
  3. Work outward toward the corners, keeping tension even.
  4. **Fold, trim and miter the corners**, keeping pleats close together, with any others underneath.
  5. Use **sub-staples** (partly driven) or pins while adjusting (H 212–217).
  - Wilson's sequence for a drop-in seat is the same: two or three staples in the middle of one edge, then the opposite edge with enough tension to pull the foam into shape, then the middles of the other two with moderate tension. That leaves a cross of stretched fabric and four loose corners, worked out alternately side to side. Staple about every 2 cm or closer if the cover is pulling the foam into shape. Corners take a single pleat on one side, or **box pleats** meeting at the corner; slipstitch obtrusive pleats down. Another way to get even tension on straight edges: push the whole edge down with a batten while a second person staples a close row (W 148). Allow 5–6 cm of fabric to grip under the base, check the pile or pattern alignment, and keep foam and wadding from wrapping onto the underside of the base (W 148). Steps: [project 3](34-fabric-and-upholstery-projects.md#project-3-a-recovered-drop-seat-h-9-212217-w-148).
  - **The golden rule** (W 148): neatness matters, if only so the work doesn't draw attention to itself, so make **temporary, reversible fixings** until you know something works: skewers or temporary tacks for fabric and pleats before the staple gun; slip knots on buttons until they're even, then half hitches. Upholsterer's skewers are 100 mm wire pins with a sharp end and an eye (W 146).
- **Staple guns**: pneumatic (strongest; fine staples for many layers), electric, hand; **long-nose** for recesses. A tack puller removes staples.
- **Cambric** dust cover underneath.
- **Stuffed-over work** (W 148–151): seats built on the chair frame itself, covering all or part of the rails (loosely, any piece where nearly all the frame is covered, like a modern sofa). The cover is usually fixed on a visible surface, and must be cut round the back uprights (and arms) with a Y-shaped cut. Place fixings carefully along a chosen line, trim the surplus just short of them, and hide them with trim.
- **Visible edges** (H 215–217):
  - **gimp or trim** over the staples (glued or tacked). Wide flat braids and gimps glue on most easily (hot glue gun, latex adhesive or a general-purpose clear glue); **gimp pins** are small tacks with coloured heads that blend into the trim; thin trims such as chair cord are better stitched than glued (W 149–151).
  - **upholstery tacks / nailhead trim**. Over staples, **nail-head trim strip** (conjoined false heads, every fourth holed for a real nail) is quicker than individual nails (W 151).
  - fabric-wrapped thin panels to cover clusters of staples (H 215–217), for example over the outsides of chair arms (W 154).
  - **piping/welt** (cord in a continuous bias strip, sewn with a piping foot) (H 215–217). Wilson's method: bias strips wide enough to go round the cord plus 25 mm, joined end to end and pressed, wrapped round the cord and sewn close with a zipper foot (W 153).
- **Covering curved edges** (W 151–152): unless the fabric is very stretchy, a scroll or round stool covered in one piece needs its fullness gathered into pleats or tucks. Place fixings at wide spacings first, always pulling the fabric at right angles to the edge (judging tension by eye and feel if you're also pulling foam down). Then pull down and fix in the middle of each gap, and keep halving the gaps until a row of close fixings separates equal gathers; fold each into a pleat and fix it. On a steady curve, space the first fixings equally; on a tightening curve, the pleats must get closer, so plan the first fixings for that. To pull foam round a scroll, glue a calico strip to its last few centimetres; make a paper template for an arm cover.
- **Bordered seats** (W 152–153): a separate border sewn to a seat piece handles curved edges and is essential for a **feather-edged** seat of any shape. For simple seats, sew the cover first: template the seat's plan view, add a small seam allowance, cut the border strip (joined if needed) with extra to grip, pin and sew (inserting piping if wanted), turn it right side out, and pull it over. Sized to the foam and wrapped in thin wadding, it fits snugly. For complex shapes, join in place: glue a 4–5 cm calico strip round the top edge of the border area, lay wadding and the seat fabric, pin it to the calico all round, hand-stitch, then pin the border (top edge turned and pressed) and slipstitch it to the seat cover before pulling it down; add piping as you sew or a cord afterwards. Steps: [project 5](34-fabric-and-upholstery-projects.md#project-5-a-bordered-feather-edged-seat-w-152153).
- **Tufting** (button-tufted diamond, square and other patterns), **pleats** (pinch, French) and **gathering** (H 215–217).
- **Buttoning** (W 153–155), derived from old ways of holding covers and fillings in place:
  - **Covered buttons:** usually two-part "cover buttons" in the upholstery fabric. For a few, cut a fabric disc about three times the button's diameter, run a gathering stitch round the edge, pull it tight round the button, tie off and push on the back. For many, use a haberdasher's jig or a commercial button coverer (W 153).
  - **Surface buttoning** makes a fairly flat pattern and can be added after covering if the cover isn't too tight: chalk the positions, pierce the cover with an awl, drill 4–5 mm holes in a ply support (push the bit right down through the filling to the ply **before** starting the drill, or it tears out foam), thread each button on twine through a long needle and out through the hole, pull each to depth and staple the twine or knot it round a scrap. On a soft support, go through and back up beside the first hole, tying round a rolled fabric **tuft** with slip knots until even, then half hitches. It's easier if the stuffing was prepared as for deep buttoning (W 153).
  - **Deep buttoning** is three-dimensional and must be planned from the start: holes in the filling, buttons in before the cover is pulled down, and the button spacing on the **cover** about **40% bigger** than on the filling, to give the fullness for the buttons to sink and the pleats to form. Foam about half the height of the diamonds; holes about twice the button size cut with a sharpened thin-walled steel tube pushed in and twisted; shallow knife cuts where the pleats go; wadding worked into the holes. Work from the centre out, pull the buttons halfway, arrange the pleats to face **down on backs and outward on seats**, form the pleats radiating from the outer buttons and fix them temporarily, then staple, pull the buttons to final depth and tie off (W 155). Steps: [project 4](34-fabric-and-upholstery-projects.md#project-4-a-deep-buttoned-panel-h-215217-w-155).
- **Faux hide** (W 147–148): stretch calico tightly over a firm foam base and prime it with flexible PVA or acrylic medium mixed with a dark base colour; work lighter colours, also rich in PVA or acrylic, in locally to mimic wear, fading and grain; finish with a clear top coat. See also [ch. 35](35-leather.md).

## For the classroom

- **Pillowcase to cushion.** Start with Hart's pillowcase (H 9), then a boxed cushion with piping, building skills progressively.
- **Drop-in seat.** Each student upholsters a drop-in seat using the full sandwich, with mitered corners and a cambric underside.
- **Comfort is optional.** Discuss why a stage sofa might get plywood under its cushions, and what else changes when upholstery is built for actors rather than sitters (W 142).
- **Deep-button maths.** Given a 120 mm diamond on the foam, students calculate the cover spacing with Wilson's 40% allowance, mark out a cover, and button a sample panel (W 155).
- **Fabric math problems.** Given a sofa back, a 27" repeat and a choice of 54" or railroaded 110" goods, students calculate yardage.
- **Leather sampler.** Students saddle-stitch a small leather pouch or knife sheath.
