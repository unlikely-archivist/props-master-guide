# 29. Wood and Carpentry

> **Sources:** H ch.7 (pp. 100–128); M ch.5 (pp. 55–66), ch.11 (pp. 152–156)
> **[Check current]** Lumber prices, species availability and grading terms vary by region and change often. Table-saw safety technology has evolved since 2013.

Wood is cheap, strong, easy to learn, and able to imitate many other materials, so it's a staple in every prop shop (H 101).

## Wood basics

- **Grain** runs along the board. Wood is much **stronger along the grain** than across it (think of splitting firewood). Stroke the board: smooth is *with* the grain, rough is *against* it (H 101).
- **Wood dust is a known human carcinogen** (IARC Group 1) and an asthma trigger. Use dust collection, ventilation and respirators (H 101).
- **Hardwood vs. softwood** describes tree type, not hardness: balsa is technically a hardwood. Props usually use softwood plus one or two inexpensive local hardwoods, then stain or paint them to look like any species. Reserve specialty woods for close-up hero props, and expect them to behave unpredictably the first time (H 122).

## Cutting

| Cut | Meaning | Typical machine |
|---|---|---|
| **Rip** | Along the grain, to width | Table saw (H 101) |
| **Crosscut** | Across the grain, to length | Miter/chop saw, radial-arm saw (H 101) |
| **Miter** | Angle across the end | Miter box or miter saw (H 106) |
| **Bevel** | Edge angled to the face | Tilted blade, table or shoe (H 106) |
| **Taper** | Angled long edge | Taper jig, guided circular saw (H 106) |
| **Curves** | Irregular shapes | Band saw (minimum radius set by blade width; make relief cuts), scroll saw, jigsaw (interior cuts start from a drilled hole), coping saw, fretsaw (H 107–108) |
| **Resaw** | Splitting a board's thickness | Band saw with a wide blade. Far better than planing a thick board down to dust (H 106) |

A **stop block** gives repeatable lengths without measuring each piece (H 103). A circular saw with a guide rail handles sheets too big for stationary machines (H 101).

### The table saw
The table saw is the most useful machine in most shops and statistically the most dangerous (H 103–106). Hart's rules:
- **Never use the rip fence and miter gauge together** (the trapped offcut kicks back).
- Lock the fence **parallel** to the blade. Hold stock against the fence **before** the blade, never past it. Run only straight edges against the fence. Hold the work down, because the blade can lift it.
- **Kickback** happens when wood pinches the blade (the blade slows and whines, the kerf closes). Stop pushing, hold the work, switch off, and wait for the blade to stop. **Leave the riving knife on.**
- **Stand to the side** of the work's path.
- **Blade height: no more than about ¼" above the stock.** Keep fingers **out of the throat-plate zone**; use **push sticks** and **featherboards**.
- **Never reach across the spinning blade.** Never push with your hand directly behind the blade. Never pull from the outfeed side; a helper only supports the work lightly.
- **Keep guards on.** Hart notes table saws cause about as many accidental amputations in the US as doors do.
- **Flesh-sensing saws** stop the blade on skin contact. The brake cartridge must be replaced after it fires, and wet wood can trigger it. Hart thinks training makes them unnecessary in a professional shop, but valuable in **educational shops** (H 105–106).

## Thickness and size

- **Planer**: takes about 1/16–3/32" per pass. Hand planing isn't realistic on prop deadlines (H 106).
- **Face-gluing for thickness**: spread glue evenly, clamp or weight with even pressure, leave the pieces oversize, and **cut to final size after the glue dries**, because pieces slide while clamping (H 106–107).
- **Edge-gluing panels**: long-grain edge joints need no reinforcement, but biscuits, dowels or pocket screws help alignment. **Alternate clamps top and bottom** to prevent bowing (H 105).

## Bending wood (H 107–110)

- **Thin sheets** take gentle curves.
- **Steam bending** for tighter bends.
- **Bending plywood** ("wiggle wood", bending lauan, Flexply):
  - **"Column"** (4'×8') bends around an 8-foot column.
  - **"Barrel"** (8'×4') bends around a 4-foot barrel.
- **Kerfing**: parallel cuts almost through the board.
- **Hold curves** with **formers** (profile-cut ribs; remove the centers to save weight) plus **stringers**, or **laminate** several thin bent layers (more layers means more rigidity).
- For very tight curves, laminate soaked cardboard (H 110).

## Shaping (H 110–111)

- Belt sanders, rasps and files; an angle grinder with flap discs; rotary tools.
- **Router** edge profiles: roundover, bullnose, chamfer, moldings.
- **Lathe** for anything with **axial symmetry** (legs, spindles, candlesticks, bowls, barrels). Some lathes also turn dense foam.

## Gluing wood (H 111–112)

- **PVA wood glue** is the standard. Hide glue is for repairing hide-glued joints.
- **Joint surfaces should be smooth** (sanded to about 200 grit) **but not burnished or sealed**. Test with a drop of water: if it doesn't soak in, sand.
- The ideal: glue both faces, spread thin, clamp until a small bead just squeezes out. **Excess squeeze-out blocks stain.**
- Keep clamped **at least 20 minutes**, firmly but without crushing. Allow **24 hours before stressing** the joint.
- On deadline, glue on one side only is often strong enough for the run.
- PVA survives rain. For immersion, use epoxy or UF glue.

### Grain orientation and joint strength (H 112–113)
- **End grain to end grain**: weakest. Needs reinforcement.
- **End grain to long grain**: moderate. Usually needs more than glue.
- **Long grain to long grain**: strongest. Glue alone is enough.

## Joinery (H 112–118)

Props need only a few joints. Good joints add alignment shoulders and make the wood, rather than the glue, the point of failure.

| Joint | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Mortise and tenon** | The strongest classic furniture joint. Use it for high-stress joints when time allows. Mussman shows how to **fake** it with butt joints, corner irons and bolted corner blocks on paint-grade tables (M 155) |
| **Half lap / halved cross joint** | Strong; simple on a band saw, table saw or router |
| **Bridle / T-bridle** | Lots of glue surface; self-locating |
| **Groove / dado / rabbet** | Groove runs with the grain, dado across it, rabbet is an L on an edge or end. **Through** or **stopped** (stopped ends need squaring with a chisel). Cut on a table saw with a sled or miter gauge, **never against the fence**, or on a router (H 113–115) |
| **Dowels** | Fast with a jig; **dowel centers** transfer hole locations. **Through-dowels** are visible but simple (H 117–118) |
| **Biscuits / splines** | Alignment and strength along edges (H 116–117) |
| **Pocket screws** | Hidden screws driven at a steep angle with a jig (H 116) |
| **Cleats / glue blocks** | Reinforce thin stock; shelf supports that locate parts automatically (H 116–117, 121) |
| **Screws / bolts** | Pilot holes and countersinks prevent splitting; putty hides countersunk heads. Nuts and bolts make **knock-down** joints and removable casters (H 115–116) |

**Dry-fit** the whole assembly before gluing (H 118).

## Faking it (H 117–121)

- **False drawers**: in a dresser, maybe only one drawer needs to open. Hart's compromise is to build one working drawer that can move to whichever opening the director chooses, and fix the false fronts as late as possible (H 117–119).
- **Thick-looking tops** (reveals): fill and paint plywood edges, iron on **edge veneer**, glue on strips, add a solid **nose**, or apply a reveal strip underneath (paint-grade only, because it exposes end grain) (H 119–120).
- **Applied joinery**: surface-applied wedges and tenons that read as a through mortise and tusk tenon (H 120).
- **Carcass work** (dressers, wardrobes, clocks): build a plain box from sheet goods, then **layer moldings, panels and spacers** to fake complexity (H 120–121). **Watch the cumulative size**: layers add width fast. Don't build a solid cube of wood.

## Finishing the carpentry (H 121)

**Break the edges.** Round them slightly, chamfer or radius. **Run your hands over the whole piece** before handoff, so you find the splinter instead of the actor.

## Buying wood (H 122–126)

- **Softwood grades**: construction/common grade has knots and warp. **Clear/select** is nearly knot-free, for visible work, at about twice the price. **Furring strips** are rough, which is useful for rustic props.
- **Dimensional lumber** is surfaced on all four sides (S4S). **Nominal vs. actual sizes:**

| Nominal | Actual | Nominal | Actual |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1×2 | ¾ × 1½ | 2×2 | 1½ × 1½ |
| 1×3 | ¾ × 2½ | 2×4 | 1½ × 3½ |
| 1×4 | ¾ × 3½ | 2×6 | 1½ × 5½ |
| 1×6 | ¾ × 5½ | 4×4 | 3½ × 3½ |
| 1×8 | ¾ × 7¼ | 6×6 | 5½ × 5½ |
| 1×12 | ¾ × 11¼ | 8×8 | 7¼ × 7¼ |

Longer boards are often cheaper per foot but more likely to be warped.

- **Rough lumber** is cheaper and comes in more species, but you mill it yourself or pay for surfacing (S2S, S4S). Thickness is in **quarters**: 4/4 is about 1" rough and about 13/16" once surfaced on two sides. It's priced by the **board foot** (L × W × T in inches ÷ 144). Order with a waste allowance. Milling order: **jointer** one face → **planer** the other → jointer one edge → crosscut one end → rip to width → cut to length. Keep a stock of finished lumber for rush jobs (H 123–125).
- **Sheet goods** (H 125–126):
  - **Plywood**: cross-grain veneers; strong over spans.
  - **MDF**: smoothest paint surface; no grain; weaker.
  - **MDO**: plywood core with MDF faces.
  - **Hardboard/"Masonite"**: dense; slick tempered face.
  - **Particleboard**: cheap hidden substrate.
  - **Lauan**: thin, light and strong. **Buy FSC-certified**, because lauan has a history of illegal logging.
  - **Homosote**: carvable faux brick; sound-deadening under casters.
  - Easycurve/Upson board; cardboard tubes (Sonotube).
- **Moldings** (H 127–128): learn the profiles (ovolo, cove, cyma recta, ogee, bead, astragal, reeding, egg-and-dart, fluting, rope). Buy them, take them from picture frames, or **router them on the edge of a wide board and then rip them off**, which is safer than routing thin strips. **Stack simple profiles** to build complex ones. Fake carved moldings with castings, fringe, Ethafoam or stiffened textured materials.

## Quick builds (M 152–156)

Mussman gives simple templates for:
- **soft flats** (1×4 frame with muslin; prime with starch if it must be translucent) and **hard ("Hollywood") flats** (frame on edge with a lauan skin; dressing can be screwed on)
- **platforms** (2×4 frame, legs, diagonal braces, plywood top)
- **rehearsal tables** (plywood top, 4×4 or laminated 2×4 legs, 1×4 apron)
- **benches** (½" ply with 1×3 legs; a rustic plank bench)

## For the classroom

- **Birdhouse (or box) project.** Hart's project-based recommendation: a small build that teaches cut lists, joinery, gluing and finishing in one afternoon (H 9).
- **Joint sampler.** Students cut and glue a half lap, a bridle, a dado, a rabbet and a dowel joint, then break-test them and relate the results to grain orientation.
- **Faux-furniture challenge.** Students take a plain plywood box and, using applied moldings, panels and a reveal top, make it read as a period cabinet. They must keep it within ±1" of the original footprint.
- **Table-saw certification.** A supervised checkout using Hart's rules as the rubric.
