# 29. Wood and Carpentry

> **Sources:** H ch.7 (pp. 100–128); M ch.5 (pp. 55–66), ch.11 (pp. 152–156); W ch.2 (pp. 20–49)
> **Companion:** [Wood Projects](29-wood-projects.md): a birdhouse or box, a rehearsal table, faked period joinery, a one-drawer dresser, curved forms, a band-sawn cabriole leg, anti-raking a table, a turned leg.
> **[Check current]** Lumber prices, species availability and grading terms vary by region and change often. Table-saw safety technology has evolved since 2013. W's sheet sizes, species names and fire classes are British (2003).

Wood is cheap, strong, light, easy to learn, and able to imitate many other materials or hold them up, so it's a staple in every prop shop (H 101; W 20). Timber-based sheets such as plywood and MDF add cheap, strong sheet stock (W 20).

## Wood basics

- **Grain** runs along the board. Wood is much **stronger along the grain** than across it (think of splitting firewood). Stroke the board: smooth is *with* the grain, rough is *against* it (H 101).
- **Wood dust is a known human carcinogen** (IARC Group 1) and an asthma trigger. Use dust collection, ventilation and respirators (H 101). Wilson specifies that softwood dust is a respiratory sensitizer that can cause occupational asthma, while some hardwood dusts are carcinogens (W 22, 28). See [ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md).
- **Hardwood vs. softwood** describes tree type, not hardness: balsa is technically a hardwood. Props usually use softwood plus one or two inexpensive local hardwoods, then stain or paint them to look like any species. Reserve specialty woods for close-up hero props, and expect them to behave unpredictably the first time (H 122). Nearly all solid timber on stage is softwood (W 21).
- **Species notes** (W 21, 41): in Europe the staple softwoods are Scots pine (*Pinus sylvestris*, "red") and a cheaper, knottier spruce/fir mix ("white"). Hemlock turns easily, Douglas fir is very strong, and parana pine comes in very wide boards. High-budget furniture uses oak, beech, mahogany or meranti. **Lime or jelutong** suit carved or turned work, or anywhere you want no obvious grain. Check that hardwood is fully dried (it's often sold part-seasoned) and comes from a sustainable source such as FSC-certified stock (W 21).
- **Knots** mean unreliable strength. Judge each piece against the design: how slender are the parts, and will a singer stand on it or just set down a hat? Knots also bleed through finishes, so they need an opaque primer. Clear softwood in large sections can cost as much as hardwood, which is then usually the better buy (W 31).

## Cutting

| Cut | Meaning | Typical machine |
|---|---|---|
| **Rip** | Along the grain, to width | Table saw (H 101) |
| **Crosscut** | Across the grain, to length | Miter/chop saw, radial-arm saw (H 101) |
| **Miter** | Angle across the end | Miter box or miter saw (H 106) |
| **Bevel** | Edge angled to the face | Tilted blade, table or shoe (H 106) |
| **Taper** | Angled long edge | Taper jig, guided circular saw (H 106) |
| **Curves** | Irregular shapes | Band saw (minimum radius set by blade width; make relief cuts), scroll saw, jigsaw (interior cuts start from a drilled hole), coping saw, fretsaw (H 107–108) |
| **Resaw** | Splitting a board's thickness | Band saw with a wide blade. Far better than planing a thick board down to dust (H 106) |

A **stop block** gives repeatable lengths without measuring each piece: mark only the first piece, clamp a stop to the fence, and slide the rest up to it (H 103; W 24). For a rip on a bench or band saw, set the fence and skip marking the stock (W 24). A circular saw with a guide rail handles sheets too big for stationary machines (H 101). The **jigsaw** is the most versatile portable tool and can do intricate work with practice; the **hand-held circular saw** cuts straight lines faster and straighter. A **wall saw** cuts sheets accurately in little floor space (W 26–27).

### The band saw
For Wilson, the **band saw** is by far the most versatile machine in a prop shop. Different blades cover straight and curved cuts, the table tilts for bevels, and simple jigs handle disc cutting and pattern following. It also cuts plastics, expanded polystyrene and upholstery foam (W 27). It produces three-dimensional furniture parts quickly: a **cabriole leg** is cut by drawing the profile on two adjacent faces, cutting one, taping the offcuts back on, and cutting the second ([project 6](29-wood-projects.md#project-6-a-band-sawn-cabriole-leg-w-33)) (W 33). Tilting the table to 45° makes square stock octagonal before you round it with a plane (W 26).

### The table saw
The table saw is the most useful machine in most shops and statistically the most dangerous (H 103–106). Hart's rules:
- **Never use the rip fence and miter gauge together** (the trapped offcut kicks back).
- Lock the fence **parallel** to the blade. Hold stock against the fence **before** the blade, never past it. Run only straight edges against the fence. Hold the work down, because the blade can lift it.
- **Kickback** happens when wood pinches the blade (the blade slows and whines, the kerf closes). Stop pushing, hold the work, switch off, and wait for the blade to stop. **Leave the riving knife on.**
- **Stand to the side** of the work's path.
- **Blade height: no more than about ¼" above the stock.** Keep fingers **out of the throat-plate zone**; use **push sticks** and **featherboards**.
- **Never reach across the spinning blade.** Never push with your hand directly behind the blade. Never pull from the outfeed side; a helper only supports the work lightly.
- **Keep guards on.** Hart notes table saws cause about as many accidental amputations in the US as doors do.
- **Flesh-sensing saws** stop the blade on skin contact. The brake cartridge must be replaced after it fires, and wet wood can trigger it. Hart thinks training makes them unnecessary in a professional shop, but valuable in **educational shops** (H 105–106).

## Thickness and size

- **Planer**: takes about 1/16–3/32" per pass. Hand planing isn't realistic on prop deadlines (H 106).
- **Face-gluing for thickness**: spread glue evenly, clamp or weight with even pressure, leave the pieces oversize, and **cut to final size after the glue dries**, because pieces slide while clamping (H 106–107).
- **Edge-gluing panels**: long-grain edge joints need no reinforcement, but biscuits, dowels or pocket screws help alignment. **Alternate clamps top and bottom** to prevent bowing (H 105).

## Curved surfaces and bending wood (H 107–110; W 39–40)

- **Cut from solid.** Chair crest rails and similar parts are traditionally sawn from a piece big enough to hold the curve, often a **blank** glued up from two or more boards, using a band saw, jigsaw or router (W 39).
- **Thin sheets** take gentle curves (H 107–110).
- **Steam bending** for tighter bends (H 107–110).
- **Bending plywood** ("wiggle wood", bending lauan, Flexply) (H 107–110):
  - **"Column"** (4'×8') bends around an 8-foot column.
  - **"Barrel"** (8'×4') bends around a 4-foot barrel.
  - British **skin ply** is 1.5 mm birch (aircraft ply), sold in 1,525 mm squares; gaboon ply is also used (W 22, 39).
- **Stressed skin** (formers and a skin). **Hold curves** with **formers** (profile-cut ribs; remove the centers to save weight) plus **stringers** (H 107–110). Wilson's version: a thin skin of 1.5 mm birch or gaboon ply (4 mm birch for strength), hardboard or thin MDF over formers of 18–25 mm ply or solid timber. Fix the skin with **glue plus grippy fasteners** (cement-coated or ring-shank nails, or air staples), because some stress stays in it. The formers are so much stronger that the stress shouldn't distort the shape (W 39).
- **Laminating.** Glue several thin bent layers over a form; more layers mean more rigidity (H 107–110). Clamp tightly, and avoid soft, creeping glues such as cheap PVA, so the lamination is about as strong as solid wood of the same thickness. Expect some **spring-back**; thinner and more numerous layers reduce it. Two layers of 4 mm ply glued in a slight curve make a quick chair back; anything more complex costs time in strip prep and jig building (W 39). Wedges between the work and an irregular former help fair the curve (W 40).
- **Staving.** A core of wooden battens sandwiched between two thin skins. Much faster than true laminating, very strong, with little spring-back (W 39).
- **Kerfing**: parallel cuts almost through the board (H 107–110). Fill the cuts with glue, bend until they close, and add a thin skin on the concave side for strength. Equal depth and spacing are crucial, which is hard without a radial-arm saw; pre-kerfed MDF can be bought (W 39–40).
- For very tight curves, laminate soaked cardboard (H 110).
- **Which to use:** cutting from solid and stressed skin are the fastest and most common. Save laminating and kerfing for when you need more strength or there's no room for formers (W 40).

## Shaping (H 110–111; W 27)

- Belt sanders, rasps and files; an angle grinder with flap discs; rotary tools (H 110–111). Rasps refine furniture parts cut on the band saw. Hand-held belt sanders are invaluable for fast smoothing, rounding and tidying (W 27).
- **Router** edge profiles: roundover, bullnose, chamfer, moldings (H 110–111). Routers and belt sanders remove material fast and make a lot of dust: wear goggles and a dust mask, and extract at source if you can (W 27).
- **Carving** with chisels and gouges stays slow even with practice and doesn't suit modern schedules; it's usually replaced by modelling and casting ([ch. 37](37-sculpting.md), [ch. 38](38-molding-and-casting.md)) (W 27). MDF carves easily and crisply with hand tools (W 35).
- **Lathe** for anything with **axial symmetry** (legs, spindles, candlesticks, bowls, barrels). Some lathes also turn dense foam (H 110–111). See [Woodturning](#woodturning-w-4049) below.

## Gluing wood (H 111–112)

- **PVA wood glue** is the standard. Hide glue is for repairing hide-glued joints.
- **Joint surfaces should be smooth** (sanded to about 200 grit) **but not burnished or sealed**. Test with a drop of water: if it doesn't soak in, sand.
- The ideal: glue both faces, spread thin, clamp until a small bead just squeezes out. **Excess squeeze-out blocks stain.**
- Keep clamped **at least 20 minutes**, firmly but without crushing. Allow **24 hours before stressing** the joint.
- On deadline, glue on one side only is often strong enough for the run.
- PVA survives rain. For immersion, use epoxy or UF glue (H 111–112). Pricier PVAs dry harder and more waterproof; that matters mainly for furniture and laminated curves (W 30).
- **Aliphatic resin ("yellow glue")** is stronger and faster than PVA and sands away, so glue lines show less (W 30).
- **Epoxy** fills gaps structurally, so a butt joint can hold without screws or pins, and a fillet of thickened epoxy can replace a wooden batten in lightweight work. It's expensive and needs strict skin protection (W 30). See [ch. 25](../4-fabrication-core/25-adhesives-and-fasteners.md) for all wood glues, including pearl (hide) glue.

### Grain orientation and joint strength (H 112–113)
- **End grain to end grain**: weakest. Needs reinforcement.
- **End grain to long grain**: moderate. Usually needs more than glue.
- **Long grain to long grain**: strongest. Glue alone is enough.

## Joinery (H 112–118)

Props need only a few joints. Good joints add alignment shoulders and make the wood, rather than the glue, the point of failure (H 112–118). Joints fall into two families: **traditional joints**, where the parts are shaped to lock mechanically or give a large glue area (strong but slow), and **lap and butt joints** held with nails, screws and glue (quicker, rarely as strong, usually good enough). The more strength you need, the more time, skill or equipment it takes (W 28).

| Joint | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Mortise and tenon** | The strongest classic furniture joint. Use it for high-stress joints when time allows (H 112–118). All but indispensable for leg-to-rail joints in chairs and tables (W 31). Mussman shows how to **fake** it with butt joints, corner irons and bolted corner blocks on paint-grade tables (M 155). **Cutting it** without a mortising/tenoning machine: by hand (few tools, but slow); tenons on a band saw, cross-cut saw with a raised blade or dado cutter, or bench saw (thickness then varies with the stock); mortises with a hollow-chisel kit on a pillar drill, a router jig, or a row of drilled holes cleaned out with a mortise chisel for a handful of joints (W 31–33) |
| **Half lap / halved cross joint** | Strong; simple on a band saw, table saw or router |
| **Bridle / T-bridle** | Lots of glue surface; self-locating |
| **Groove / dado / rabbet** | Groove runs with the grain, dado across it, rabbet is an L on an edge or end. **Through** or **stopped** (stopped ends need squaring with a chisel). Cut on a table saw with a sled or miter gauge, **never against the fence**, or on a router (H 113–115) |
| **Dowels** | Fast with a jig; **dowel centers** transfer hole locations. **Through-dowels** are visible but simple (H 117–118). Dowel joints can replace mortise and tenons where stress is lower, or anywhere if glued with epoxy, though stage use isn't always predictable. To mark matching hole centres, tape a small tack to one part and press the two together (W 34) |
| **Biscuits / splines** | Alignment and strength along edges (H 116–117) |
| **Pocket screws** | Hidden screws driven at a steep angle with a jig (H 116) |
| **Cleats / glue blocks** | Reinforce thin stock; shelf supports that locate parts automatically (H 116–117, 121) |
| **Screws / bolts** | Pilot holes and countersinks prevent splitting; putty hides countersunk heads. Nuts and bolts make **knock-down** joints and removable casters (H 115–116). Screws pull a joint tight, rarely work loose, and work where the piece isn't firmly supported. Modern parallel-sided screws need only a small pilot and countersink in softwood. Put at least half to two-thirds of the screw's length into the receiving piece (W 29–30) |
| **Nails / pins / staples** | Cheap and fast, but need solid backing to hammer against and can loosen under repeated stress, so use them where stress is limited or the structure is braced. **Ring-shank** or **cement-coated** nails grip better (thin ply round curves). **Panel pins** attach mouldings; nip or file the point off to stop delicate parts splitting. **Air staplers and nail guns** need no backing, so large light forms can be built "tea-chest style" from 20 × 20 mm timber skinned with 4 mm ply (W 28–30) |

**Dry-fit** the whole assembly before gluing (H 118).

## Faking it (H 117–121)

- **False drawers**: in a dresser, maybe only one drawer needs to open. Hart's compromise is to build one working drawer that can move to whichever opening the director chooses, and fix the false fronts as late as possible (H 117–119).
- **Thick-looking tops** (reveals): fill and paint plywood edges, iron on **edge veneer**, glue on strips, add a solid **nose**, or apply a reveal strip underneath (paint-grade only, because it exposes end grain) (H 119–120).
- **Applied joinery**: surface-applied wedges and tenons that read as a through mortise and tusk tenon (H 120).
- **Carcass work** (dressers, wardrobes, clocks): build a plain box from sheet goods, then **layer moldings, panels and spacers** to fake complexity (H 120–121). **Watch the cumulative size**: layers add width fast. Don't build a solid cube of wood.

## Building stage furniture (W 30–35)

Much stage furniture is hired, bought or borrowed; you build it when that's cheaper or the design demands it (W 30). How big a job it is depends on:
- **Type and style.** Chairs are harder than tables; a rustic bench is far quicker to build and finish than an elegant dining chair (W 30).
- **Finish.** High-class period furniture needn't match the real thing in finesse, but **finishing can take as long as the construction** (W 30). A group of students built a prototype chair in a few hours; the joinery is often quicker than the finishing (W 35).
- **Equipment.** A simple bench needs a few hand tools and maybe a router; elaborate pieces call for a band saw or lathe and dust extraction. If you lack the kit or time, **buy hard-to-make parts** such as turned or cabriole legs (W 30–31). A few suppliers sell ready-made furniture parts, usually in hardwood; they speed the work and can be cheaper than making the parts (W 22).
- **Sheet stock.** MDF routs and carves well and shows cleaner edges than ply or blockboard, and is flatter than ply of the same price. Blockboard is lighter and comes with various face veneers (W 31). Under upholstery, a carcass of cheap softwood is fine: only the legs and arm fronts will show (W 35).

### Mending and reinforcing furniture (W 34–36)

There are two kinds of repair, and both are easier if the furniture is yours rather than hired or borrowed (W 34):
- **Temporary structural repairs** keep a piece going for a few more shows. Splint a broken part with a piece of wood or a metal bracket; the aim is to stop the damage spreading, not to restore full strength, and the stronger the repair, the uglier it tends to be. Re-glue loose joints if you can get glue in; otherwise use a steel strap or bracket. **Don't screw through a chair leg into the end grain of a loose rail**: the screw won't stay tight and the leg is weakened (W 34, 36).
- **Restoration** restores strength and looks, and takes time even for small damage, since replacing one rail may mean taking apart several joints. Old hide glue can be softened with steam, but hidden brads often hold the joints, and pulling them causes more damage. Re-glue splits or copy the part in similar-coloured timber; the copy needn't match every detail, only avoid drawing attention. When reassembling, choose between **epoxy** (strong, convenient, but no future dismantling) and **hide glue** (reversible) (W 34–36).

**Chairs fail in chains:** when one joint goes, the stress moves to its neighbours. If the piece was already weak, it was badly chosen, and careful repair will probably do. If it was sound and the **staging broke it** (a fight, a dance), it will break again however well you mend it. That matters for performer safety, and there will be pressure to get it back into rehearsal fast. What's needed is **reinforcement** based on what the furniture will go through onstage: re-made joints in epoxy reinforced with fillets of epoxy and chopped glass, or bespoke heavy-gauge steel brackets (W 36). See [ch. 12](../2-admin/12-rehearsals-tech-and-performance.md) for repairs during the run.

## Furniture on raked stages (W 36–39)

A **rake** is a sloping floor: a whole stage (many older theatres have a slight slope) or part of a set. On a rake an ordinary table's legs are no longer vertical and its top tilts. On gentle architectural rakes this rarely matters, and levelling the top would only draw attention to the slope; on steep design rakes, adjusting furniture to sit level is common and is called **anti-raking** (also counter-raking; see [ch. 22](../4-fabrication-core/22-the-build-method.md)) (W 36).

**What you need to know first** (W 36–37):
- **The slope**, as degrees, a ratio or a percentage. 7.125°, 1 in 8 and 12.5% are the same slope. Degrees are the most accurate and lead straight into trigonometry; ratios are traditional and easy to picture; percentages are rare. **The tangent of the rake angle = 1 ÷ the second number of the ratio = the percentage ÷ 100.** (A 1 in 5 rake is 20%, or 11.3°.)
- **How the piece sits relative to the slope.** A table anti-raked to sit level with its length running up and down the rake is level **only** in that position: turn it 90° and the top tilts crazily; turn it 180° and it slopes at twice the rake. If the director may move it, anti-rake as late as possible; build a new piece with four equal legs and modify it later.
- **The direction of steepest slope** (the "way" of the rake) isn't necessarily straight up- and downstage, nor parallel to the rake's sides; rakes with a tapering front step are what people loosely call a "double rake." In plan, the direction of steepest slope is at right angles to any line joining two points of equal height. Whoever built the rake may already have the geometry.

**Anti-raking tables and chairs** (W 37–39):
- With plain legs, the easiest method is to **cut the right amount off each leg**. Adding to legs is harder, especially on chairs, which take dynamic loads. Turned or cabriole legs don't anti-rake easily and may need new legs of different lengths. You rarely can trim or extend hired or borrowed furniture.
- **Method** (on the floor or a scale drawing): draw the direction of slope and, at right angles, a **base line** joining points of equal height. Place the piece at its intended angle with its most downhill leg on the base line. Measure each leg's perpendicular distance to the base line, and **multiply by the tangent of the rake** (or divide by the ratio's second number) to get how much to cut. Only the distances between the leg bottoms matter, not the size of the top. Step-by-step in [project 7](29-wood-projects.md#project-7-anti-raking-a-table-w-3739).
- **Extending a leg:** find steel tube of similar diameter to the leg bottom; cut it to the extension plus about 30% for fixing (less a plug if needed), deburr it inside and out, carve the leg end until the tube fits tightly, tap it on with an old mallet, and fit a carved or turned wooden plug to protect the floor (W 38).

## Woodturning (W 40–49)

Turning matters for furniture and countless other props, from chandeliers to porridge bowls. The work spins on the lathe spindle, and a tool braced on the tool rest removes material (W 40). The basics come with reasonable practice. Ignore snobbery about sandpaper and scrapers not being "proper" turning: the high polish of craft turners is rarely needed on stage, and a **sanded surface takes paint better** (W 40–41).

**Timber for turning** (W 41–42):
- Carcassing and joinery softwoods are frustrating: knots, and big hardness differences between early and late wood, so a catch can do a lot of damage and fine detail is fragile. They're still fine for much work. Better pines, clear hemlock and Douglas fir come in large sections; oak, beech and mahogany turn well; jelutong is light and cuts easily where strength doesn't matter.
- **Glued-up blanks** solve the cost of big solid stock, but they must be sound: a blank breaking up at 600 rpm is dangerous. Fit faces closely (or use gap-filling epoxy), spread glue generously, clamp, and **cure 24 hours for epoxy, 48 for PVA**. Cracks can appear later as the pieces move, worsened by the moisture in PVA and by fresh timber; usually you just fill them, but if that won't do, let the wood acclimatise in the shop and glue with epoxy.
- **"Brick-laid" bowl blanks** build up small pieces on a circular base, planned on a life-size drawing; allow more tolerance the more layers there are.

**The lathe** (W 42–43): for props, a wide speed range (about **300 rpm for roughing large work up to 2,000 for slender spindles**), as heavy and rigid as possible, a centre height of about **100 mm** and about **800 mm between centres** to take a table leg. **Outboard** turning on a faceplate takes wide work such as bowls. Bolt a light bench-top lathe to your heaviest bench and don't push it past its limits. Long tool rests help with tapers. Used metal-lathe chucks can fit bigger wood lathes (three-jaw self-centring for round stock, four-jaw independent for square or off-centre work; guard projecting parts). A **tailstock drill chuck** drills down the axis.

**Tools** (W 43–44): buy a few heavy, good tools with handles up to twice the blade length, balanced where blade meets handle, not a set of cheap undersized ones.

| Tool | Action | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Roughing gouge** | Slicing | Square stock to cylinder; rough shaping |
| **Skew chisel** | Slicing | The key tool: planes cylinders, tapers, convex and shallow concave forms; cuts in and faces ends. Edge at about 60° to the tool's axis; two bevels about 20° apart, no secondary bevel; stone each whole bevel when sharpening |
| **Fingernail (spindle) gouge** | Slicing | Deep coves between centres; can do much of a skew's work |
| **Bowl gouge** | Slicing | Outsides of bowls and large-diameter faceplate work |
| **Round-nosed / square scrapers; form tools** | Scraping (edge is a burr from the grinder; used pointing down, just below centre) | Coves, levelling, facing hollows, spigots; form tools repeat a profile such as a bead |
| **Parting tool; bedan** | Both | Narrow slots to set depths; parting off; spigots and small beads |

**Mounting work** (W 44–45): long work goes **between centres**, driven by a spur at the headstock (saw shallow cuts across the end so it bites) and supported by a live or dead tailstock centre; a washer or drilled coin over the centre stops it burying itself. A narrow bore (for lamp wiring) can be drilled with a **long boring tool** through a hollow tailstock; larger bores are drilled or laminated in first and mounted on plugs or a **mandrel**. Small work such as finials goes in a chuck; wide work on a faceplate or screw chuck.

**Shaping sequence** (W 45–49): rough to a cylinder → cut reference slots to measured depths → add detail → sand → part off.
- **Roughing:** tool rest close and at centre height (spin the work by hand after every move to check clearance); lowest speed; gouge angled slightly in the direction of travel, rolled and with the handle dropped so the bevel slices. Work both directions. Feel for remaining flats with a trailing thumb.
- **Pommels** (a square section, as at the top of a table leg): make knife and relieving cuts with the skew's long point before roughing up to the line.
- **Slots:** with a parting tool or bedan used as a scraper; set calipers to the target diameter and stop when they slip over. Repeating the same slot positions and depths is the key to **matching sets** of turnings.
- **Skew planing:** about 30° to the direction of travel, so the edge sits about 45° to the axis; ride the bevel, then lift until a shaving appears. If the long point touches or the bevel lifts, it digs in. Always cut **"downhill"** from large to small diameter. For a ball, mark an equator and round each half symmetrically.
- **Coves:** scoop with a spindle gouge from each side alternately, rolling it face-up at the bottom. Bowl outsides on a faceplate are cut "uphill."
- **Thin, whippy spindles** vibrate: support them with a bought or scrap-wood **steady**, or with a gloved hand (a clean leather welding gauntlet) behind the work.
- **Finishing:** shape-correcting with 40–80 grit, otherwise start at 120–180 and go to 240–320 for finer finishes; move the tool rest away, wear goggles and a mask, and gloves against the heat. Apply brush-and-rub finishes such as **shellac** on the lathe, and rub down any filler there too.
- **Parting off** a base (a candlestick): angle the parting tool slightly so the base is concave and stands steadily; support delicate work with a gloved hand as it comes free.

## Finishing the carpentry (H 121)

**Break the edges.** Round them slightly, chamfer or radius. **Run your hands over the whole piece** before handoff, so you find the splinter instead of the actor.

## Buying wood (H 122–126)

- **Softwood grades**: construction/common grade has knots and warp. **Clear/select** is nearly knot-free, for visible work, at about twice the price. **Furring strips** are rough, which is useful for rustic props.
- **Dimensional lumber** is surfaced on all four sides (S4S). **Nominal vs. actual sizes:**

| Nominal | Actual | Nominal | Actual |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1×2 | ¾ × 1½ | 2×2 | 1½ × 1½ |
| 1×3 | ¾ × 2½ | 2×4 | 1½ × 3½ |
| 1×4 | ¾ × 3½ | 2×6 | 1½ × 5½ |
| 1×6 | ¾ × 5½ | 4×4 | 3½ × 3½ |
| 1×8 | ¾ × 7¼ | 6×6 | 5½ × 5½ |
| 1×12 | ¾ × 11¼ | 8×8 | 7¼ × 7¼ |

Longer boards are often cheaper per foot but more likely to be warped (H 122–126). British timber is quoted in millimetres, still derived from inches; as in the US, the quoted size is the **sawn** size, so planed ("PAR," planed all round) stock is about 6 mm smaller on each dimension. Sawn timber is fine for rough structure and turning. If you need particular lengths, say so when ordering (W 21–22).

- **Rough lumber** is cheaper and comes in more species, but you mill it yourself or pay for surfacing (S2S, S4S). Thickness is in **quarters**: 4/4 is about 1" rough and about 13/16" once surfaced on two sides. It's priced by the **board foot** (L × W × T in inches ÷ 144). Order with a waste allowance. Milling order: **jointer** one face → **planer** the other → jointer one edge → crosscut one end → rip to width → cut to length. Keep a stock of finished lumber for rush jobs (H 123–125).
- **Sheet goods** (H 125–126; W 21–22). The standard sheet is 2,440 × 1,220 mm (8' × 4'). Quoted that way round, plywood's face grain runs along the length; "4 × 8" implies grain across the width and is less common. Larger sheets exist but are rarely cheaper (W 22).
  - **Plywood**: cross-grain veneers; strong over spans (H 125–126). Where solid timber is strong along the grain and weak across it, ply is about half as strong but equally so in all directions. Flexible types bend; decorative or plain faces; fire-retardant grades. Fire rules often require ply up to 18 mm to be certified, which in practice (in the UK) means hardwood ply; softwood ply under 12 mm is mostly poor. Softwood shuttering and site ply (swirling grain, repeating knots) is a cheaper option in thicker sheets. **Most ply isn't truly flat** and relies on a structure to flatten it; inherently flat ply costs more (W 21).
  - **MDF**: smoothest paint surface; no grain; weaker (H 125–126). Sold 3–50 mm thick; fine, dense and uniform, though it can split into layers parallel to the face. Inherently flat in all but thin sheets; fire-retardant grades exist; carves, turns and routs easily. Thin MDF bends and can be laminated. It breaks suddenly when overbent but has good local compressive strength (W 21). MDF dust and formaldehyde: see [ch. 20](../4-fabrication-core/20-health-and-safety.md).
  - **MDO**: plywood core with MDF faces.
  - **Hardboard/"Masonite"**: dense; slick tempered face (H 125–126). Flexible, one rough and one smooth side, few thicknesses. Standard hardboard swells irreversibly when painted, so buy **oil-tempered**. Mainly for shop items such as templates and mould walls (W 21).
  - **Blockboard**: lighter than ply or MDF, with various face veneers (W 31).
  - **Particleboard**: cheap hidden substrate.
  - **Lauan**: thin, light and strong. **Buy FSC-certified**, because lauan has a history of illegal logging.
  - **Homosote**: carvable faux brick; sound-deadening under casters.
  - Easycurve/Upson board; cardboard tubes (Sonotube).
- **Moldings** (H 127–128): learn the profiles (ovolo, cove, cyma recta, ogee, bead, astragal, reeding, egg-and-dart, fluting, rope). Buy them, take them from picture frames, or **router them on the edge of a wide board and then rip them off**, which is safer than routing thin strips. **Stack simple profiles** to build complex ones (H 127–128); Wilson shows a large chalice built entirely from off-the-shelf mouldings (W 23). Panel mouldings, architraves and stair spindles are the most versatile. Specialist moulding firms and timber merchants serving theatre and film have the best ranges; get their **profile charts** so designers can choose from them (W 22). Fake carved moldings with castings, fringe, Ethafoam or stiffened textured materials (H 127–128), or injection-moulded plastic mouldings on picture frames (W 33).

## For the classroom

- **Birdhouse (or box) project.** Hart's project-based recommendation: a small build that teaches cut lists, joinery, gluing and finishing in one afternoon (H 9).
- **Joint sampler.** Students cut and glue a half lap, a bridle, a dado, a rabbet and a dowel joint, then break-test them and relate the results to grain orientation.
- **Faux-furniture challenge.** Students take a plain plywood box and, using applied moldings, panels and a reveal top, make it read as a period cabinet. They must keep it within ±1" of the original footprint.
- **Table-saw certification.** A supervised checkout using Hart's rules as the rubric.
- **Anti-rake it.** Give students a rake as a ratio (say 1 in 8) and a table set at 30° to the steepest slope. They calculate how much to cut from each leg using Wilson's base-line method, then check it on a ramp (W 37–39).
- **Wreck and reinforce.** Show a chair damaged in a fight scene. Students decide whether it needs repair or reinforcement, and design the reinforcement from what the staging does to it (W 36).
