# 51. Set Dressing

> **Sources:** M ch.1 (pp. 10–11), ch.9 (pp. 128–134); H ch.1 (pp. 4, 7). Sections marked **[General knowledge]** aren't from the guide's sources.

**Set dressing** is everything on the set that creates place and atmosphere but isn't handled by actors: books on shelves, pictures on walls, clutter on a desk, curtains at a window. In **theatre** the props department handles it; in **film and TV** a separate **set decoration** department, led by the set decorator, usually does (M 11; H 4, 7; [ch. 3](../1-intro/03-theatre-film-and-tv.md)). It's often the lowest priority on the prop list, and one of the most visible parts of the design.

## Categories (M 10–11; H 4)

- **Set props**: furniture, rugs, and items on floors, walls and ceilings.
- **Trim props**: set props hung on walls, such as curtains, blinds and pictures (H 4).
- **Set dressing**: items that create the world but aren't handled.
- **Set pieces** are scenery built and attached by the scene shop, not props (M 10–11).

See [ch. 1](../1-intro/01-what-is-a-prop.md#the-core-categories).

## Dressing the set (M 128–134)

The core procedures are in [ch. 12](../2-admin/12-rehearsals-tech-and-performance.md#load-in-and-set-dressing-m-128134):
- The **designer makes or approves** every dressing decision; carry a notepad (M 130).
- **Secure everything** that could tip, slide or fall (M 131–132; [securing props](../2-admin/12-rehearsals-tech-and-performance.md#securing-props-m-131132)).
- Keep dressing out of actors' paths, and bring heavy dressing into rehearsal early (M 130).
- **"Hide the uglies"** from every seat before the audience arrives (M 132–133).
- **Tell stage management** about every change (M 134).

## Dressing that tells a story [General knowledge]

- **Start from the character.** Who lives or works here? How much money do they have, how tidy are they, how long have they been here, what do they love? Every object should answer one of those questions.
- **Layer it.** Real rooms build up over time: a new lamp beside an inherited chair, last year's calendar still on the wall, piles that grew. Mix ages and conditions.
- **Work in zones.** Dress the areas the audience looks at most (around the actors' main playing areas, and at eye level) in the most detail.
- **Balance and composition.** Look at the set as a picture from several seats. Group objects in odd numbers, vary heights, and leave negative space so the actors read clearly.
- **Colour.** Dressing can carry the design's palette or quietly break it. Check with the designer and the lighting designer; some colours shift under stage light.
- **Period.** Check every item against the period, including books, packaging and electrical items ([ch. 7](../2-admin/07-research-and-period-accuracy.md)).
- **Books.** Real books are heavy. Shelves are often dressed with hollow book fronts, spines glued to blocks, or lightweight discarded books, with any visible titles checked for period and meaning.

## Sourcing dressing [General knowledge]

Dressing is usually pulled from stock, borrowed or rented rather than built ([Part 3](../3-sourcing/15-shopping.md)). Because there's so much of it:
- **Keep dressing kits in stock** by type (office, kitchen, study, bar, nursery) and by period.
- **Rent in bulk** from rental houses for big naturalistic sets ([ch. 16](../3-sourcing/16-renting.md)).
- **Thrift and charity shops** are the cheapest source of everyday clutter ([ch. 15](../3-sourcing/15-shopping.md)).
- **Check fire safety**: paper, fabric and dried plants near lights need flame-retardant treatment ([ch. 27](../4-fabrication-core/27-surfaces-and-texture.md#flame-retardancy-h-320)).

## Film and TV set decoration [General knowledge]

On screen, set decoration is its own department. The set decorator designs the dressing with the production designer; a **buyer** finds items; a **leadperson** runs the crew of **set dressers**, who dress, maintain and strike the sets. Continuity photographs record every placement so the set matches across days of shooting ([ch. 3](../1-intro/03-theatre-film-and-tv.md#film-h-23-7-15)).

## For the classroom

- **Character room.** Students dress a small corner (a desk, a shelf, a bedside table) for a character from a play, then classmates guess facts about the character from the dressing alone.
- **Dressing plot.** For one scene, students produce a dressing list and a plan drawing showing where every piece goes, its source and how it's secured.
- **Front-row walk.** After dressing a set, students walk the house from the front row and the balcony and log every "ugly" they find (M 132–133).
