# 53. Careers and Professional Practice

> **Sources:** M ch.2 (pp. 15–25), ch.4 (pp. 36–45); H ch.1 (pp. 6–10); B (front matter, pp. 102–109). Sections marked **[General knowledge]** aren't from the guide's sources.
> **[Check current]** Union rules, pay rates, job titles and hiring practices vary by country, city and employer, and change.

Props skills lead to many kinds of work. This chapter covers where prop people work, how they get hired, and how to build a career. The prop master's role and duties are in [ch. 2](../1-intro/02-the-prop-master.md).

## Where prop makers work (H 6–8)

- **Theatre**: regional and resident companies, Broadway and commercial touring, opera, dance, and university and school theatres (H 6–7).
- **Film and television**: props and set decoration departments, outside prop shops, rental houses (H 7–8).
- **Commercials and music videos**: often more hands-on, especially non-union work (H 8).
- **Other careers** (H 8): effects, models, puppetry, animatronics, magic, display and exhibition work, trade shows, events, museums, theme parks, haunted attractions, and private commissions.
- **Museum replicas** are a niche but real employment opportunity (B 104).

## Job titles (M 17, 19; H 6–7)

Properties director, prop master (properties manager, supervisor, designer), props coordinator, assistant prop master, prop artisans (props carpenters, props craftspeople, soft-goods artisans), and shopper/buyers. See [ch. 2](../1-intro/02-the-prop-master.md#where-props-sits-in-the-organization) for what each does.

## Skills employers want (M 20–23)

Mussman's lists: drafting, woodworking, sewing, painting, crafts, metalwork, computer graphics and spreadsheets, research and shopping; and personal attributes including responsibility, common sense, creative problem-solving, attention to detail, flexibility, a good memory, networking, organisation and self-teaching (M 20–23). No one arrives knowing all of them; learn constantly (M 20–21).

Berls adds that employers value people who have failed and come back: "I would rather hire an individual that has failed on a project and then revisited it with success than a person who has never tasted failure" (B 24).

## Contracts and protecting yourself (M 41–45)

- Paid positions usually come with a **letter of agreement**: check that it states pay rate, pay period and method, term, benefits and duties (M 41–45).
- Positions may be **freelance, seasonal or year-round** (M 41–45).
- **Ask what hours are expected**, and compare your workload against your time early (M 41–45).
- Look after yourself: sleep, eat, know your limits and use your vacation (M 41–45).

## Building a career [General knowledge]

- **Portfolio.** Photograph every prop you make: finished, in progress, and onstage under lights. Show your process (research, drawings, mock-ups, builds) as well as results. Keep a simple website or online portfolio and a printed or tablet version for interviews. Appendix C lists portfolio guides (Jaen, *Show Case*; Moody, *The Business of Theatrical Design*) ([Appendix C](../../appendices/C-sources-and-editions.md#further-reading-cited-by-the-sources)).
- **Résumé.** List productions with company, role and year, plus skills and machine certifications. Keep it to one or two pages.
- **Network.** Most props work is found through people. Work summer stock, assist experienced prop masters, attend conferences and join professional groups, and stay in touch with everyone you've worked with.
- **Training.** University programmes, apprenticeships, internships and short courses all lead into props. Keep adding skills, especially in-demand ones like digital fabrication ([ch. 40](../5-materials/40-3d-printing.md)), upholstery, metalwork and electronics.
- **Unions.** In many places professional film, TV and large theatre props work is unionised (in the US, under IATSE locals). Union membership affects pay, benefits, hours and which tasks you may perform (H 6–7). Find out how membership works in your area.
- **Freelancing.** Keep good records, set aside money for taxes, use written agreements for every job, price your labour honestly (including shopping and travel time), and keep receipts ([ch. 8](../2-admin/08-budgets-and-paperwork.md)).
- **Reputation.** Reliability, honesty about problems ([ch. 10](../2-admin/10-working-with-other-departments.md#raise-your-red-flag-early-m-107108-127-137)) and good etiquette with lenders and colleagues ([ch. 2](../1-intro/02-the-prop-master.md#professional-etiquette-m-2325)) are what get you hired again.

## For the classroom

- **Portfolio page.** Each student builds one portfolio page for a prop they made: research, process photos, finished photo and a short description.
- **Job ad analysis.** Students find three current props job adverts in different fields (theatre, film, theme park or museum) and compare required skills with their own.
- **Interview.** Students interview a working prop person about how they got their first job and what they look for when hiring (M 15–25).
- **Career map.** Students map a five-year plan from graduation to a target props job, including skills to learn and people to meet.
