# 12. Rehearsals, Tech and Performance

> **Sources:** M ch.9 (pp. 120–140); H ch.2 (pp. 13, 15–16), ch.6 (pp. 95–96)

## Rehearsal props

A **rehearsal prop** stands in for the performance prop. It must closely match the real thing **in size and function**, and ideally in weight, but it can be pulled from stock, found, or quickly built (M 120; H 2). A rehearsal stand-in for furniture may itself be a build, so its dimensions match the real item (W 181). Interactive props may be needed in rehearsal long before the production moves onto the stage, so schedule them first (W 13).

**Priorities** (M 121):
1. Props that matter to the action: hand props and set props.
2. Set dressing that affects blocking.
3. Everything else.

**Timing.** Professional actors usually expect props from the first blocking rehearsal. Agree the deadline for initial rehearsal props with the director and SM **before rehearsals begin** (M 121).

**Swap in real props as early as possible.** The longer actors work with the real prop, the better they adapt to it. Even small differences can throw them off (M 121). A rehearsal prop that's safe and that the team loves may become the performance prop.

**When you can't supply a stand-in** (special orders, complex builds), explain to the room what's coming and show research, drawings or model pieces (M 121).

**Mock-ups** (H 95–96) are full-scale stand-ins made from cheap, easily modified materials. They:
- let actors work with the prop while the design is still evolving
- let the director and designer judge scale next to real bodies, especially for furniture
- reduce waste if built from salvaged set lumber or shipping cardboard

Build rehearsal props cheaply but safely. If use is uncertain or the team wants to "discover" it in rehearsal, getting a rehearsal prop in early tells you how to build the real one (H 13).

## Keeping the rehearsal room organized (M 121–122)

- Every prop gets a designated spot.
- Place furniture per the ground plan, adjusted to the room.
- Set up tables or shelves at the "wings."
- **Deliver props outside rehearsal time**: before, after, or during breaks. Never interrupt rehearsal unless the director or SM asks you to.
- Meet stage management regularly about:
  - which props are rented or need special care
  - how trick props work
  - which rehearsal props are actually the final props

## The rehearsal report (M 122–126)

The SM sends a daily report listing what was rehearsed, run times, the next day's schedule and **notes for every department**. Sample prop notes from Mussman's illustration:
- "Letter should be smaller—4" × 6"."
- "Add a cup of water."
- "Fix the armchair; the seat sags."
- "Add two trumpets."
- "The coffee table was tipped over in the fight."

**How to handle reports:**
- **Build time into the start of each day** to read and respond (M 126).
- Update the prop list and the to-do list, and decide what's urgent.
- **Reply to the SM** with answers and an estimated delivery date for each note.
- **Ask clarifying questions** about vague notes (see the tea example in [ch. 10](10-working-with-other-departments.md#clarify-vague-requests-m-126)).
- **Read every department's notes**, because some affect props or have been filed in the wrong section (M 124).
- Watch for evidence of **use and abuse**. If the report reveals that an actor now jousts with the table lamp, swap the antique for a reinforced replica before it breaks (M 124–125). Actors in performance will go further than they did in rehearsal, so plan for that (H 13).
- **Check the budget** before promising new items (M 127).

## Caring about actors (M 125–126)

Actors find every hazard: unsanded edges, forgotten staples, screw tips through upholstery, knives a bit too sharp. Fix these immediately. **Inspect your props as if you were the actor.** Hart's habit is to run your hands over the entire prop before handing it over (H 41, 121), carefully on metal, because a razor edge cuts before you feel it (H 157).

## Attending rehearsals and meetings (M 127)

Production meetings are required. Watch at least a rehearsal or two before tech to see how props are actually used.

## The prop request deadline (M 127–128)

Set a polite but firm cutoff for **major** additions or changes, usually a few days before tech. It lets the department catch up so that everything already requested is ready for tech. Minor notes will keep coming, and that's normal.

## Load-in and set dressing (M 128–134)

**Prop load-in** usually happens the day before tech, when the stage is free and the actors no longer need rehearsal props. Coordinate stage time with the other departments and bring the transportable tool kit and enough people to carry things.

**Stage management knows where everything lives.** Rely on them for preset positions and for where props travel during the show (M 128).

### Prop tables (M 129)
- Place them **out of traffic paths and scene-shift routes**. Mussman has seen tables flattened by moving scenery and an actor run into one coming off a bright stage into the dark.
- Cover with **brown kraft paper**, not white, which reflects stage light.
- Grid the surface with tape or marker and **label each space** with its prop name. Traced outlines work until a prop changes.
- Light them with **blue-gelled, low-wattage clip lights**. An actor who grabs the butcher knife instead of the flowers is in trouble.
- Hand food and consumable replacements to the SM at load-in, and **demonstrate any food preparation** and the operation of special props.

### Dressing the set (M 130)
Dressing is the payoff after weeks of work: setting furniture, hanging pictures and shelves, installing window treatments, wiring practicals, filling surfaces, and **securing everything**. The **designer makes or approves** every decision, so always carry a notepad.

- Keep placements consistent with rehearsal, and don't introduce new dressing into actors' paths.
- Bring heavy dressing into rehearsal early if actors must work around it.
- Ask where unplotted dressing goes.

### Securing props (M 131–132)
**Secure** anything that could be knocked over, anything that could slide (rugs, sofas, tables, chairs), and anything that hangs. Two of Mussman's stories:
- A prop fell off a moving wagon during a scene shift and stopped the show.
- A rug curled from years in storage tripped an actor.

| Method | Good for | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Mortite / putty** | Small items on surfaces | May leave slight residue |
| **Staple gun** | Rug edges, fabric, paper | Small holes |
| **Screws** | Light fixtures, rods, shelves, pictures. Angled through upstage legs into the deck; corner irons | Larger holes; irons may be visible |
| **Tie line** | Tying almost anything | None; easily removed |
| **Gaff tape** | Quick holds, light-leak seams | Can pull paint or veneer |
| **Felt/plastic glides** | Letting things slide; protecting floors | — |

Before choosing a method, ask: Is the item rented, and what am I allowed to do to it? Am I willing to trade value for safety? What does the show need?

### "Hide the uglies" (M 132–133)
Before audiences arrive, walk the house, including the **front row and the balcony**, and fix:
- dust, smudges and things needing repair
- loose cord ends (dress cords neatly and hide them)
- reflective glass, mirrors and metal (dull them; see [ch. 28](../4-fabrication-core/28-painting-and-finishing.md))
- excess Mortite
- visible screws
- loose threads
- wrinkled fabric (iron or steam it)
- ugly furniture backs facing the audience (panel them or cover with black fabric)
- raw wood not called for by the design (stain, paint or black it out)
- visible undersides and tops
- screw tips poking through to the backstage side of flats (pad and tape them)

**After load-in, tell the SM about every change**: anything added, cut, moved or altered (M 134).

## Tech rehearsals (M 134–137)

- **The first day of tech is the deadline** for performance props. Arriving with about 90% done, with only minor dressing left, is on track. Missing hand props or major set props is a problem (M 134).
- Missing props hurt more than the actors:
  - **Lighting**: prop color and reflectance affect levels and gel choices, and positions affect focus.
  - **Crew**: shift rehearsals.
  - **Director and designer**: they need to see the full picture (M 135).
- **Tech hours.** Regional tech days of 6–12 hours are set by managers under union rules, and the prop crew's day often runs longer, with limited stage access (M 135) **[check current: your union and institution rules]**.
- **Watching rehearsal.** If you have staff, delegate and watch. If you are a department of one, keep working, but tell the PM, director, SM and designer where you'll be and how to reach you. **Don't miss run-throughs** (M 136).
- **Notes sessions** follow each tech and preview. Add the notes to your lists and finish them fast, because actors' time with new props shrinks every day. Never risk your health or safety to meet a note (M 136–137).

## Performances (M 137–138)

Once the show opens, notes come only through the SM's **performance reports**, usually maintenance and repair. The prop list is now final.

**Prop security:**
- Enforce **"don't touch someone else's props."** A prop moved even a few feet can be lost at a critical moment.
- Expect a **preshow prop check** by stage management and actors.
- Decide **what gets locked up** each night: the whole auditorium, everything not attached, or only valuables and **weapons**. The prop master should help make that decision.

**Running maintenance.** From tech onward, finished props are largely **stage management's responsibility**. Ongoing tasks include food preparation, effect resets, minor repairs and replenishing consumables (M 138).

**When furniture breaks in the run** (W 36): if a sound piece broke because of how it's used onstage (a fight, a dance), it will break again however well it's mended, and there will be pressure to get it back fast. Treat it as a **reinforcement** job based on what the staging does to it, not just a repair, because performer safety is at stake. Temporary splints and brackets only stop damage spreading. Methods in [ch. 29](../5-materials/29-wood.md#mending-and-reinforcing-furniture-w-3436).

## Co-productions (M 138–139)

If your theatre builds a show that will transfer:
- Prepare a packet with photos, the prop list and a **numbered placement spreadsheet**.
- Pack props grouped by stage location.
- Have rentals returned to you first so you can return them to their owners.

## For the classroom

- **Prop-table build.** Students design and lay out a prop table for a short scene: paper, grid, labels and lighting. Peers then run a mock preset check.
- **Rehearsal-report drill.** Hand students five days of mock rehearsal reports. They triage the notes, update a prop list and draft replies to the SM.
- **"Hide the uglies" walk.** On a dressed set (or a classroom set-up), students find and log every distraction from three audience positions.
