# 11. Buy, Source or Build?

> **Sources:** M ch.6 (pp. 78–91), ch.8 (pp. 109–119); H ch.1 (pp. 4–6), ch.5 (pp. 60–61), ch.19 (pp. 363–364); W ch.1 (p. 8), ch.2 (pp. 22, 30–31)

Every prop on the list gets the same first question: **pull it, borrow it, rent it, buy it, or build it?** This chapter covers how to decide. ## Start with rehearsal props

The first physical step in propping a show is supplying **rehearsal props**, the stand-ins actors use from the start of blocking (M 109). Mussman insists this is *not* "propping the show twice": digging through stock for rehearsal props shows you which performance props you already own (M 109–110). More in [ch. 12](12-rehearsals-tech-and-performance.md).

## The four-way to-do list (M 110–112)

Go through the prop list and cost breakdown prop by prop and sort each into one of four lists:

| List | Typical candidates (M 111) |
|---|---|
| **To Find / Pull** | Items already in stock, or findable for free |
| **To Borrow / Rent** | Set props and dressing (often too costly to buy) |
| **To Buy** | Building materials, consumables, and things cheaper to buy than to rent or hunt down |
| **To Build** | Show-specific items, props that take abuse or must work a particular way, things that will be permanently altered, anything that must match designer drawings |

Questions to ask for each prop (M 111):
- Is it in stock?
- Will it be hard to find?
- Is it cheaper to buy or to build?
- Can it wait (dressing), or is it a priority (hand props)?
- How is it used?
- Does it need special ordering, with lead time?
- Does it still fit the budget?
- Who can do it: crew or overhire?

The list makes your plan visible and lets assistants **pick up where you left off**. It will change constantly: borrowed items turn into purchases, purchases into rentals, and props you spent days building get cut (M 111–112). ## Build (M 115–117; H 5–6)

Theatre "builds" everything. Build when:
- the prop is specific to the play or design
- it must survive abuse
- the antique would be too fragile
- it must look, weigh, feel or function a particular way

Follow designer drawings, and deviate only with the designer's approval (M 116). Parts 4 and 5 of this guide cover building in depth. Two quick strategies:

- **Found objects and kitbashing.** Model makers "kitbash" by borrowing parts from commercial kits. For a prop maker, "the world is their kit to bash" (H 60–61). A golf ball becomes a curtain-rod finial (M 116). Collect interesting parts as you find them and organize them by material, by shape, or both (H 61).
- **Beating "creator's block"** (M 116–117):
  1. Research how similar real objects are built.
  2. If there's no reference, picture the finished object in detail.
  3. Mentally take it apart: how do the parts connect, and what's inside?
  4. Match each part to a material or found object.
  5. Still stuck? Ask a colleague.

## Why build instead of buy?

Hart gives three reasons to build (H 5–6):

1. **It doesn't exist.** Imaginary or design-specific objects, or real objects in a specific size or look: an oil portrait of the lead actor, an oversized mug, furniture in forced perspective.
2. **It must do something real objects weren't made for.** Furniture gets danced on, thrown, tipped and carried in quick changes. Large items get faked in lighter materials.
3. **The real thing is too expensive**: gold, antiques, hardwood finishes, real food every night.

Wilson's reasons (W 8) overlap and add one:
1. **It can't be got any other way**: a stuffed tableau of a dodo fighting an alien, or, more likely, attempts to buy, borrow or hire have failed.
2. **The design is very specific** in size or look: a portrait that's a likeness of a cast member, a kite made by a ten-year-old, furniture that must fit a certain space. Furniture that was once grand but is now battered is surprisingly hard to find, because hire firms keep stock in good shape "and will not appreciate your ripping out the stuffing."
3. **It must perform a technical function or take rough treatment**: a china Buddha broken nightly, a chaise upended by two dancers and climbed on.
4. **It's cheaper to make than to buy or hire.** On a long run or tour, **hire charges can exceed the cost of making**; bought goods may be finished in richer materials than the stage needs; and making is often cheapest where labour is already paid for.

Whether to use a prop is an artistic decision for the director and designer; **how to get it** is also logistical and financial, involving the production manager or stage manager, the designer, and the prop maker if it may need making (W 8). Much stage furniture is hired, bought or borrowed, and built only when that's cheaper or the design requires it; buying hard-to-make parts such as turned or cabriole legs is a middle way (W 22, 30–31).

Hart also warns that **adapting** a store-bought object can cost more than building one. You have to match its existing finish, and modern furniture is often honeycomb paper under a thin skin, with nothing to fasten to (H 6).

## For the classroom

- **Sourcing sprint.** Give teams a prop list and a fake budget. Each team produces a four-way to-do list with justifications and a sourcing plan for the five hardest items.
- **Agreement drafting.** Students write a loan agreement for borrowing a valuable antique from a private lender. Discuss what makes the lender comfortable.
- **Kitbash challenge.** Students build a "sci-fi device" or "steampunk tool" from thrift-store and hardware-store found objects within a time limit.
