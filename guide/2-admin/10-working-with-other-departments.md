# 10. Working with Other Departments

> **Sources:** M ch.3 (pp. 26–35), ch.7 (pp. 102, 108), ch.8 (p. 119), ch.9; H ch.2 (pp. 13–14), ch.6 (pp. 80–81)

Props sits at the center of a production's communication web. Mussman estimates that a prop master talks with at least fifteen different people on a typical day and reframes that load positively: **"conversing equals information sharing"** (M 30). Most prop problems are communication problems that surfaced late.

## Who you work with, and what each needs from you

### Production staff

| Person | Their role | What props shares with them |
|---|---|---|
| **Production manager** | Your direct supervisor; sets budgets and the season calendar; solves production-wide problems | Budget status, schedule risks and early warnings ("red flags") (M 31) |
| **Technical director / scene shop** | Turns designs into working drawings and builds and installs the set | Shared shop time and tools, load-in schedules, built-in vs. prop furniture, anything attached to scenery (M 31–32) |
| **Scenic artist** | Paints and textures the set | Color mixing, paint techniques, matching finishes. Treat their tools with respect (M 32) |
| **Master electrician / electrics** | Hangs, focuses and runs lighting (and sometimes sound) | **Every practical**, window treatments and anything wired (M 32) |
| **Costume shop manager** | Runs the costume build and fittings | Who supplies costume props, color coordination. Props often borrows costume-shop machines (M 32–33) |
| **Stage manager & team** | Runs rehearsals and performances; tracks every prop backstage | Prop changes from rehearsal, repair notes from performances, special-handling information, rented-item lists (M 33) |

### Creative team and guest artists

| Person | What props shares with them |
|---|---|
| **Director** | Final say on concept and function. They tend to focus on hand props (M 33, 100) |
| **Set designer** | Your primary design partner for props and dressing. They tend to focus on set props and dressing (M 34, 100) |
| **Lighting designer** | Practical options that serve both the set and the light plot (M 34) |
| **Costume designer** | Props that complete a character's look (M 34) |
| **Sound designer** | Live effects such as crash boxes, thunder sheets and door slams when the director prefers live sound to recorded (M 34) |
| **Choreographers** (fight and dance) | Props needed early so actors can train with them (M 34) |
| **Music director** | Instruments played onstage (M 34) |
| **Actors** | How the prop functions in their hands. Most notes route through the SM or director (M 35) |

### The wider organization

Theatre is a business. Board members, the artistic director, the managing director, and the development, marketing, finance and audience-services departments all affect props (M 26–30):

- **Board and managing director**: community contacts who can find hard-to-get props or services.
- **Development**: may ask for auction items.
- **Marketing**: window displays, photo shoots.
- **Finance**: receipts and reimbursements.
- **Box office**: complimentary tickets you can use to barter with lenders, where policy allows.

Expect occasional requests to help with fundraisers or school visits. Learn and use everyone's correct title; mixing up "Director of" and "Manager of" can offend (M 28).

## Communication practices that work

### Start the conversation yourself (M 102)
Once you have a preliminary prop list (see [ch. 6](06-script-breakdown-and-prop-list.md)):
1. Send it to the director and set designer and explain what it is based on.
2. Ask them to add to it, change it or cut from it, and request a meeting.
3. Come with questions about concept, period and use. This is the time to ask why *Hamlet* is set in 1950s small-town America.
4. Take thorough notes and update the list immediately.

### Share findings for approval (M 119)
When you find a candidate prop, send photographs **with dimensions** to the designer and director, especially for important props. You are part of the decision-making process. You are not propping the show alone.

### Raise your red flag early (M 107–108, 127, 137)
If money, time or know-how looks insufficient, say so **as early as possible** and propose alternatives. Mussman recounts a show where she didn't raise a concern in time: the prop was finished the afternoon of the first preview, and the actors never rehearsed with it. Don't cry wolf, but don't stay silent.

### Never use constraints as an excuse (M 107)
If budget or time truly prevents something, explain the situation and **offer alternatives**. If you simply don't want to do it, that's a different conversation.

### Clarify vague requests (M 126)
A note reading "add tea for the actors" hides a dozen questions:
- Hot or iced? Sweetened? Bags or loose?
- Decaf? Herbal?
- Glass or plastic? A tray and pitcher?
- How much, and from when?

Ask before you buy.

### Production meetings are not optional (M 127)
Bring your **prop bible**. If you must miss a meeting, tell your supervisor and the SM and send a representative. Use meetings to raise anything that crosses departments, such as size limits set by a costume pocket or a doorway (H 14).

### Read the whole rehearsal report (M 124)
Notes in other departments' sections often affect props. A set note like "the front door will be slammed" means the wall dressing needs securing. Costume-prop notes are often filed under the wrong department.

### Critical measurements and relative dimensions (H 80–81)
Designers often specify sizes relative to other elements: "standard chair height," "taller than the actor," "must fit inside the champagne box." Keep in contact with whoever controls those elements while you build.

## Working with actors

- Actors need time to get used to props. Get real props into rehearsal as early as possible (M 121).
- **Put yourself in the actor's place.** Find stray staples, rough edges and sharp screw tips before they do. Actors will find every one (M 125).
- Remember that the prop matters more to the actor than to you. They live with it for the whole run, while you move on to the next show (M 125–126).
- Paper props deserve particular care. Some actors want their lines written on the page and some want nothing like their lines there. **Never write anything "funny"**, because it can break an actor's focus onstage (H 353).

## For the classroom

- **Role-play.** Run a mock production meeting. Students play director, set designer, lighting designer, costume designer, SM and prop master, working through a list of 10 contentious props such as a practical lamp, a sword, a stained tablecloth or a period phone.
- **Writing exercise.** Give students a vague rehearsal note and have them draft a professional email reply asking for clarification, with an estimated delivery date.
- **Case study.** Discuss Mussman's "red flag" anecdote. At what point should she have spoken up, and to whom?
