# 7. Research and Period Accuracy

> **Sources:** J (whole book, especially the introduction, pp. 9–10); M pp. 78–82, 95
> **Companion:** [Appendix E — Period Props Timeline](../../appendices/E-period-props-timeline.md) gives quick-reference dates by object category.
> **[Check current]** James's book was written in 1992 and draws on popular histories. Treat its dates as a **starting point** and confirm anything that will be seen close up, photographed or filmed (see [Limits of the source](#limits-of-the-source-and-how-to-verify) below).
> **See also:** B's museum-replica and period-jewellery projects (scanning, rebuilding missing parts, matching patina) in [ch. 40](../5-materials/40-3d-printing.md#further-projects-b-102116) (B 104–109).

Props establish the world of a play: its period, place, class and mood ([ch. 1](../1-intro/01-what-is-a-prop.md)). One wrong object can break that world. Thurston James spent 14 years as prop master at UCLA, and he points out that nobody can be expected to *know* when everyday objects came into use. Could a Shakespearean character wear eyeglasses? Should Mercutio smoke a tobacco pipe? Did young Abraham Lincoln read by a kerosene lamp? And recent history is no easier: when did pinball get flippers, or households get microwave ovens or CD players? James notes that **recent history is no easier to find in the library than ancient history** (J 9).

His answer is a reference organized around three questions, which also make a good research method for any prop:

1. **What** is it called, and what does it look like? (Names matter. You need the right word to search for it, to talk to the designer and to label it in the prop list.)
2. **Where** was it used? (Country, region, city or countryside, social class.)
3. **When** was it introduced, and **when was it common**?

## "Invented" is not the same as "in use"

The most common research mistake is to treat an invention date as the date an object appeared in ordinary homes. James's entries give the date an object was *introduced*, and he repeatedly shows the gap between introduction and everyday use:

- **Equipment stays in service for decades.** A Bell telephone model might be installed 15 years after its introduction and then last 20 more, so it could be in use **35 years or longer**. Old and new equipment worked side by side: operator-connected phones ran on the same lines as dial phones, and dial phones worked alongside touch-tone (J 188).
- **Technology reaches cities first.** Early dial telephones were useful only in big cities that had automatic exchanges (J 189). Heavy stove-heated **sad irons** stayed in use in rural America into the 1930s, until electric wiring arrived (J 118).
- **Old ways persist.** Terra-cotta oil lamps and animal-fat candles were in constant use from ancient Egypt to the end of the American Civil War (J 98). Long after metal tools existed, flint knives were kept for ceremonies (J 179). People opened cans with a pocket knife into the mid-20th century, a hundred years after the can opener was invented (J 117).
- **Class matters.** The wealthy got new things first. Chamber pots, rush lights and snuff rasps belonged to ordinary households long after the rich had moved on (J 38, 49, 102).

**[Added best practice]** Treat a date as **"no earlier than."** Then ask a second question: *for this character, in this place and income bracket, would it be new, normal or old-fashioned?* A widow in a farmhouse in 1925 may have a hand-cranked wall telephone and a sad iron, while her city cousin has a candlestick phone and an electric iron. That contrast is a storytelling opportunity, not just a fact to check.

The same applies to furniture. **[Added best practice]** Real rooms accumulate objects over generations. A style's date tells you the *earliest* a piece could appear. A convincing period room usually mixes current pieces with older, inherited ones, with the proportion set by the household's wealth and taste.

## Classic anachronism traps

James's introduction poses questions like the ones below (J 9). The table answers them from his entries, with corrections where historians differ (marked †).

| Question | What the chronology says |
|---|---|
| Eyeglasses in Shakespeare? | Yes. Two-lens spectacles were used by the wealthy and the clergy from the 14th century, and printing created wider demand in the 16th. But they were held, clipped to the nose or tied on with straps. **Sidepieces (temples) arrived only around 1727–1728** (J 112–114). |
| Mercutio with a tobacco pipe? | Tobacco reached Spain from the Americas in 1518 and the English court in 1558, and Raleigh made pipe smoking fashionable in the late 1500s (J 32, 38–39). That works for an Elizabethan staging. For a production set in the play's original medieval Verona, it's an anachronism. |
| Young Lincoln reading by kerosene? | No. Kerosene arrived in the 1850s–1860s, when Lincoln was in his forties. A boyhood scene calls for firelight, tallow candles or a simple grease lamp (J 101, 104). |
| A clock with a minute hand in a 16th-century scene? | No. Clocks had a single hour hand for about 300 years. James dates the minute hand to 1670; † other sources put it between the 1650s and about 1690 (J 92). |
| A sealed envelope in a Jane Austen adaptation? | Unlikely. Letters were folded and sealed with wax. † James dates the envelope to 1839 (New York). Envelopes existed earlier but were rare in Britain until the 1840 postal reforms (J 108). |
| Striking a match in 1800? | No. The friction match dates to 1827, and safety matches to the 1840s–1850s. Before that, flint, steel and tinder were used, or a wood **spill** was lit from the fire (J 105–107). |
| A man's wristwatch before World War I? | Rare. Wristwatches were a novelty among court ladies in the 18th century. Men considered them effeminate until soldiers proved them practical in World War I (J 97). |
| Pinball with flippers in a 1940s bar? | Only from 1947 on (J 47). |

## What the chronology teaches about behavior and staging

James's entries are full of details that become **business**: things for actors to do, and reasons for objects to be where they are.

- **Early rooms had few surfaces.** Rooms in the time of Elizabeth I, James I and Louis XIII–XIV were sparsely furnished, so spectacles, keys, snuff, watches and books were carried or worn, pinned or chained to clothing. Side tables and occasional tables didn't become common until the early 18th century (J 76–77). A noblewoman's **chatelaine** watch hung at her waist with her keys, purse and mirror (J 93). Early watches weren't carried in pockets either. They sat on tables, hung around the neck or were fastened to clothing (J 95). **Coordinate with costumes.**
- **Furniture was shaped by dress.** The broad farthingale chair and the wide-armed caqueteuse ("gossip's chair") made room for hoop skirts (J 63, 66). Check costume renderings before choosing period chairs.
- **Chairs signal status.** James calls the chair the clearest indicator of its owner's rank and taste (J 60). The hard, unupholstered **hall chair**, often carved with the family crest, was for messengers and servants in muddy outdoor clothes (J 67).
- **Light was dim, smoky and needed tending.** Before the Argand lamp of 1784, lamps gave weak, smoky light. A **rush light** had to be repositioned constantly and its falling embers put out. The Betty lamp needed its wick picked forward with an attached pick (J 98–102). Lighting a pipe before matches meant carrying a lit spill from the fireplace (J 105). These are ready-made pieces of business for actors.
- **Seeing aids were fashionable, hearing aids weren't.** Monocles and lorgnettes were carried for show even without any need (J 112–113). Ear trumpets and speaking tubes, by contrast, were associated with infirmity and usually hidden (J 115). Either can define a character.
- **Telephones dictated posture.** The first commercial box phone made the user bend down to shout into it, then turn their head to listen at the same opening (J 188–189). Each era's telephone gives actors a different physical vocabulary.
- **Clock faces.** Roman numerals dominated until the late 1800s, and many dials show **IIII** rather than IV for visual balance (J 94). A modern Arabic-numeral clock in a period set is an easy mistake to spot.
- **Ceremony and rank.** In 15th-century England, a flag's size and shape showed the bearer's rank. A king might cut the swallowtails off a knight's pennon on the battlefield to promote him (J 138, 140). Public punishments drew crowds in a carnival atmosphere (J 141).

## Furniture styles and their names

James includes a dictionary of furniture styles (J 87–91). The naming conventions are worth teaching because designers and rental houses use them constantly:

- **English styles** are named for monarchs (Tudor, Elizabethan, Jacobean, Carolean, William and Mary, Queen Anne, Georgian, Regency, Victorian) or for designers whose **pattern books** spread their designs (Chippendale, Hepplewhite, Sheraton, Adam).
- **French styles** are named for kings (Louis XIV, XV, XVI) or political periods (Directoire, Empire).
- **Movements** cut across countries: Renaissance, Baroque, Rococo, Arts and Crafts, Art Nouveau.
- **Revivals complicate everything.** Victorian makers revived Louis XV, Gothic and Greek motifs, and the Regency revived the Greek klismos chair (J 67, 90–91). A "Louis XV" chair in a play set in 1880 may well be a Victorian reproduction, which is itself period-accurate.

See [Appendix E](../../appendices/E-period-props-timeline.md#10-furniture-style-periods) for date ranges.

## Arms, armor and authority: vocabulary first

James's warfare sections are mainly a **vocabulary and dating guide**: helmet types (great helm, bascinet, sallet, armet, burgonet, cabasset), armor pieces from gorget to sabaton, polearms (bill, halberd, glaive, partisan, spontoon), and lock mechanisms (matchlock, wheel lock, flintlock) (J 149–176). Precise names let you talk clearly with designers, fight directors and rental houses, and pick a helmet or firearm that matches the period. James himself notes that the lines between knife, dagger, machete and sword are blurry (J 162).

**Safety and policy come first.** Everything in [ch. 42](../6-weapons/42-weapons-safety-and-policy.md#what-the-sources-say-m-147149-h-7) applies, and James's 1992 descriptions predate current weapons practice. Period accuracy never overrides your institution's weapons policy or the fight director's requirements.

Civil-authority objects (scepters, orbs, crowns, standards, flags and the vocabulary of flag parts such as hoist, fly, canton and halyard) are covered in J 131–140. These props often appear in histories and Shakespeare.

## Religious and ceremonial objects

James covers Catholic and Jewish ceremonial objects (J 195–206). Two points generalize:

- **Some traditions are very stable.** James notes that Catholic altar furnishings and sacred vessels changed little from about 300 AD until the 1960s (J 195). This makes them easy to date and easy to get wrong in detail: getting a chalice, ciborium, monstrance, cruets or the altar linens wrong is noticeable to anyone who knows them.
- **Some traditions leave form open.** James notes that Jewish tradition prescribes the function of ritual objects more than their form. A Kiddush cup can be any cup that holds wine, plain or ornate, which gives the designer freedom (J 203).

**[Added best practice]**
- **Consult practitioners** (clergy, community members, a religious-studies colleague) for any production that depicts worship or ritual. Ask about correct objects *and* handling customs.
- **Don't use consecrated or genuine ritual items** (a real Torah scroll, consecrated hosts, a functioning mezuzah with scripture) as props. Build or source purpose-made replicas, and ask about sensitivities such as writing divine names.
- **Remember what's missing.** James covers only two traditions, and the Catholic material reflects pre-1992 practice. (For example, he lists black as the Good Friday color. Red replaced it in the Roman rite with the 1970 Missal.) Islamic, Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh, Orthodox, Protestant and Indigenous objects need their own research and consultation.

## Limits of the source, and how to verify

James is candid about his book's limits (J 10). It focuses on Western Europe and the United States, mentions the Middle East and Asia mainly where they introduced something, and probably over-represents England. In addition:

- **It stops in 1992.** It can't tell you about smartphones, streaming or anything since. In 1992 James saw the CD as king and digital audio tape as the coming thing (J 121–122).
- **It relies on popular histories** (for example Panati's *Extraordinary Origins of Everyday Things*) and collectors' guides (J 207–209). Some dates are legends or disputed, and some descriptions contain errors (see the table below).
- **Some terminology is dated.** One weapons entry uses an ethnic slur that shouldn't be repeated in class materials.

**Spot-checks made for this guide** (examples where James differs from other sources; † = corrected in Appendix E):

**[Added best practice] How to verify**
1. **Match the effort to the exposure.** A chair seen from 50 feet in a proscenium house needs the right silhouette. A letter read in close-up on camera needs the right paper, ink, folding and handwriting ([ch. 39](../5-materials/39-paper-and-graphics.md)).
2. **Prefer primary evidence.** Use period photographs, paintings, trade catalogs (both Mussman and James recommend Sears catalog reprints), dated advertisements, patents, newspapers and surviving objects in museum collections. Large museums (the Metropolitan Museum, the V&A, the Smithsonian) and national libraries put large collections online **[check current]**.
3. **Use two independent sources** for any date that matters, and record where each came from.
4. **Ask the director.** Some productions deliberately mix periods. Accuracy serves the concept, not the other way around (M 95).

## Building a research file

James kept a personal research file of clippings covering "everything" (his filled two packed file cabinets), and he argues that every theatre practitioner needs one (J 9). Mussman and Hart make the same point about keeping a library of references and a research file from past shows (M 78–82; [ch. 15](../3-sourcing/15-shopping.md#catalogs-and-references-m-7882)).

**[Added best practice] A digital research file:**
- Organize it by **object category**, then **period** (for example *Lighting → 1850–1900*). That matches how prop lists and Appendix E are organized.
- With every image, save the **source, the date of the object and the date of the image**. An undated picture is only inspiration, not evidence.
- Save the designer's research for each show with the show's prop bible ([ch. 13](13-strike-storage-and-records.md)), so the next production can reuse it.
- Note anachronisms you've caught, and how you caught them. That list becomes your best teaching material.

## For the classroom

- **Anachronism hunt.** Show production photos or a scene from a period film. Students list every object, date each one with sources, and flag anything that's anachronistic, unlikely for the character's class, or deliberately stylized.
- **Object biography.** Each student picks one object (oil lamp, telephone, fountain pen, chair) and traces when it was invented, when it became common, who owned it, and how long it stayed in use. They present it with primary-source images.
- **Dress the room.** Give a setting (a farmhouse parlor, 1925; a London townhouse, 1890) and a character profile. Students build a period prop list in which pieces span several decades, and justify which are new, which are normal and which are inherited.
- **Business workshop.** With safe replicas (battery candles for a rush light, a spill, a snuffbox, a quill and sealing wax, a candlestick phone), actors and props students develop period business together, then discuss what it reveals about character.
- **Check the reference.** Give students five entries from any props reference (James, a website or an AI answer) and have them verify each against two independent sources. Discuss how errors spread.
- **Consultation plan.** For a play involving a religious ritual from a tradition not covered in class, students identify whom to consult, what to ask, and what not to use as a prop.
