# 9. Staffing, Time and Scheduling

> **Sources:** M ch.2 (pp. 19–20), ch.4 (pp. 40–45), ch.7 (pp. 104–107); H ch.2 (p. 20), ch.19 (pp. 364–366). Sections marked **[General knowledge]** aren't from the guide's sources.
> **[Check current]** Labour law, union rules on hours and overtime, and minimum crew requirements vary by place and contract.

Money is only one resource. This chapter covers the other two: **time** and **people**. Budgets and paperwork are in [ch. 8](08-budgets-and-paperwork.md).

## Time

> "Performance dates never change as a result of props being incomplete." (M 106)

### Labor vs. turnaround (H 364–365)
- **Labor time**: the hours someone actually works on the prop.
- **Turnaround time**: the calendar time until the prop is ready. This adds shopping, shipping, setup, **drying and curing**, cleanup and delivery, and depends on how many hours a day anyone can work on it. An eight-hour job at two hours a day takes four days.

### Estimating better
- **Keep a work journal** of how long tasks take. Hart discovered that putting casters on furniture (16 holes, 16 bolts) took 30–60 minutes he had never budgeted (H 364–365).
- **Break big tasks into small ones you can estimate.** You may not know how long a dead goose takes to build, but you know how long it takes to glue on twelve feather pads (H 365).
- Add a **time contingency** for the forgotten hammer or the dried-out can of paint (H 365).
- Estimate at **your** speed, not a faster colleague's (H 366).

### Plan backward from deadlines (H 365)
Work back from the hard dates:
- Order by Friday or lose the weekend.
- The last coat needs three hours to dry before curtain.
- The designer must deliver final drawings by a certain date if the prop is to be ready for first tech.

Then tell people those dates.

### Time-saving habits (M 106)
- Batch your shopping and plan routes.
- Gather all materials and tools before starting a build.
- Don't procrastinate.

### Feasibility check (M 104–107)
Do you have enough **money**, **time**, and **people with the right skills**? If not, raise the flag now (see [ch. 10](10-working-with-other-departments.md#raise-your-red-flag-early-m-107108-127-137)).

## Expertise (H 366)

Build in materials you know, especially on a tight schedule. A strong carpenter should lean on wood. Hart suggests that an unfamiliar material like fiberglass doesn't belong on a short timeline, and that a hard-coated foam may be the better choice. Experiment when the schedule allows, with a backup plan ready (H 20).

## Running a shop crew (H 365)

- Give each artisan a mix of large and small tasks so no one sits idle while paint dries.
- Post a **master to-do list** of big and small projects. Artisans **write their names** on the tasks they take, so two people don't start the same job.
- Keep quick filler tasks ("shorten the wand one inch") for the fifteen minutes before lunch.

## Building the props calendar [General knowledge]

Put every hard date on one calendar as soon as you're hired (M 40):
1. **Fixed dates from the production calendar**: design meetings, first rehearsal, designer run, load-in, first tech, first dress, previews, opening, strike.
2. **Your deadlines**, working backward from those (H 365): when rehearsal props must be in the room, when the prop request deadline falls ([ch. 12](12-rehearsals-tech-and-performance.md#the-prop-request-deadline-m-127128)), when builds must start so paint and finishes can cure, and when rentals and orders must be placed.
3. **Lead times**: special orders, shipping, rentals that need booking, and anything needing outside vendors.
4. **Other people's dates**: the scene shop's build schedule, the paint shop's time on stage, electrics' focus, and the costume shop's fittings when costume props are shared.

A common rule of thumb is to aim for **every prop finished, or represented by a usable stand-in, by first tech**, and to keep previews for notes, not first builds.

## Estimating labour

Hart's method (above) is the core: keep a work journal, break big tasks into small ones, estimate at your own speed and add a contingency (H 364–366). **[General knowledge]** A simple way to turn a list into a staffing plan:
1. Estimate **labour hours** for every build, repair and alteration.
2. Add hours for **shopping, pickups and returns** (often a full day a week in busy periods), **rehearsal notes** and **load-in and strike**.
3. Divide by the **working hours available** before first tech for each person.
4. If the total exceeds what your team can do, you have three options: cut or simplify props, find more people or money (overhire), or move deadlines. Raise it early ([ch. 10](10-working-with-other-departments.md#raise-your-red-flag-early-m-107108-127-137)).

## Staffing the department

- **Who you might have** (M 19; H 6–7): assistant prop master, prop artisans (carpenters, craftspeople, soft-goods artisans), shopper/buyers, and run crew. See [ch. 2](../1-intro/02-the-prop-master.md#where-props-sits-in-the-organization).
- **Managing people** (M 19–20): train continuously; delegate with complete information up front; trust eager people even before they're expert; praise publicly and critique privately; pass on compliments.
- **Give people the right work** (H 365–366): mix big and small tasks, use a posted master to-do list, and match tasks to skills, especially on a short schedule.

**[General knowledge]**
- **Overhire** (extra paid help for a busy period) is usually cheaper than missing a deadline. Know your institution's process and rates for adding staff before you need them.
- **Students and volunteers** need training time and supervision. Budget that time as real labour. Give them complete, achievable tasks so they finish things and learn.
- **Specialists** (an upholsterer, a welder, a scenic artist, an armorer) can be booked for a day rather than learning a skill under deadline. This is Hart's expertise point in practice (H 366).
- **Run crew.** Props running crew (often under stage management) preset, track, reset and maintain props during performances. Write clear preset sheets and run sheets so they can work without you ([Appendix B](../../appendices/B-paperwork-templates.md); [ch. 12](12-rehearsals-tech-and-performance.md)).

## Hours, breaks and burnout

- Theatre is never a 40-hour week, and tech is long. Compare your list against your time **early** and tell your supervisor if they don't fit (M 41–45).
- Sleep, eat and know your limits (M 41–45).
- **[General knowledge]** Tired people cause accidents in a shop. Schedule power-tool work for when people are fresh, never let anyone use machines alone late at night, and follow any union or institutional rules on maximum hours and turnaround between calls.

## For the classroom

- **Backward schedule.** Give students a production calendar and a 25-item prop list with build estimates. They produce a props calendar working backward from first tech, with order and booking dates.
- **Can we do it?** Students total labour hours for a list, divide by the team's available hours, and present a plan to cut, add people or move deadlines.
- **Delegation brief.** Each student writes the instructions for one build as if handing it to an artisan they won't see for three days, then swaps with a partner to find what's missing.
- **Time journal.** Students log actual hours on shop tasks for a month and compare with their estimates (H 364–365).
